Centre for the Governance of AI

Researches how governments and AI developers can manage advanced AI risks, and runs fellowships and visitor programmes that train people for AI governance work.

Works on

  • Founding new organisations on unowned problems Building the field
    Fund people to start the organisations the field lacks.
  • Forecasting Building the field
    Predict capability timelines as an input to prioritisation.
  • Think tank for AI labs Building the field
    On-demand independent expertise for lab safety and governance teams.
  • Research training and upskilling programmes Building the field
    Run courses, bootcamps and mentored fellowships that train newcomers for AI safety research, engineering and policy work.
  • Dangerous-capability evaluations Testing before release
    Test models for bio, cyber, persuasion, autonomy and scheming capabilities before release.
  • Safety cases Testing before release
    A structured, auditable argument that a system is acceptably safe, borrowed from nuclear and aviation.
  • Agent identity and accountability (IDs for AI systems) Releasing and running
    Give AI systems unique IDs so their actions can be traced to the system and the party responsible.
  • Limiting proliferation of high-risk models Releasing and running
    Restrict distribution, for example through monitored application interfaces.
  • Independent third-party evaluation and audit AI companies
    Outside parties test and verify a lab's safety and capability claims.
  • Regulation, standards and licensing AI companies
    Government or standards-body rules that constrain development or deployment.
  • Compute governance AI companies
    Govern hardware and compute inputs as a lever on who can train frontier systems.
  • Frontier-lab internal policy and safety culture AI companies
    Shape the labs' own risk management, red lines and incident reporting.
  • Duties for deployers (companies that put models into use) AI companies
    Put legal duties on the companies that use AI systems, such as human oversight, monitoring, keeping logs and telling affected people.
  • Know-your-customer checks by compute and cloud providers AI companies
    Require cloud and compute providers to verify their customers and report large training runs, making providers checkpoints on frontier development.
  • AI incident reporting systems and databases AI companies
    Collect reports of AI harms and near-harms in shared databases or required filings, so others learn and regulators can act.
  • Model cards, system cards and transparency reporting AI companies
    Publish a standard document with each model on its data, evaluations, limits and risks, so outsiders can check the developer's claims.
  • Jurisdictional certification Global coordination
    An international body certifies whole national AI regulatory regimes. States then block imports of AI-built goods from countries that are not certified.
  • Benefit-sharing for joining governance (Atoms for Peace model) Global coordination
    Offers states access to AI benefits in return for joining safety governance, as Atoms for Peace did for nuclear technology.
  • Offence–defence balance research Society and power
    Model how AI shifts the relative cost of attack versus defence in a domain.
  • Market-driven resilience mechanisms Society and power
    Use market incentives to build society's capacity to absorb anticipated AI threats.
  • Predistribution and windfall mechanisms Society and power
    Decide who holds equity, access and governance rights before the gains arrive, not after.
