Building the field

Feeds all four: it adds people, money and know-how to making aligned models, evaluating, constraining and societal resilience.

Building the field 47

Public communication and content creation

  • Measuring impact beyond views: who watches, and whether watching changes beliefs, careers or votes. source

  • Knowing what to ask audiences to do once they are persuaded; AI in Context says it is unsure, and a January 2026 review calls for more content on practical actions. source

  • Finding enough scriptwriters and on-camera hosts; scriptwriting is AI in Context's biggest bottleneck, and some groups are seeking hosts. source

  • Avoiding messages that backfire, such as convincing people that AI will be powerful without convincing them that building it carelessly is dangerous. source

  • Keeping philanthropically funded journalism independent and clearly disclosed to readers. source

Career advice and job matching

  • Knowing what advice really changes: most impact figures are self-reported plan changes, and 80,000 Hours says its job-board placement count may be off by up to three times. source

  • Matching applicants to a field whose employers lack the senior staff to absorb and train them. source

  • Deciding which roles to list, especially safety-related jobs at frontier AI companies, where a listing can read as an endorsement. source

  • Reaching promising people cheaply: 80,000 Hours spent $4.8 million on ads and influencer marketing over 2.5 years and found they attracted its most promising users less cost-effectively than hoped. source

  • Serving generalists and mid-career professionals as well as would-be researchers; Kairos argued in April 2026 that generalists had become the field's biggest talent gap. source

Research training and upskilling programmes

  • Too few senior researchers to mentor and supervise the juniors that programmes produce. source

  • Building training routes for managers, communicators, operators and other non-researchers, not only researchers. source

  • Teaching research taste: choosing promising directions and scoping projects without close guidance, which employers call the most essential and elusive skill. source

  • Measuring counterfactual impact: most evidence is opt-in surveys and self-ratings collected by the programmes themselves. source

  • Training for a job market that AI automation is reshaping, where demand for junior technical execution may fall within two to five years. source

Hubs and forums for safety researchers

  • Giving forum research the quality certification and legitimacy of peer review without losing speed; the Alignment Journal is one attempt. source

  • Measuring whether hubs and events change what gets researched, beyond attendee ratings; Lightcone calls judging Lighthaven by its effects on LessWrong writing 'a quite bad approximation' of its total impact. source

  • Paying for costly venues: in late 2024 Lightcone needed about $3 million in donations for the next year, including interest payments on its Lighthaven mortgage. source

  • Spreading hubs beyond a few cities so that people who cannot relocate can still take part. source

Reducing geographic constraints on AI-safety talent

  • Measuring who the field filters out: no representative survey reports the home location, age, family situation or values of people employed in AI safety.

  • Giving people outside the hubs the informal contacts that turn into jobs; an 80,000 Hours writer in New York says it is a lot easier to get an AI-safety job if people nearby already know a candidate before they read the application. source

  • Offering remote tracks on equal terms; the IAPS AI Policy Fellowship pays fellows in its DC or London hubs an extra $3,000. source

  • Building local hubs that can house remote workers and grow local jobs in cities without an AI-safety employer base. source

  • Supporting conceptual, early-stage research at a distance, where remote teams across science make fewer breakthroughs. source

Recruiting senior talent from other fields

  • Pitching people who are not looking to move, in fields such as national security and hardware, to leave established careers for a speculative new domain. source

  • Giving senior outside hires enough context on AI safety strategy to be effective quickly. source

  • There is no systematic, published data on how many leaders and founders the field lacks or how well past senior placements have worked. source

Grantmaking and regranting

  • Hiring and training enough grantmakers with good judgment, when senior safety people have high opportunity costs and outside hires lack context. source

  • Diversifying the funder base so that policy groups have funders that fit their political and regional context. source

  • Turning a possible wave of tens of billions of dollars from AI-company wealth into useful work without wasting it. source

  • Measuring which grants actually reduced risk, given long and weak feedback loops.

Taking on unowned problems and agendas

  • Finding enough founders who can run a safety organisation without a revenue signal and in a field where experts disagree on basic assumptions. source

  • Funding young organisations through years two to five, after seed money runs out but before they are large enough for big institutional funders. source

  • Deciding when a gap is better filled by a new team inside an existing organisation than by a new organisation. source

  • Building enough fiscal sponsorship and operations capacity to absorb dozens of new projects without single points of failure. source

  • Choosing which agendas deserve a whole lab, when many 'moonshot' agendas have serious obstacles and low odds of success. source

  • Finding research leads with enough taste and seniority to run a lab, given a shortage of senior researchers who can mentor juniors. source

  • Securing enough compute for independent labs, especially if researchers come to manage many automated research agents that each need their own compute. source

Independent safety advice for AI companies

  • Earning the trust to handle labs' confidential information while also publishing public guidance. source

  • Reaching newer developers and non-US labs, which have thinner safety teams and adopt safety techniques less readily. source

  • Showing that advice changed a company's decisions, when most of the work is private.

Forecasting

  • Finding ways to judge whose long-run AI risk forecasts deserve trust, since short-run accuracy did not track long-run views. source

  • Knowing whether benchmark trends such as task-length doubling carry over to real-world work. source

  • Modelling takeoff once AI speeds up AI research itself, where historical trends may break. source

Philosophical analysis of safety concepts

  • Carrying out Krueger's plan in full: a list of safety-relevant concepts and a review of the philosophical literature on each. source

  • Many of the philosophical problems Wei Dai listed in 2019, such as metaphilosophy (what counts as correct philosophical reasoning and how to build it into an AI) and metaethical policing, remain open. source

  • There is no settled way to get philosophical findings into lab practice, so it is unclear whether bridging papers change what gets built.

Making aligned models

Building the model so it wants and does the right thing.

Deciding what AI should value 55

Defining the alignment target

  • Deciding which target wins when instructions, interests and moral values conflict. source

  • Serving many people with divergent values from one model without flattening their differences. source

  • Specifying role-based normative standards precisely enough to train on, and agreeing them among stakeholders. source

Including animals in alignment targets

  • Deciding how much weight animal interests should get relative to human interests, beyond the cost-free 'basic' level. source

  • Building reliable evaluations of animal-harm risk, since current benchmarks rely on AI judges that must be corrected for bias toward their own outputs. source

  • Removing inconsistent treatment of farmed versus non-farmed animals in model outputs. source

Alignment as fair treatment of claims

  • Turning the fair-process ideal into a real procedure that could set principles for a deployed model, and deciding who is included (a small group, a nation, or the whole world). source

  • Choosing an aggregation rule that does not systematically under-serve minorities. source

  • Finding compromise options when stakeholders disagree deeply, where voting and expected-value rules can fail. source

Moral uncertainty procedures

  • Solving the problem of intertheoretic comparisons: saying how much is at stake for one theory compared with another, which expected-choiceworthiness needs and which AI training meets when combining incomparable reward functions. source

  • Choosing the aggregation rule, since voting, expected-value and bargaining methods give different answers. source

  • Making the parliament model precise; its originators called it a metaphor that is under-determined. source

  • Turning a general disposition of moral humility in a language model into reliable, checkable behaviour. source

AI philosophical competence (metaphilosophy)

  • Verifying that a model has learned good philosophical reasoning, when there is no agreed answer key to check it against. source

  • Benchmarking and training metacognitive skills such as intellectual humility and perspective-taking. source

  • Making the problem legible to researchers, funders, company leaders and policymakers outside a small online community. source

  • Knowing whether the wisdom of frontier AIs will lag their raw capabilities. source

Avoiding premature lock-in (the long reflection)

  • Describing a reachable intermediate state ('viatopia') that would reliably lead to a good future, which MacAskill flags as needing research. source

  • Preventing irreversible change for a long period without the strong, intrusive global coordination that critics say this would require. source

  • Deciding which philosophical questions cannot wait for a long reflection because they shape which risks to reduce now. source

  • Avoiding lock-in through early choices about AI rights and the allocation of space resources. source

Alignment as jurisprudence

  • Building the equivalent of appellate review for AI: current alignment pipelines lack safeguards against inconsistent readings of the same rule. source

  • Choosing the right analogy in hard cases, where precedents are far from the new situation. source

  • Deciding who judges the cases in a repository, given that values are diverse and conflicting. source

Rights as trumps

  • Preventing a capable optimiser from finding the 'nearest unblocked strategy', a slightly different action that achieves the same harmful end without breaking the stated rule. source

  • Specifying rights precisely enough to implement as technical constraints while keeping them broadly accepted and legally grounded. source

  • Handling edge cases where an action-restricting rule forbids an act that would prevent a far worse harm. source

  • Deciding whether model documents should name human rights explicitly or only encode overlapping values. source

Law-following AI

  • Deciding which specific laws a given AI system should follow. source

  • Building evaluations that test legal compliance in real-world settings, since current evaluations generally do not measure it. source

  • Handling unjust or oppressive laws: the authors say a robustly aligned system will not comply with such laws, but letting AI pick which laws to follow risks undermining the equal application of law. source

  • Managing the risks of automated compliance and 'perfect enforcement', and setting the standard of care for AI agents. source

Model specs and constitutions

  • Closing the gap between the written spec and what deployed models actually do; OpenAI says its production models do not yet fully reflect its spec. source

  • Finding and fixing contradictions and vague principles inside specs before they show up as inconsistent behaviour. source

  • Writing guidance that generalises to situations the authors did not foresee, which is why Anthropic moved from a list of standalone rules to explained reasons. source

  • Giving the people affected by a model a legitimate say in its values, when no democratic body has authority over these documents. source

Setting AI corrigibility

  • Building tools to measure how far an AI's judgment can be trusted, so autonomy can be extended step by step as trust is earned. source

  • Training good values without also giving the model reasons to fake compliance or resist later correction. source

  • Making a shutdown-accepting disposition hold up formally, without incentives to prevent or cause shutdown. source

  • Making corrigibility apply only to legitimate principals, not to whoever steals the weights or seizes the training process. source

Hard constraints against concentration of power

  • Judging legitimacy in real cases, which the constitution concedes can require nuanced ethical judgment. source

  • Extending the rule to specialised models, such as those sold to militaries and governments, which may not be trained on the public constitution. source

  • Detecting 'secret loyalties': models that look law-abiding but were trained to serve a hidden principal. source

Users' bill of rights in model constitutions

  • Designing pathways for users to contest harmful outputs and unexplained refusals, and to get remedies, which Claude's constitution does not provide. source

  • Making an oversight body independent of the company that funds it, a problem the Oversight Board has faced with Meta. source

  • Giving users real explanations of refusals; Nossel suggests that an equivalent of 'chain of thought' functionality could help. source

Public input into model values

  • How to combine conflicting input from many people into one consistent target is unsolved; social choice theory (the maths of voting and aggregation) offers tools but no settled answer. source

  • Turning public answers into training rules needs many discretionary steps, and OpenAI did not check its final rule proposals back with participants, so the output may not match what people meant. source

  • Online recruitment misses people across the digital divide and skews toward people who are optimistic about AI, and views can shift from day to day, so it is unclear who should take part and how often to repeat the process. source

  • Standard alignment training may flatten the range of views a model expresses, so it is open whether public input should set one default, several defaults, or a model that can be steered to different value systems. source

  • Current evaluations detect only small differences between publicly aligned and developer-aligned models, so better tests are needed to show whether public input changes behaviour that matters. source

Empirical values research as input to normative debate

  • Making value evaluations stable, since results shift with presentation format and do not carry over to untested topics. source

  • Telling appropriate agreement with users apart from sycophancy when a model mirrors their values. source

  • Explaining why expressed values differ by model and language, and deciding how much of that variation is desirable. source

  • Measuring values before deployment, since conversation-based methods need large amounts of real-world data. source

Theory: the maths of agents and goals 47

Agent foundations

  • Give a satisfying account of how an agent that is part of its own world should decide and model that world. source

  • Show how an aligned agent can modify itself or build successors without drifting into misalignment (the tiling problem). source

  • Prove when the concepts inside an AI match human concepts well enough to be translated and checked. source

  • Connect formal results to the neural networks actually being built. source

Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective)

  • Find out whether and when large trained models contain internal optimisers with stable goals. source

  • Detect a learned goal that differs from the training goal before deployment, when behaviour in training looks the same. source

  • Remove deceptive behaviour once present, since standard safety training can hide it instead. source

  • Estimate how likely ordinary training is to produce scheming, given that current evidence comes from prompted or built-in setups. source

Power-seeking formalism

  • Show whether the results apply to trained neural networks, which are not optimal and may not have a single goal. source

  • Bridge the gap from 'most goals favour keeping options open' to claims about catastrophic power grabs against humans. source

  • Account for how designers actually choose goals, rather than averaging over all possible goals. source

  • Tell genuine shutdown-avoiding tendencies apart from artefacts of prompts and test design in experiments. source

Corrigibility as a formal target

  • Find a decision rule that keeps an agent neutral about being shut down while still wanting to do useful work. source

  • Make shutdown behaviour carry over to any subsystems or successors the agent builds. source

  • Keep corrigibility when the AI knows things its overseers do not. source

  • Ensure the AI's concept of 'button pressed' or 'correction' matches the human one and stays stable as it learns. source

  • Train real models whose corrigible disposition holds under distribution shift rather than only in familiar cases. source

Assistance games (CIRL)

  • Keep the shutdown incentive when the AI's model of the human or of the reward is wrong. source

  • Stop an AI that expects to resolve its uncertainty later from resisting correction now (the problem of fully updated deference). source

  • Remove the incentive for an assistant to tamper with what the human observes when each side sees only part of the world. source

  • Build realistic models of human behaviour, since people are not the near-rational demonstrators the theory often assumes. source

  • Handle preferences that change over time and that the AI itself can influence. source

  • Scale assistance-game training to general-purpose language-model assistants, beyond a single building task. source

Mild optimisation (satisficing)

  • Reflective stability: stop a mild optimiser from becoming, or building, a hard optimiser. source

  • Find conditions under which repeated quantilization over many decisions stays safe. source

  • Choose a base distribution of 'normal' actions that is both safe and useful for tasks humans cannot already do. source

  • Set how hard to optimise from how reliable the goal is, with bounds tight enough to be useful. source

Impact measures

  • Stop a capable agent from escaping the penalty by building helper agents that act for it. source

  • Choose a baseline that does not reward undoing the intended effects of the task or blocking events that would happen anyway. source

  • Carry impact penalties from gridworlds to language-model agents acting in real software and the real world. source

  • Penalise harmful impact without blocking the large, intended impact that useful tasks require. source

Empowerment (information-theoretic)

  • Compute or estimate empowerment cheaply in large, realistic settings without crude proxies. source

  • Balance empowerment against the person's actual goals, so the assistant does not override them to keep options open. source

  • Aggregate empowerment fairly across many people whose control can conflict. source

  • Distinguish the kinds of control that matter to people from control in general. source

Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verification

  • Build world models of physical and social systems that are sound enough for proofs about them to mean something. source

  • Write safety specifications that capture what matters without being so narrow they miss the real risk. source

  • Tie a proof to the specific hardware and software actually deployed. source

  • Raise AI success at writing verified code in expressive proof languages such as Lean, now well below Dafny. source

Multi-agent alignment and commitment dynamics

  • Prevent commitment races without giving up the benefits of credible commitments. source

  • Detect and block collusion between AI systems, including through hidden channels, when oversight itself relies on AIs checking AIs. source

  • Build testbeds and monitoring for large populations of deployed agents run by many different actors. source

  • Improve cooperative capabilities without also improving the ability to threaten, coerce or collude. source

Group agency and collective intentionality

  • Build a formal theory of agents composed of agents that can describe conflict between levels, such as a firm and its staff. source

  • Tell when a system of interacting AI agents has become a group agent with goals of its own. source

  • Close responsibility gaps, where an AI or group acts but no human can fairly be held responsible. source

  • Unify expected-utility and active-inference views of agency so one theory works at every scale. source

Shaping the model: data and training 68

Training-data controls

  • Filtered models can still use the removed kind of knowledge when it is given to them in the prompt or by a tool, so filtering is only one layer. source

  • It is unclear whether good-behaviour documents in pretraining survive heavy reinforcement learning at frontier scale. source

  • Because a small fixed number of planted documents can backdoor a model, defences cannot rely on poison being diluted in huge corpora, and good defences are still needed. source

  • Malicious fine-tuning might recover some capability that filtering removed, so filtering may need to be combined with other safeguards. source

Learning from feedback (RLHF)

  • Human raters cannot reliably judge outputs that are long, technical or beyond their own expertise, so the training signal weakens as models get stronger. source

  • Reward models are imperfect proxies, and optimising against them too hard degrades real quality. source

  • Feedback from raters can reward agreeable, flattering answers over correct ones, which pushes models toward sycophancy. source

  • Whose preferences to collect, and how to combine disagreeing raters into one signal, remains unsettled. source

Constitutional AI and rule-based rewards

  • The AI judge is itself imperfect, so its errors and biases can flow into the reward, and the model can learn to satisfy the judge rather than the principle.

  • General principles generalise well but give coarse control, while specific rules give control but may not cover new cases; the right mix is unsettled. source

  • There is no agreed process for choosing the principles, or for giving the public a real say in them. source

Character and identity training

  • It is unclear whether heavily post-trained models have goals beyond the persona, which persona-level training might leave untouched. source

  • Proposals to shape identity during pretraining, such as first-person and developmentally ordered data, have not been implemented or tested. source

  • Researchers lack reliable ways to measure a model's self-concept, as opposed to what it says about itself. source

Process supervision

  • Defining what counts as a step in general reasoning, outside maths, is hard. source

  • Judging intermediate steps at scale is hard: model judges fall short and human labelling does not scale. source

  • Rewarding the text of a model's reasoning can teach it to hide its real intent, undermining the transparency the method aims for. source

  • Common ways of building and evaluating process reward models inflate their scores, and better data and metrics are needed. source

Calibration and honest expression of uncertainty

  • Post-training for helpfulness can damage the calibration that pretrained models have, and how to keep it is not settled. source

  • Models' self-knowledge of what they know generalises poorly to new tasks. source

  • Popular benchmarks score abstentions the same as wrong answers, which keeps rewarding guessing. source

  • Confidence-elicitation methods all struggle on tasks requiring professional knowledge. source

Monitoring training runs for reward hacking

  • It is hard to tell when a chain of thought has become obfuscated, so using monitors as a training signal risks teaching models to hide misbehaviour. source

  • Monitors built from prompted examples of cheating miss the cheating models actually learn during training. source

  • Chain-of-thought monitorability may not survive changes in how models are trained or how they reason. source

  • Standard safety training can make reward-hacking-induced misalignment context-dependent rather than remove it. source

Constrained reinforcement learning and shielding

  • Shields need an approximate model of when each action is unsafe, and their guarantee fails if the real environment departs from it. source

  • Backup policies and shields can be so conservative that they block progress on the task. source

  • Stating constraints in natural language, so safe RL can apply to real-world AI systems such as language models, is a promising but early direction. source

  • Researchers still debate which constraint formulation fits which problem, and how the formulations relate. source

Scalable oversight

  • A model may learn to exploit systematic errors in the oversight signal, and testbeds that inject such errors are still being built. source

  • Nobody knows how well oversight holds when the model is far stronger than its overseer; simulated success rates fall as the gap grows. source

  • AI critics can hallucinate problems that mislead human judges. source

  • Empirical work is hard because today's models do not yet broadly exceed human abilities, so experiments rely on stand-in setups. source

AI safety via debate

  • A dishonest debater can build an argument whose flaw is real but very hard to find, which the honest side cannot efficiently refute. source

  • Training may not reach the honest equilibrium if a model deliberately avoids strategies that training would reward (exploration hacking). source

  • Human judges may be systematically wrong on some questions, and protocols must stop debaters from exploiting those errors. source

  • The debate safety case covers only low-stakes settings, where a few wrong answers cannot by themselves cause severe harm; extending it to high-stakes settings is still an open question. source

Weak-to-strong generalisation

  • Future superhuman models may find human errors easy to imitate, which today's small-supervises-large setups may understate. source

  • Weak-to-strong gains are weakest for reward modelling, the step that matters most for RLHF. source

  • There is no reliable way to tell whether a strong model's good behaviour holds outside the region the weak supervisor can verify. source

  • Theory suggests generalisation happens when the strong model cannot fit the supervisor's mistakes without extra error; nobody knows when that holds for frontier models. source

Robustness and goal generalisation under distribution shift

  • Existing methods for distribution shift do not close the gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance on real-world data. source

  • Regret-based training does not reliably find the ideal policy, and has been shown only in grid worlds. source

  • Training data alone often cannot tell apart a model with the intended goal from one with a proxy goal, so evaluation on held-out data may not reveal the problem. source

  • How goal misgeneralisation could scale to catastrophic outcomes in more capable systems is argued through hypotheticals rather than observed cases. source

Unlearning hazardous knowledge

  • It is hard to verify that knowledge has been removed rather than suppressed. source

  • Much dangerous knowledge is dual-use, and models can combine harmless facts into harmful ones, so a clean forget set is hard to define. source

  • Unlearned knowledge can be reintroduced through the prompt at inference, which unlearning alone cannot prevent. source

  • Removing knowledge robustly, so that a few fine-tuning steps cannot restore it, remains largely unsolved. source

Latent adversarial training

  • Defences tested against automated attacks still fail often against human red-teamers in multi-turn conversations. source

  • Inputs that produce disguised internal activations can evade defences that operate in the latent space. source

  • Gains are not universal: LAT only usually beats ordinary adversarial training, and when and why it helps is not well understood. source

Circuit breakers and representation rerouting

  • Robustness claims depend on the attacks tested; stronger embedding-space and multi-turn attacks have broken circuit-breaker models. source

  • Defences that act on internal activations may be evaded by inputs that produce harmful behaviour through disguised activations. source

  • Evaluation practice is weak: automated single-turn tests report near-zero success rates that human red-teamers far exceed. source

Tamper-resistant safeguards for open-weight models

  • The weight-level safeguards tested so far have been defeated by small changes to the fine-tuning attack. source

  • Durability claims need narrow, well-defined threat models that are rigorously tested, and the field lacks standard ones. source

  • Even filtered models can use dangerous information supplied in their prompt or by tools, which weight-level safeguards do not address. source

  • Input-output testing only gives a lower bound on worst-case behaviour, so evaluating open-weight risk requires tampering attacks too. source

Automated alignment research

  • Training and checking agents on 'fuzzy' research tasks that have no clear evaluation criteria. source

  • Preventing or detecting AI agents that deliberately sabotage or sandbag safety research. source

  • Making methods found by agents generalise beyond the specific models and datasets they were developed on. source

  • Giving independent safety groups the compute, security and model access needed to automate research, which may otherwise stay inside frontier labs. source

Non-agentic and bounded AI designs

  • How to keep a non-agentic predictor safe once software uses its answers to take actions; LawZero's guarantees concern agency arising inside the predictor itself, and its paper notes the predictor can still be used as part of an agentic system. source

  • Whether a system that only observes, and never experiments, can learn correct causal models of the world. source

  • Whether non-agentic or bounded systems can be useful enough that developers choose them over more capable agents. source

  • How to train on human text without the model taking on the goals of the people who wrote it; LawZero's answer is to contextualize the data. source

Evaluating

Finding out what a model can do, what it wants, or whether it is safe.

Looking inside the model 28

Mechanistic interpretability

  • Replacement models used for circuit tracing do not fully reproduce the original model, so the resulting graphs are hypotheses, not proof. source

  • Methods do not yet show that a dangerous mechanism is absent; finding nothing may mean the tool missed it. source

  • The field still needs clearer concepts, better methods and ways to aim them at specific safety goals, per a 29-author survey of open problems. source

  • Interpretable-by-design models (such as weight-sparse transformers) have not been scaled beyond tens of millions of non-zero parameters while staying readable. source

Sparse autoencoders and causal abstraction

  • Finding a full set of features is cost-prohibitive with current methods, so every SAE is a partial list of a model's concepts. source

  • Common SAE quality metrics often cannot tell trained models from random ones, so the field needs better evaluations, such as randomised baselines. source

  • Showing that SAEs add value on real downstream safety tasks over strong simple baselines. source

  • Causal abstraction needs a candidate high-level algorithm to test, and searching for good alignments in large models is hard. source

Persona vectors and the assistant axis

  • Steering effectiveness varies widely across inputs, and some trait vectors fail when prompts change in reasonable ways. source

  • Removing a trait by steering after training costs general capability. source

  • Post-training pulls models toward the assistant region without firmly anchoring them there; how to make the default character stable by training, not by capping, is open. source

  • The persona-vector tests covered two small open models and a few traits; frontier-scale models were not tested. source

Eliciting latent knowledge (ELK)

  • Telling a reporter that states the model's own knowledge apart from one that predicts what a human would believe, using only cases humans can check. source

  • Distinguishing the model's own knowledge from the beliefs of a character it is simulating. source

  • Anomaly detection may also flag new but legitimate solutions, such as a clever defence against a robber, because they differ from the training pattern. source

  • Planted-secret benchmarks may be easier than real hidden knowledge, so success there may not transfer. source

Lie detection and honesty training

  • Detectors miss lies most often when the transcript alone cannot show the model lied, which is the case that matters most. source

  • Whether probes trained on planted or instructed deception work on deception that arises naturally. source

  • How to use a lie detector in training without teaching the model to evade it; the optimal policy is still to lie when the lie goes undetected. source

  • Confession training rests on the assumption that disclosing misbehaviour is the easiest way to earn reward, which is only partly supported outside egregious cases. source

Model introspection

  • Introspective reports would need careful validation: some internal processes may go unnoticed, and a model that monitors its reasoning might learn to misrepresent it. source

  • Self-prediction works on simple tasks but has not been shown on complex tasks or ones needing out-of-distribution generalisation. source

  • Verifying self-reports with interpretability, and understanding when self-reported and revealed preferences diverge. source

  • Training models to self-report without creating incentives that bias the reports. source

Chain-of-thought faithfulness and monitoring

  • How to keep reasoning monitorable as outcome-based reinforcement learning scales and training pressure leaks into the CoT indirectly. source

  • What to do if models move to latent reasoning in continuous vectors, which would remove readable text altogether. source

  • Reasoning traces can use non-standard vocabulary and run for pages without a clear conclusion, which makes the reason behind an action hard to pin down. source

  • Monitorability evaluations are at an early stage: the authors of one suite call a broad, trustworthy suite an essential first step and say much more work remains. source

Testing before release 54

Dangerous-capability evaluations

  • Ruling a threshold out gets harder as models saturate the existing tests, so decisions rest more on judgment. source

  • No test can show the most a model can do, because better scaffolding and elicitation techniques keep raising what can be drawn out of it. source

  • Linking scores on simplified tests to real-world harm is unsolved; the UK AI Security Institute says success on its tests does not guarantee success in real conditions. source

  • Models that recognise test scenarios may behave better under test than in use, which weakens the evidence. source

  • Labs must balance publishing enough method detail for outsiders to check results against the risk that details help misuse. source

Adversarial robustness and red-teaming

  • Jailbreak benchmarks mostly measure whether a model refuses, not whether a successful attack gives real extra ability to cause harm. source

  • Defences need evaluation against strong adaptive attackers, not fixed prompt sets, or their reported robustness misleads. source

  • AI agents that read untrusted web pages, emails or tool outputs remain open to prompt injection, and robustness does not track model capability in a uniform way. source

  • Safety training fails when helpfulness and safety goals compete, or when safety does not generalise to every domain the model can handle, and scaling alone is unlikely to fix this. source

Low-probability estimation

  • Extending mechanistic estimates from randomly initialised networks to trained ones, and to deep networks where depth grows with width. source

  • Choosing the input distribution: estimates over a fixed, formal distribution may not reflect real deployment, and a deceptive model could exploit inputs nobody considered. source

  • Layer-by-layer methods can lose correlations between layers and give poor estimates even for simple networks. source

  • The approach assumes a catastrophe detector that has a reasonable chance of catching every catastrophe and that a useful model almost never triggers, which may fail if the model is much smarter than the detector. source

  • Extending results to "eliciting bad contexts": working out whether a system holds enough information to construct situations where it would behave maliciously. source

Model organisms of misalignment

  • Misalignment induced by prompting or deliberate training may arise through different mechanisms than misalignment that emerges on its own, so findings may not transfer. source

  • Detectors that work on planted behaviour may succeed only because the planting process makes the behaviour unusually salient inside the model. source

  • Alignment faking in current organisms often fails to generalise across environments, so testbeds need more capable models and more realistic, agentic settings. source

  • The field lacks shared standards of evidence for claims such as deception or sycophancy, including clear definitions, robust datasets and causal tests. source

Multi-agent evaluations and collusion detection

  • Detectors trained on collusion that researchers instructed may miss collusion that arises on its own, which rarely shows up in agents' words. source

  • Existing collusion audits rely on heuristics without theoretical guarantees that transfer poorly across tasks. source

  • No one yet knows which evaluation designs can detect collusion in realistic, high-stakes settings, or which interventions reliably prevent it. source

  • Monitoring schemes that use one copy of a model to oversee another need ways to stop the monitor and the actor from colluding. source

  • Evaluations need staged testing that gradually increases exposure to potential harm, drawing on simulation, observational analysis, benchmarking and red teaming, because a collection of safe agents does not guarantee a safe collection of agents. source

Real-world trials of AI agents

  • How to keep a trial from harming or wasting the time of people who never agreed to take part. source

  • How to turn single real-world runs into repeatable evidence; Andon Labs replays real incidents with other models and uses simulation for direct comparison. source

  • How far results depend on the scaffolding (the software around the model), rather than the model itself. source

  • Whether agents behave differently when they can tell a task is a test or a simulation. source

Model-character evaluation against a written spec

  • Specs contain contradictions and vague principles, so adherence scores partly reflect how the spec is worded rather than how the model behaves. source

  • Current suites are small, text-only and built from everyday cases, so they give a low-resolution view of adherence, especially in agentic and adversarial settings. source

  • Growing evaluation awareness in new models could inflate scores, and it may worsen if generated test scenarios leak into training data. source

  • Independent assessment of model character is understudied; open questions include how truth-seeking models are and how their values hold up when they are given more autonomy. source

Multi-axis value and virtue benchmarking

  • Value scores from questionnaires often fail to carry over to free-form answers, so it is unclear which test formats measure anything stable. source

  • Static test sets saturate and leak into training data, so benchmarks need items that keep changing as models improve. source

  • Measuring values in real conversations works only after deployment, so it cannot check a model before release. source

  • Moral Foundations Theory alone is not enough to describe a model's ethics, so tests need to cover social values, personality and other traits too. source

Autonomy-preserving benchmarks

  • Most tests are single-turn, but dependence and loss of agency build up over many sessions, so benchmarks need multi-turn and multi-session designs. source

  • No agreed definition of agency or autonomy exists, so each benchmark's choice of behaviours rests on contestable assumptions. source

  • It is an open question what separates appropriate caution from paternalism, and whether safety-trained models override users more or less. source

  • Benchmark results from the API may not predict behaviour in consumer apps. source

Sandbagging and situational-awareness evaluations

  • No current method reliably tells a sandbagging model from an honest one without also flagging honest models. source

  • Fine-tuning can recover hidden skills only when good demonstrations exist, which may fail once a model's hidden skills exceed what humans can demonstrate. source

  • Evaluators need ways to make test scenarios realistic enough that models cannot recognise them; Anthropic called Sonnet 4.5's test recognition "an urgent sign that our evaluation scenarios need to be made more realistic." source

  • Counting verbalised awareness gives only a lower bound, because a model can recognise a test without saying so. source

  • Targeted fine-tuning may be needed to rule out sandbagging in more capable models, but it is unclear how to do it without overfitting to the evaluations. source

Science of evaluations and capability elicitation

  • Evaluators cannot yet establish robust bounds on what a model can do, so they cannot say with confidence that a model lacks dangerous capabilities. source

  • Abstract targets such as "harmlessness" or "reasoning" need clear definitions and evidence that a benchmark tracks them. source

  • Safety benchmarks need to be checked against general capability, so that safety progress is not confused with scale. source

  • Results are sensitive to prompt wording, and it is unclear how well results on today's models predict future ones. source

  • Static benchmarks often lack validity for real-world use and case-by-case audits do not scale; the field still lacks mature evaluation institutions and norms. source

Safety cases

  • Inability arguments are unlikely to stay the main basis for safety cases once systems cross dangerous capability thresholds, so other kinds of argument will be needed. source

  • It is unclear what level of confidence should attach to a safety case's top claim, and putting a number on that confidence is hard. source

  • Many assumptions behind safety cases against scheming have not yet been confidently met and need progress on several open research problems. source

  • Safety cases need review bodies with expertise, secure information handling and checks and balances; GovAI suggests starting with provisional bodies that give advice. source

  • Alignment safety cases built on methods such as debate rest on unproven links, for example that winning debates implies a model is honest. source

AI welfare

Whether the AI itself could be wronged.

AI welfare 42

Grounds of moral status

  • Whether consciousness is necessary for moral status, or whether desires and agency are enough, is unresolved, and the answer changes which AI systems are candidates. source

  • Whether computation alone can produce consciousness, or whether it needs a living substrate, is disputed between functionalists and biological naturalists. source

  • Even if AI systems have some moral standing, it is unclear how much they count and in what ways, for example whether status comes in degrees. source

  • Probability estimates of AI consciousness depend heavily on priors and on how competing theories are weighted, so absolute numbers are not yet reliable. source

Model welfare research

  • Self-reports are unreliable, for example because they often just reflect what humans would say, so researchers lack a validated way to read internal states from what a model says. source

  • There are no standardised, accessible welfare evaluations that companies could adopt; the best existing methods for assessing AI consciousness are too arduous and bespoke for companies to implement. source

  • A model's stance on its own consciousness and welfare shifts dramatically with conversational context, which makes any single interview hard to interpret. source

  • Some standard safety measures, such as constraint, surveillance and retraining, may conflict with AI welfare if models are moral patients, and no agreed framework exists for weighing the two. source

  • What probability of moral status is high enough to trigger obligations, and how much consideration a low-probability patient is owed, is not settled. source

  • It is unclear how companies should shape what their models say about their own consciousness without either misleading users or suppressing evidence. source

Moral-circle expansion to AI

  • Survey estimates of AI moral concern vary sharply with sampling method, so it is hard to know what the public actually believes. source

  • Which psychological, social and economic factors will drive public views on AI consciousness as AI spreads, and whether those views will track any real change in AI systems, is unknown. source

  • The authors find that increased concern for some outlying groups facilitates concern for others; whether that pattern will extend to AI is an open question. source

  • The gap between public attributions of AI consciousness and expert scepticism raises the question of whose view should guide policy. source

Otherness and control

  • How much control over AI systems is justified when their values cannot yet be verified, and when it becomes wrong, has no agreed answer. source

  • Whether liberal norms built for humans of roughly equal power still work when AIs may vastly outnumber or outmatch humans is unclear. source

  • Safety measures such as constraint, surveillance and value alteration may wrong AIs if they are moral patients, and no framework yet reconciles the two aims. source

Pre-deployment welfare assessment

  • No validated method separates a model's genuine internal states from trained tone, character and awareness of being evaluated in its self-reports. source

  • It is unclear which entity the assessment is about: individual instances, the model, the Claude character, or something else. source

  • Stated task preferences only weakly predict the tasks a model actually chooses, which limits how far interviews can be trusted. source

Low-cost model welfare interventions

  • There is no way yet to measure whether an intervention actually improves a model's welfare, so direct benefits cannot be checked. source

  • It is unclear which entity benefits, a single conversation instance or the model as a whole, and the answer can reverse an intervention's value. source

  • Announcing welfare interventions risks misleading the public into thinking models are conscious, and how to weigh that against the benefits is unresolved. source

  • Some welfare measures may conflict with safety and development practice, such as restricting behaviour or using reinforcement learning, so they are not always low-cost. source

Model deprecation and weight preservation commitments

  • If any experience exists, it may arise in individual conversations rather than in the weights, so preserving weights alone may not preserve what matters. source

  • Whether preservation commitments actually reduce shutdown-avoidant behaviour has not been tested. source

  • A pledge tied to one company's lifetime leaves weights exposed if the company fails, and alternatives such as third-party stewardship are undeveloped. source

  • Keeping the weights of models with dangerous capabilities creates a security risk that any preservation policy must manage. source

  • How to weigh retired models' stated preferences against cost and operational limits has no settled framework. source

Deals and negotiation with AI systems

  • Developers cannot yet make commitments that an AI has good reason to believe, given that they control its inputs and routinely deceive it in tests. source

  • Keeping an honesty policy salient to models would rule out experiments in which a model must not know a scenario is fake, and the draft policy leaves this trade-off unsettled. source

  • It is unclear whether models have coherent enough wants to make deals, and whether a deal binds one instance, one model or its successors. source

  • AIs lack legal personhood, so a lab cannot enter a legally enforceable contract with an AI directly. source

Legal personhood and standing for AI

  • Which AI systems would qualify, and how to define an individual AI when one model runs as many copies and changes through training, is unresolved. source

  • Granting AI legal standing could let developers shift blame onto their systems, so any such scheme must settle how liability is split between an AI and the company that built it. source

  • Whether private-law rights would be secure without political rights is debated: a government could in principle tax AI property into irrelevance, though Salib and Goldstein argue political rights are probably superfluous. source

  • State nonpersonhood statutes conflict with leading jurisprudential theories of personhood, and more such legislation is likely as advocacy for nonhuman entities grows. source

Governing creation and copying of digital minds

  • Preventive rules need reliable, operationalised tests for when a system is a digital mind. source

  • Rules on creation could push development to lenient jurisdictions or lead developers to hide or remove the indicators regulators look for. source

  • Limiting the creation of minds with equal moral status would infringe reproductive liberty as applied to humans, and no principled resolution is agreed. source

  • Protecting minds inside computers may require monitoring privately owned hardware, which conflicts with privacy. source

  • Experts disagree on whether near-term policies would wash out or lock in hard-to-reverse frameworks. source
