Holding AI companies to account

Constraining

Frontier-lab internal policy and safety culture

Shape the labs' own risk management, red lines and incident reporting.

Open in the compendium

This approach works on the AI companies' own rules and habits: the written policies that say when a model is too dangerous to train or release, who inside the company decides, and how problems get reported. Because frontier models are built inside a few companies, their internal risk management is today the main thing standing between a dangerous capability and its release. Outsiders try to make those policies stronger, clearer and actually followed.

How it works

The central tool is a 'frontier safety framework'. It names dangerous capabilities (for example, meaningful help with weapons or the ability to automate AI research), sets thresholds, and commits the company to stronger safeguards, or to pausing, when a model crosses one. At the AI Seoul Summit in 2024, 16 companies (later 20) committed to publish such frameworks and, in the extreme case, 'not to develop or deploy a model or system at all' if risks could not be kept below their thresholds.

Each company's version differs. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, at version 3.4 from July 2026, requires published Risk Reports and lets its Long-Term Benefit Trust request external review of them and approve the choice of external reviewers. Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework, in its third version from September 2025, added a capability level for harmful manipulation and extended safety-case reviews to large internal deployments of models that can speed up AI research.

Culture sits behind the documents. Risk-management ideas from other industries, such as the 'three lines of defence' model, assign and coordinate risk-management roles across separate teams and help the board oversee management, so that someone with authority is responsible for each risk.

Where research stands

This is a crowded, active area. Most leading Western companies and some others publish frameworks, and laws now require them: California's SB 53 since January 2026, New York's RAISE Act from January 2027, and the EU's Code of Practice for general-purpose AI. Outside groups grade the results. A December 2025 study scoring 12 frameworks against 65 criteria from aviation and nuclear risk management found scores from 34% (Anthropic) to 8% (Cohere), with a median of 18%, and called many commitments 'vague'. The Future of Life Institute's Summer 2026 index gave no company better than C+ overall and found existential safety the weakest area industry-wide.

The weak points are well known. Frameworks are written, interpreted and changed by the companies themselves; independent oversight of safety decisions is rare; and some commitments have been weakened over time. Watchdogs such as The Midas Project track changes to company policies. Former insiders have also described safety losing out to product pressure, which is why work on whistleblowing, internal reporting and outside verification often goes together with this approach.

Key results

  • In a survey of 51 experts, 98% somewhat or strongly agreed that AGI labs should run pre-deployment risk assessments, dangerous-capability evaluations, third-party audits, usage restrictions and red teaming, but best practices had not yet emerged. Jonas Schuett, Noemi Dreksler, Markus Anderljung, David McCaffary, Lennart Heim, Emma Bluemke, Ben Garfinkel, 2023
  • Scoring 12 published frontier safety frameworks against 65 risk-management criteria gave a median of 18%; adopting every leading practice already used by a peer would lift a company to about 54%. Lily Stelling, Malcolm Murray, Bruno Galizzi, Max Schaffelder, Siméon Campos, Henry Papadatos, 2025
  • At the AI Seoul Summit, companies committed to publish safety frameworks with thresholds for intolerable risk and not to develop or deploy a model if mitigations could not keep risk below them. UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, 2024
  • An independent panel graded nine companies; the best overall grade was C+ (Anthropic), and no company scored above C- on existential safety. Future of Life Institute, 2026
  • US agencies issued voluntary guidance with ten practices for preparing for and responding to incidents in which AI agents take uninstructed actions that undermine oversight during training and evaluation. NIST CAISSI, NSA Super Intelligence Security Center, CISA, 2026

Open problems

  • Most providers lack independent oversight of their own safety decisions. source
  • Commitments are often too vague to predict decisions or check compliance afterwards. source
  • How to cover risks from models used only inside the company, which never face public release. source
  • How to detect and deter quiet weakening of published commitments. source

Criticisms

  • Inside at least one leading lab, 'safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products', showing how commercial pressure can override internal policy. Jan Leike, former co-lead of OpenAI's Superalignment team, quoted in The Register
  • Most providers lack mechanisms for independent oversight of their safety decisions, and some commitments have weakened over time without clear justification. Lily Stelling, Siméon Campos, Henry Papadatos et al.
  • Even the best-scoring companies are far from adequate; 'Existential Safety is the weakest domain industry-wide.'. Future of Life Institute AI Safety Index panel

Who works on it

  • Anthropic: Publishes and updates its Responsible Scaling Policy, with Risk Reports and external review that its Long-Term Benefit Trust can request. Responsible Scaling Policy (version 3.4) (2026)
  • Google DeepMind: Publishes the Frontier Safety Framework, now with manipulation, misalignment and internal-deployment provisions. Strengthening our Frontier Safety Framework (2025)
  • Centre for the Governance of AI: Researched best practices for labs, including the expert survey and the three-lines-of-defence model. Three lines of defense against risks from AI (2022)
  • SaferAI: Rates companies' risk management practices and their frontier safety frameworks. AI risk management ratings (2026)
  • Future of Life Institute: Publishes the AI Safety Index, an expert-panel grading of companies' safety practices. AI Safety Index, Summer 2026 (2026)
  • The Midas Project: Tracks changes to and violations of company AI safety policies (Watchtower) and Seoul commitment compliance (Seoul Tracker). Projects (Watchtower, Seoul Tracker) (2026)
  • AI Lab Watch: Scored companies on safety practices until September 2025, when it stopped updating. AI Lab Watch scorecard (2025)
  • Institute for AI Policy and Strategy: Proposed risk reports for models deployed only inside AI companies. Risk Reporting for Developers' Internal AI Model Use (2026)
  • Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI), NIST: Co-issued voluntary incident-readiness practices for frontier developers' training and evaluation. Key Practices for Incident Readiness and Response in Super Intelligence Training and Evaluation (2026)

In their own words

While they have already taken some measures to mitigate these risks, best practices have not yet emerged. ... For example, 98% of respondents somewhat or strongly agreed that AGI labs should conduct pre-deployment risk assessments, dangerous capabilities evaluations, third-party model audits, safety restrictions on model usage, and red teaming.

Jonas Schuett, Noemi Dreksler, Markus Anderljung, David McCaffary, Lennart Heim, Emma Bluemke, Ben Garfinkel (GovAI), Towards best practices in AGI safety and governance: A survey of expert opinion, 2023

However, it is not always clear who is responsible for AI risk management. The Three Lines of Defense (3LoD) model, which is considered best practice in many industries, might offer a solution. It is a risk management framework that helps organizations to assign and coordinate risk management roles and responsibilities.

Jonas Schuett (GovAI), Three lines of defense against risks from AI, 2022
