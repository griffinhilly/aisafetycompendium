AI welfare

Moral-circle expansion to AI

Study empirically who extends moral concern to AI and why.

Open in the compendium

This is empirical social science on who extends moral concern to AI systems, how much, and why. The 'moral circle' is the set of beings a person or society thinks deserve moral consideration; the work asks whether and how it is expanding to include AI. It matters for safety and governance because public beliefs about AI minds will shape regulation, how people use companion chatbots, and how societies respond if AI systems start claiming rights.

How it works

The main tool is the survey. Sentience Institute's AI, Morality, and Sentience (AIMS) survey asks nationally representative samples of US adults the same questions in repeated waves (2021, 2023 and 2024), for example whether any current AI system is sentient, whether sentient AI should have legal rights, and whether developing sentient AI should be banned. Comparing waves shows how attitudes shift as AI spreads. Smaller experiments test what drives concern: in one preregistered study of 300 Americans, the strongest predictors of moral consideration for AI were substratism (the belief that what a mind is made of matters morally), identifying as a science-fiction fan, techno-animism, and positive emotions toward AI.

Other researchers ask related questions. Clara Colombatto and Steve Fleming found that about two-thirds of a US sample thought ChatGPT has some degree of consciousness, and that people who used it more often were more likely to say so. Lucius Caviola, Jeff Sebo and Jonathan Birch draw on how people perceive animal minds to predict which psychological, social and economic factors will shape views on AI consciousness.

Where research stands

The field is small but now has several years of comparable data. Its headline finding is that public concern for AI welfare is higher than many expected and rose between 2021 and 2023: in 2023 about one in five US adults believed some AI systems are already sentient, and 38% supported legal rights for sentient AI. At the same time, most people support restrictions: in 2023, 61.5% of the main survey sample and 69.5% of a supplementary sample supported banning the development of sentience in AI. Public views often run ahead of expert views; a 2023 expert report found that no current AI system appears to be conscious.

Methods remain a weak point. A February 2026 Sentience Institute study found that an opt-in online panel (Prolific) differed from a nationally representative sample on 82% of survey items, with less moral concern for AI, so results depend heavily on who is asked. Survey answers also measure perception, not whether AI systems actually have moral status, and critics argue that perceptions driven by human-like chatbots are exactly what should not guide policy.

Key results

  • In a nationally representative US survey (N = 3,500 across 2021 and 2023), mind perception and moral concern for AI rose significantly; in 2023 one in five adults thought some AI is already sentient and 38% backed legal rights for sentient AI. Jacy Reese Anthis, Janet V. T. Pauketat, Ali Ladak, Aikaterina Manoli (Sentience Institute), 2025
  • Moral consideration of AI has three dimensions (mind perception, psychological expansion, practical consideration), and substratism, sci-fi fandom, techno-animism and positive emotions were its strongest predictors. Janet V. T. Pauketat and Jacy Reese Anthis, 2022
  • About two-thirds of a stratified US sample of 300 attributed some degree of consciousness to ChatGPT, and attribution rose with frequency of use. Clara Colombatto and Stephen M. Fleming, 2024
  • AI researchers (582) and the US public (838) both supported AI welfare protections more than they opposed them, but far less than protections for animals or the environment. Noemi Dreksler, Lucius Caviola, David Chalmers and others, 2025
  • In the 2024 AIMS wave, an opt-in Prolific sample scored lower than a census-balanced Ipsos sample on all six moral-consideration and social-integration indices, and 11% versus 17% believed sentient AI already exists. Janet Pauketat, Ali Ladak, Jacy Reese Anthis (Sentience Institute), 2026

Open problems

  • Survey estimates of AI moral concern vary sharply with sampling method, so it is hard to know what the public actually believes. source
  • Which psychological, social and economic factors will drive public views on AI consciousness as AI spreads, and whether those views will track any real change in AI systems, is unknown. source
  • The authors find that increased concern for some outlying groups facilitates concern for others; whether that pattern will extend to AI is an open question. source
  • The gap between public attributions of AI consciousness and expert scepticism raises the question of whose view should guide policy. source

Criticisms

  • Rising belief that AI is conscious is an illusion produced by design, and spreading it risks delusion, unhealthy attachment and premature campaigns for AI rights. Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft AI)
  • Some biases lead people astray in thinking AI could develop consciousness, so public perception is weak evidence about AI minds. Anil Seth
  • Directing moral attention to machines diverts it from people harmed by AI systems, especially the least privileged. Abeba Birhane and Jelle van Dijk

Who works on it

  • Sentience Institute: Nonprofit think tank that runs the AIMS survey and studies moral circle expansion to digital minds. Perceptions of Sentient AI and Other Digital Minds: Evidence from the AIMS Survey (2025)
  • University College London and University of Waterloo (Colombatto and Fleming): Measured how often members of the public attribute consciousness to ChatGPT and how use affects it. Folk psychological attributions of consciousness to large language models (2024)
  • NYU Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy: Co-authored surveys and analysis of how researchers and the public think about AI subjective experience. What will society think about AI consciousness? Lessons from the animal case (2025)

We present initial results from 2021 and 2023 for the nationally representative AI, Morality, and Sentience (AIMS) survey (N = 3,500). Mind perception and moral concern for AI welfare were surprisingly high and significantly increased: in 2023, one in five U.S. adults believed some AI systems are currently sentient, and 38% supported legal rights for sentient AI.

Jacy Reese Anthis, Janet V. T. Pauketat, Ali Ladak, Aikaterina Manoli (Sentience Institute), Perceptions of Sentient AI and Other Digital Minds: Evidence from the AI, Morality, and Sentience (AIMS) Survey, 2025

Understanding the moral consideration of AIs as moral patients is increasingly critical given their rapid integration into daily life and the projected proliferation of advanced AIs. ... The strongest predictors were substratism, sci-fi fan identity, techno-animism, and positive emotions.

Janet V. T. Pauketat and Jacy Reese Anthis (Sentience Institute), Predicting the moral consideration of artificial intelligences, 2022
