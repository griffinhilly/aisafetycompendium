Worker bargaining power over AI at work

Give workers and unions a negotiated say over how employers use AI, through contracts and labour agreements with tech firms.

Open in the compendium

This approach gives workers and their unions a negotiated say over how employers use AI, through collective-bargaining contracts and agreements with technology companies. It already works in some sectors: Hollywood writers and video-game performers have won binding rules on AI. Its link to AI safety is indirect: it is one of the few existing "countervailing powers" that can check how a few firms deploy AI, and keep human voices in decisions about it.

How it works

Unions bargain over AI in the same way they have long bargained over other workplace technology. Contracts can require advance notice before new technology is introduced, limit its uses, protect jobs and pay, and set up joint committees to oversee it. The UC Berkeley Labor Center's "Negotiating Tech" inventory, last updated in July 2025, collects provisions from more than 175 union agreements, drawn from a review of over 500 contracts.

The best-known example is the Writers Guild of America's 2023 contract with the studios, won after a 148-day strike. It says AI cannot write or rewrite literary material, and AI output cannot count as source material that undercuts a writer's credit. Studios cannot require writers to use AI and must disclose AI-generated material they hand over. Writers ratified a successor contract in 2026, running to May 1, 2030, which keeps these terms and adds requirements if studios license writers' scripts to generative AI systems. In July 2025 SAG-AFTRA members ratified a video-game contract requiring consent and disclosure for AI "digital replicas" of performers, with the right to suspend consent during a strike.

A second model is partnership with AI developers. In December 2023 the AFL-CIO, a US federation of labour unions, and Microsoft agreed to share information on AI with workers, create channels for workers' feedback to Microsoft's AI developers, and work jointly on policy, alongside a neutrality framework for union organising.

Where research stands

This is in practice in some sectors, mostly creative and media work, and spreading. In January 2025 more than 200 union members and technologists met in Sacramento at a conference convened by University of California labour centres to plan bargaining strategies on AI. Researchers are documenting what contracts say, but there is little evidence yet on how well the clauses work in practice, or on their effect on how AI is developed.

The main limits are reach and leverage. Collective bargaining covers only unionised workplaces. Consent clauses may help star performers more than ordinary ones, and bargaining power may shrink as automation makes labour less essential. Economists such as Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson support unions as countervailing powers to a few AI firms, while warning that labour faces an uphill battle.

Key results

  • Writers won binding rules that AI cannot write or rewrite literary material, that its output is not source material, and that studios cannot require writers to use AI. Writers Guild of America, 2023
  • WGA members ratified a four-year successor contract (90.38% in favour) that keeps the 2023 AI terms and adds requirements for licensing scripts to generative AI systems. Jeremy Kay (Screen Daily), 2026
  • Video-game performers ratified a contract with consent and disclosure rules for AI digital replicas and the right to suspend consent during a strike. Ted Litchfield (PC Gamer), 2025
  • Billed as the first partnership of its kind between a labour organisation and a technology company on AI, it set up worker education, direct feedback to AI developers and joint policy work, plus an organising neutrality framework. Microsoft and AFL-CIO, 2023
  • An inventory of provisions from more than 175 union agreements shows unions addressing technology through notice rights, job protections, limits on monitoring and joint governance committees. Lisa Kresge (UC Berkeley Labor Center), 2025

Open problems

  • Whether bargaining power survives as AI makes labour less essential to employers. source
  • How to make consent rules meaningful for workers without the leverage to refuse. source
  • How effective negotiated technology clauses are in practice, beyond what contracts say on paper. source
  • Whether worker voice can extend from how AI is used at work to how it is developed. source

Criticisms

  • Consent and disclosure clauses may prove ineffective for ordinary performers, who may face "sign this AI consent form, or you don't get the part". Ted Litchfield (PC Gamer)
  • Labour movements in a world of widespread automation will face an uphill battle to secure basic rights. Daron Acemoglu, as reported by the Wheeler Institute, London Business School

Status

In practice in some sectors. The WGA's 2023 contract (Sept 2023 to May 2026) says AI cannot write or rewrite literary material and studios cannot require writers to use it. Members ratified a successor contract in April 2026, running to May 2030.

In their own words

AI can't write or rewrite literary material, and AI-generated material will not be considered source material under the MBA, meaning that AI-generated material can't be used to undermine a writer's credit or separated rights.

Writers Guild of America, Summary of the 2023 WGA MBA, 2023

Create countervailing powers: Given the growing control of a few private sector corporations over technologies like AI, the presence of countervailing powers, such as regulatory institutions and trade unions, may be needed to ensure that citizens' and workers' voices are heard and interests taken into account.

Wheeler Institute, London Business School, Rethinking Capitalism: In Conversation with Daron Acemoglu, 2023
