Moral uncertainty procedures

Combine several ethical theories' verdicts into one decision rather than picking a theory.

Open in the compendium

Moral uncertainty procedures are rules for acting well when it is unclear which ethical theory is right. Instead of building an AI around one theory, such as pure utilitarianism, they combine the verdicts of several plausible theories, giving each a say in proportion to how likely it is to be correct. This matters for safety because an AI fully committed to a single theory could follow it to extreme conclusions that most people, and most other theories, would reject.

How it works

One procedure is the 'moral parliament', proposed informally by Nick Bostrom in 2009. Suppose an agent gives 50% credence to one theory, 30% to a second and 20% to a third. Imagine a parliament where each theory sends delegates in that proportion. The delegates bargain and vote. A small faction can still win on issues it cares about intensely by trading away influence on issues it cares less about. Toby Newberry and Toby Ord formalised the idea in 2021 and recommended a specific rule, 'proportional chances voting'.

An alternative is 'maximising expected choiceworthiness', developed at length by William MacAskill, Krister Bykvist and Toby Ord in their 2020 book Moral Uncertainty. It works like expected-value reasoning: score each option under each theory, weight the scores by the credence in each theory, and pick the highest total. This requires putting different theories' scores on a common scale, which is often hard. The book's own example: a rights-based theory says it is wrong to kill one person to save fifty, utilitarianism says it is wrong not to, and it is unclear for which theory more is at stake. This is called the problem of intertheoretic comparisons. In AI, the same ideas can be built into training: a 2021 study trained reinforcement-learning agents to act under credences split across several ethical theories.

Where research stands

The philosophy is mature. Much of the theoretical work came from philosophers at Oxford; the formal parliament report was published by Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute, which closed on 16 April 2024. Work continues elsewhere. In July 2024 Rethink Priorities released a Moral Parliament Tool that lets users represent worldviews as delegates and compare allocation methods; different methods can recommend quite different allocations. In the 2024 Philosophical Studies paper 'Disagreement, AI alignment, and bargaining', Harry Lloyd argued that both voting-based parliaments and expected-choiceworthiness fail to pick good compromise options, and proposed a bargaining-based alignment target instead.

In deployed AI, the idea appears as a disposition rather than an explicit procedure. Claude's 2026 constitution asks Claude to 'approach ethics nondogmatically' and to try to have 'calibrated uncertainty across ethical and metaethical positions'. Technical work remains early: the 2021 reinforcement-learning study found that training under moral uncertainty can curb extreme behaviour from commitment to one theory, but also hit the problem of trading off reward functions that cannot be compared on one scale.

Key results

  • Each moral theory sends delegates in proportion to its probability; delegates bargain and vote, so weaker theories can still win on issues they care most about. Nick Bostrom, 2009
  • A formal treatment of the moral parliament identifies proportional chances voting as the best specific version, and shows existing approaches can be seen as parliaments with different rules. Toby Newberry, Toby Ord, 2021
  • There are distinctive norms for deciding under moral uncertainty, and the right method depends on how the theories in question can be compared. William MacAskill, Krister Bykvist, Toby Ord, 2020
  • Reinforcement-learning agents trained with credence split across ethical theories avoided some extreme behaviour that comes from commitment to a single theory. Adrien Ecoffet, Joel Lehman, 2021
  • Voting-based moral parliaments and maximising socially expected choiceworthiness both fail to select attractive compromise options; a bargaining-theoretic target avoids this. Harry R. Lloyd, 2024
  • In a working moral-parliament tool, different allocation methods (such as expected choiceworthiness, approval voting and Nash bargaining) can recommend quite different allocations from the same credences. Derek Shiller, Arvo Muñoz Morán, Bob Fischer, Hayley Clatterbuck, 2024

Open problems

  • Solving the problem of intertheoretic comparisons: saying how much is at stake for one theory compared with another, which expected-choiceworthiness needs and which AI training meets when combining incomparable reward functions. source
  • Choosing the aggregation rule, since voting, expected-value and bargaining methods give different answers. source
  • Making the parliament model precise; its originators called it a metaphor that is under-determined. source
  • Turning a general disposition of moral humility in a language model into reliable, checkable behaviour. source

Criticisms

  • Both moral parliaments and expected-choiceworthiness fail in cases where an attractive compromise option exists, so neither is a good alignment target under disagreement. Harry R. Lloyd (Philosophical Studies, 2024)
  • The parliament is 'a kind of metaphor' that is not literally correct and is under-determined, so it does not by itself yield a decision rule. Nick Bostrom (on his own proposal)

Who works on it

  • Future of Humanity Institute (closed April 2024): Produced the formal moral-parliament report before closing on 16 April 2024. The Parliamentary Approach to Moral Uncertainty (2021)
  • Rethink Priorities (Worldview Investigations team): Built a public Moral Parliament Tool for decisions under normative uncertainty. Rethink Priorities' Moral Parliament Tool (2024)

In their own words

Now imagine that each of these theories gets to send some number of delegates to The Parliament. The number of delegates each theory gets to send is proportional to the probability of the theory. Then the delegates bargain with one another for support on various issues; and the Parliament reaches a decision by the delegates voting.

Nick Bostrom (Overcoming Bias), Moral uncertainty - towards a solution?, 2009

The idea here is that moral theories get more influence the more probable they are; yet even a relatively weak theory can still get its way on some issues that the theory think are extremely important by sacrificing its influence on other issues that other theories deem more important.

Nick Bostrom (Overcoming Bias), Moral uncertainty - towards a solution?, 2009
