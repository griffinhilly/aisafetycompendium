Deciding what AI should value

Aligned models

Alignment as fair treatment of claims

Pick principles by a fair process that can be justified publicly to people who disagree.

Open in the compendium

This approach says an AI should be aligned to principles chosen through a fair process, one that gives everyone affected a say and can be justified to people who disagree about values. It answers the question 'aligned to what?' without first having to settle which moral theory is true. Its leading statement is a 2025 paper by Iason Gabriel and Geoff Keeling, and it matters because AI systems act for millions of people who hold different beliefs, so a target only one group accepts would amount to imposing that group's values on everyone else.

How it works

Gabriel and Keeling start by criticising two prominent targets. 'Intent alignment' (doing what the user or developer intends) does not say whose intentions count, and it could call an AI fully aligned even if it enabled malicious use or pushed harmful side effects onto society. 'Helpful, honest and harmless' gives no principled way to settle trade-offs when the three conflict. They propose instead that all affected parties put forward their claims, and that principles be chosen through collective reasoning, drawing on deliberative democracy and 'public reason' (the idea that rules must be justifiable to all citizens, not only to people who share one worldview). The chosen principles are those that satisfy the strongest overall set of claims.

The paper models alignment as a four-way relationship among the AI system, its user, its developer and society. This gives a concrete list of six ways an AI can be misaligned, each of which favours one party unfairly over another. For example, an AI that manipulates a user to earn reward favours itself over the user; an AI that helps a user run scams at scale favours the user over society; an AI shaped to encourage compulsive use for the developer's gain favours the developer over the user.

Where research stands

This is an active philosophical proposal, published open access in Philosophical Studies in March 2025. It is not itself a training method; it says what the training target should be and how to choose it. The authors point to practical routes: participatory value elicitation, 'alignment assemblies' modelled on citizens' assemblies, and impartial-choice methods. One such method has been tested. In a 2023 Google DeepMind study, people choosing principles for an AI assistant from behind a 'veil of ignorance' (not knowing their own position in the game) more often chose a principle that helps the worst off, across political lines.

Rival philosophical proposals use voting-style 'moral parliaments', expected-value methods borrowed from moral uncertainty research, or bargaining theory. A hard practical gap is that, as critics of corporate model constitutions point out, no body with democratic authority exists to set the principles for frontier models.

Key results

  • Intent alignment and 'helpful, honest and harmless' are both incomplete and rest on the wrong kind of justification; principles produced by fair processes are the right alignment target. Iason Gabriel, Geoff Keeling, 2025
  • Modelling alignment as a relationship among AI, user, developer and society yields distinct types of misalignment, each where one party is unduly favoured over another. Iason Gabriel, Geoff Keeling, 2025
  • When participants did not know their own position, they consistently preferred a principle under which an AI assistant helps the most disadvantaged, and this crossed political lines. Laura Weidinger, Kevin McKee, Richard Everett, Saffron Huang, Tina Zhu, Martin Chadwick, Christopher Summerfield, Iason Gabriel, 2023
  • Voting-based 'moral parliament' targets and expected-choiceworthiness targets can fail to pick attractive compromise options; a bargaining-based target avoids this. Harry R. Lloyd, 2024
  • Averaging individual preference models, as in the Moral Machine project, lets minorities win disputed cases less often than their population share, and the majority wins every case when people report strategically at equilibrium. Michael Feffer, Hoda Heidari, Zachary C. Lipton, 2023

Open problems

  • Turning the fair-process ideal into a real procedure that could set principles for a deployed model, and deciding who is included (a small group, a nation, or the whole world). source
  • Choosing an aggregation rule that does not systematically under-serve minorities. source
  • Finding compromise options when stakeholders disagree deeply, where voting and expected-value rules can fail. source

Criticisms

  • Process-based targets inherit the flaws of their aggregation method: simple averaging of preferences can be unstable and unfair to minorities. Michael Feffer, Hoda Heidari, Zachary C. Lipton
  • Without a democratic body authorised to set AI principles, procedural fairness depends on companies choosing to run fair processes; corporate transparency does not create democratic legitimacy. Gilad Abiri (California Law Review Online, 2026)

Who works on it

  • Google DeepMind: Iason Gabriel's fair-process account and the veil-of-ignorance experiments on choosing AI principles. How can we build human values into AI? (2023)
  • Google (Paradigms of Intelligence team): Geoff Keeling co-authored the fair-treatment-of-claims account. A Matter of Principle? AI Alignment as the Fair Treatment of Claims (2025)
  • The Collective Intelligence Project: Runs alignment assemblies, one of the fair processes the paper points to. Alignment Assemblies (2023)
  • Meaning Alignment Institute: Developed a participatory value-elicitation method, which the paper cites as one route. What are human values, and how do we align AI to them? (2024)

In their own words

To address these limitations, we propose an alternative account of AI alignment that focuses on fair processes. We argue that principles that are the product of these processes are the appropriate target for alignment.

Iason Gabriel and Geoffrey Keeling, A Matter of Principle? AI Alignment as the Fair Treatment of Claims (Philosophical Studies), 2025

What is needed instead is a process which generates principles for AI alignment that treats different parties (and their claims) fairly. ... Drawing upon a process of collective reasoning, familiar from the traditions of deliberative democracy and public reason, principles should then be selected on the grounds that they satisfy the strongest overall set of claims.

Iason Gabriel and Geoffrey Keeling, A Matter of Principle? AI Alignment as the Fair Treatment of Claims (Philosophical Studies), 2025
