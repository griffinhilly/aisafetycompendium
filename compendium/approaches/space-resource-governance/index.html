Space resource governance

Agree rules now against exclusive grabs of offworld resources, before advanced AI makes them reachable.

Open in the compendium

This approach aims to agree rules now on who may claim and use resources in space, such as asteroid metals or lunar ice, before advanced AI makes large-scale space industry possible. The concern is that one country or company with a temporary AI lead could grab offworld resources and turn that lead into permanent dominance without fighting a war. It matters for safety because it targets one route to an extreme and lasting concentration of power.

How it works

William MacAskill and Fin Moorhouse set out the case in Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion (Forethought, March 2025). They argue that the fast technological progress of an intelligence explosion could drive down launch costs and make harvesting offworld resources profitable. Industry in space could then grow larger than all industry on Earth, so the first actor to grab it could dominate everyone else. Star systems may also be easy to defend, so early settlers could lock in control indefinitely. The proposed remedy is clear space governance agreed well in advance: outright bans on owning or using space resources, a requirement for UN approval before claims, or at least widely endorsed norms. If an exclusive grab plainly broke international law, other states would be more likely to act against it early.

The legal starting point already exists. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty, with 118 parties as of October 2025, forbids any state from appropriating a celestial body by claim of sovereignty, use, occupation or any other means. It is silent on whether resources extracted from a body can be owned, and that gap is where today's negotiations sit.

Where research stands

Most current activity happens in space law, not AI safety, and states are moving in different directions. The US holds that outer space is not a 'global commons' and rejects the 1979 Moon Agreement, which only 18 countries had ratified by 2020 and the US has never joined. The US-led Artemis Accords, a common set of principles for civil exploration of the Moon, Mars and beyond, reached 76 signatory countries by 28 September 2026. At the UN, the Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS) has a working group drafting non-binding principles for space resource activities. Its September 2026 draft leaves title to extracted material undefined, and makes benefit-sharing voluntary and 'as appropriate'. Further meetings are planned before the April 2027 Legal Subcommittee session.

From the AI angle, the approach is new and small. The 80,000 Hours problem profile on space governance (first published February 2022) estimated that fewer than five people researched space governance from a long-term perspective. It also notes that transformative AI may arrive before large space projects and could make early space rules irrelevant.

Key results

  • If one nation or company grabs offworld resources first, it could turn a temporary technological lead into overwhelming material dominance without military action; robust space governance agreed in advance could change this. William MacAskill, Fin Moorhouse (Forethought), 2025
  • Space governance could shape the long-run future by preventing conflict and avoiding lock-in of poor norms, with rules on property and the distribution of windfalls as key questions. Fin Moorhouse (80,000 Hours), 2022
  • The US declared in April 2020 that it does not view outer space as a global commons, and that it rejects the Moon Agreement as customary international law. The White House, 2020
  • The September 2026 COPUOS draft principles on space resources do not define title to extracted material, and make benefit-sharing voluntary; if adopted, they would be COPUOS's first consensus instrument on the topic. Güneş Ünüvar (Opinio Juris), 2026
  • The Artemis Accords reached 76 signatory countries in September 2026, nearly two-thirds of Outer Space Treaty signatories. NASA, 2026

Open problems

  • Whether rules agreed today would still bind an actor that gains a decisive AI-driven lead later. source
  • Who can own resources once extracted, which the Outer Space Treaty does not settle. source
  • How to share the benefits of space resources when current drafts make sharing voluntary. source
  • How to reconcile the US-led Artemis approach, which treats extraction as lawful, with states that resist ownership claims. source

Criticisms

  • Outer space is not a global commons, and Americans should be free to recover and use space resources commercially; the Moon Agreement is not an effective or necessary instrument. US executive order on space resources (6 April 2020)
  • Transformative AI may arrive before large space projects and could wash out early space governance work, so working on AI directly may be more pressing; and powerful countries and companies mostly act on existing interests. Fin Moorhouse (80,000 Hours)
  • The emerging consensus on space resources is fragile and far from settled, and soft-law language may quietly shift how the binding treaty is read. Güneş Ünüvar (University of Exeter), January 2026

In their own words

And if one nation or company grabs offworld resources first, they could turn a temporary technological lead into a huge material advantage: coming to utterly dominate others without ever engaging in military actions ... or even ever making other countries or companies worse-off in absolute terms. Robust space governance could change how resource acquisition plays out.

William MacAskill, Fin Moorhouse (Forethought), Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion, 2025

We could advocate for restrictions on offworld resource grabs. Restrictions could take the form of outright bans on owning or using space resources, regulations such as requiring UN approval before claiming or using space resources, or just widely-endorsed norms.

William MacAskill, Fin Moorhouse (Forethought), Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion, 2025
