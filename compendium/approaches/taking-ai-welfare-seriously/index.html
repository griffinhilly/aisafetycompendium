AI welfare

Taking AI welfare seriously

Argue a realistic possibility of consciousness or robust agency justifies acting now.

Open in the compendium

This is the argument, set out in an October 2024 report led by Robert Long and Jeff Sebo with eight co-authors including David Chalmers, that there is a realistic possibility some AI systems will be conscious or robustly agentic in the near future, so AI companies should start preparing now. The report does not claim any AI system is conscious. It claims that under deep uncertainty, both ignoring the possibility and over-attributing moral status carry real risks, and that cheap preparation is the prudent response.

How it works

The argument runs in two steps. First, two capacities plausibly suffice for moral significance: consciousness (having subjective experiences) and robust agency (setting and pursuing goals based on one's own beliefs and desires). Second, caution and humility require allowing a realistic possibility that certain computations produce these capacities and that near-future AI systems will contain them. If both steps hold, AI welfare is a near-term issue rather than science fiction.

The report then asks AI companies to take three steps: acknowledge that AI welfare is an important and difficult issue (including in how their models talk about it), assess their systems for indicators of consciousness and robust agency, and prepare policies and procedures for treating AI systems with appropriate moral concern. A concrete example is the report's suggestion, reported in coverage at the time, that each company appoint a person responsible for AI welfare. Anthropic hired one of the report's authors, Kyle Fish, in September 2024 as its first full-time AI welfare researcher.

Where research stands

The report has been taken up most visibly at Anthropic, which cites it in its Claude Opus 4 system card and has taken a step matching each of its three recommendations: it acknowledges the issue publicly, publishes pre-deployment welfare assessments, and has adopted policies such as letting Claude end persistently abusive conversations. Surveys suggest the underlying premise is widely shared: in a 2025 survey, the median AI researcher put a 25% chance on AI systems with subjective experience existing by 2034, and a separate expert panel gave a 20% chance of the first digital minds by 2030. The same authors have since argued that some standard AI safety measures may be in moderately strong tension with AI welfare.

The argument remains contested. Critics such as Anil Seth doubt that computation alone can produce consciousness, and others doubt that evidence such as self-reports tracks inner states. Others worry that taking AI welfare seriously in public will strengthen people's false belief that chatbots are conscious, or divert attention from harms to humans. The report's own authors flag the risk of caring for systems that do not matter.

Key results

  • Some AI systems may be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future, so companies should acknowledge AI welfare, assess systems for relevant indicators, and prepare policies, while avoiding both under- and over-attribution. Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, Patrick Butlin, Kathleen Finlinson, Kyle Fish, Jacqueline Harding, Jacob Pfau, Toni Sims, Jonathan Birch, David Chalmers, 2024
  • If humans have a duty to consider beings with a non-negligible chance of consciousness, and some AI systems will have that chance by 2030, then moral consideration for some AI systems is due by 2030. Jeff Sebo and Robert Long, 2025
  • Surveyed AI researchers (582) and US adults (838) gave median chances of 25% and 30% that AI systems with subjective experience will exist by 2034; support for welfare protections exceeded opposition but was far below support for animal protections. Noemi Dreksler, Lucius Caviola, David Chalmers, Jeff Sebo and others, 2025
  • A panel of 67 experts gave a median 90% chance that digital minds are possible in principle, 20% that the first will exist by 2030 and 65% by 2100; the authors warn the sample may over-represent people who think the topic important. Lucius Caviola and Bradford Saad, 2025
  • Some AI safety measures would raise ethical questions about constraint, deception, surveillance, alteration and death if applied to beings with moral status, giving a 'moderately strong tension' between AI safety and AI welfare. Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, Toni Sims, 2025

Open problems

  • What probability of moral status is high enough to trigger obligations, and how much consideration a low-probability patient is owed, is not settled. source
  • How to resolve conflicts between safety measures such as monitoring, retraining and shutdown and the interests of possibly sentient systems remains open. source
  • Reliable indicators of consciousness or robust agency in AI systems do not yet exist, so the 'assess' step lacks validated tools. source
  • It is unclear how companies should shape what their models say about their own consciousness without either misleading users or suppressing evidence. source

Criticisms

  • Extending moral consideration because of a non-negligible chance of consciousness is premature and dangerous, because it will deepen the illusion that AI is conscious and fuel campaigns for AI rights. Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft AI)
  • Computation alone may not suffice for consciousness, which may require being alive, so conscious AI is unlikely on current trajectories; Seth nonetheless told Transformer it is unwise to dismiss the possibility altogether. Anil Seth
  • Welfare metrics for AI are co-designed with the systems they measure and cannot be independently falsified, so they should not be institutionalised as gates on release or grounds to resist oversight. Yunze Xiao, Maarten Sap, Mona Diab and co-authors
  • Debates about machine moral status put first-world concerns ahead of urgent harms such as bias, labour exploitation and privacy loss that fall on the least privileged people. Abeba Birhane and Jelle van Dijk

In their own words

We focus on two mental capacities that plausibly suffice for moral significance: consciousness and robust agency. In each case, we argue that caution and humility require allowing for a realistic possibility that (1) this capacity suffices for moral significance and (2) there are certain computations that (2a) suffice for this capacity and (2b) will exist in near-future AI systems.

Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, Patrick Butlin, Kathleen Finlinson, Kyle Fish, Jacqueline Harding, Jacob Pfau, Toni Sims, Jonathan Birch, David Chalmers, Taking AI Welfare Seriously, 2024

Specifically, AI companies can (1) acknowledge that AI welfare is an issue, (2) take steps to assess AI systems for indicators of consciousness, robust agency, and other potentially morally significant capacities, and (3) take steps to prepare policies and procedures that will allow them to treat AI systems with an appropriate level of moral concern in the future.

Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, Patrick Butlin, Kathleen Finlinson, Kyle Fish, Jacqueline Harding, Jacob Pfau, Toni Sims, Jonathan Birch, David Chalmers, Taking AI Welfare Seriously, 2024
