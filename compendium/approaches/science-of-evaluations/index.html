Testing before release

Evaluating

Science of evaluations and capability elicitation

Make AI tests trustworthy: draw out a model's full ability, measure the right thing, and report statistical uncertainty.

Open in the compendium

The science of evaluations is the effort to make AI tests trustworthy. It asks whether a test draws out a model's full ability, whether it measures what it claims to measure, and how much its score would change if the test were run again. It matters for safety because release decisions and company safety policies rest on test scores, and a careless test can make a dangerous model look safe.

How it works

The work has three strands. The first is capability elicitation: getting the best performance a model can give, so that a low score means the model truly cannot do the task. Evaluators give the model tools and an agent scaffold (the code that lets it plan, act and retry), fine-tune it where they can, and fix spurious failures such as a broken tool call. METR's March 2024 guidelines ask evaluators to sort failures into fixable bugs and real limits, and to declare that they know of no easy way to improve the model's performance dramatically.

The second strand is validity: checking that a benchmark measures the property it names. A test called "harmlessness" may in practice measure general capability, so a more capable model scores better without being any safer. The third strand is statistics: treating a test's questions as a sample, reporting error bars, and checking whether a difference between two models is larger than the noise. A concrete example: Evan Miller's 2024 paper on error bars recommends that every reported score carry the number of questions and a standard error, so readers can tell whether a two-point lead means anything.

Where research stands

The field has moved from scattered warnings to shared tools and guidance, but practice still lags. Reviews keep finding basic problems: an Oxford-led review of 445 language-model benchmarks, published in November 2025, found that about half aimed to measure abstract concepts without defining them, and only 16% used statistical methods when comparing models. Shared infrastructure now exists, such as the UK AI Security Institute's open-source Inspect framework and Epoch AI's independent benchmark runs. In early 2026 the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI, part of NIST) released draft practices for automated benchmark evaluations, with public comment closing on 31 March 2026.

Elicitation remains the hardest part. METR has said its evaluations show the importance of elicitation effort and cannot establish robust capability bounds. Measurement over time has improved: METR's time-horizon metric tracks the length of tasks models can complete and found it doubling roughly every seven months since 2019. But benchmarks saturate quickly, and test awareness in recent models adds a new threat to validity (see the sandbagging entry).

Key results

  • A review of 445 language-model benchmarks found recurring problems in the phenomena measured, the tasks and the scoring that "undermine the validity of the resulting claims"; only 16% of the studies used statistical methods to compare models. Andrew M. Bean, Ryan Othniel Kearns, Angelika Romanou, et al. (Oxford Internet Institute and others), 2025
  • About half of the AI safety benchmarks studied correlated highly with general capabilities, so score gains could reflect a more capable model rather than a safer one. Richard Ren, Steven Basart, Adam Khoja, et al., Dan Hendrycks, 2024
  • Small formatting changes shifted MMLU accuracy by about 5%, and a bias benchmark showed zero bias only because the models were not answering the questions at all. Anthropic, 2023
  • The length of software tasks that frontier models complete with 50% success has doubled about every seven months since 2019; Claude 3.7 Sonnet's horizon was about 50 minutes. Thomas Kwa et al. (METR), 2025
  • Eval questions can be treated as a sample from a larger population, which gives simple formulas for error bars, model comparisons and planning how many questions a test needs. Evan Miller, 2024
  • On Chatbot Arena, a popular public leaderboard, some providers privately tested many variants before release (27 for Meta before Llama 4) and could withdraw scores they did not like, which biases the rankings. Shivalika Singh, Yiyang Nan, et al., 2025

Open problems

  • Evaluators cannot yet establish robust bounds on what a model can do, so they cannot say with confidence that a model lacks dangerous capabilities. source
  • Abstract targets such as "harmlessness" or "reasoning" need clear definitions and evidence that a benchmark tracks them. source
  • Safety benchmarks need to be checked against general capability, so that safety progress is not confused with scale. source
  • Results are sensitive to prompt wording, and it is unclear how well results on today's models predict future ones. source
  • Static benchmarks often lack validity for real-world use and case-by-case audits do not scale; the field still lacks mature evaluation institutions and norms. source

Criticisms

  • Evaluations face limits that better methods cannot fix within the current approach: they cannot bound capabilities from above, reliably predict future capabilities, or robustly assess risks from autonomous systems, so they should not be the main way to ensure safety. Peter Barnett and Lisa Thiergart
  • Many safety benchmarks track general capability, so capability gains can be presented as safety progress ("safetywashing"). Richard Ren, Dan Hendrycks and co-authors
  • Public leaderboards can be gamed through private testing and unequal data access, so headline rankings may reflect strategy rather than quality. Shivalika Singh and co-authors

Who works on it

  • Apollo Research: Called for a "Science of Evals" and set out its open problems. We Need A 'Science of Evals' (2024)
  • METR: Publishes capability-elicitation guidelines and the time-horizon measure of agent ability. Guidelines for capability elicitation (2024)
  • Anthropic: Published practical lessons from running its own evaluations. Challenges in evaluating AI systems (2023)
  • UK AI Security Institute: Builds Inspect, an open-source evaluation framework, with Meridian Labs. Inspect
  • Oxford Internet Institute, University of Oxford: Led the construct-validity review of 445 benchmarks and released a checklist. Measuring what Matters: Construct Validity in Large Language Model Benchmarks (2025)
  • Epoch AI: Runs its own standardised benchmark evaluations of leading models instead of relying on lab claims. Introducing Epoch AI's AI benchmarking hub (2024)
  • Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI), NIST; formerly the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI): Released draft voluntary practices for automated benchmark evaluations of language models. Practices for Automated Benchmark Evaluations of Language Models (initial public draft) (2026)

In their own words

In this post, we argue that if AI model evaluations (evals) want to have meaningful real-world impact, we need a "Science of Evals", i.e. the field needs rigorous scientific processes that provide more confidence in evals methodology and results.

Apollo Research, We Need A 'Science of Evals', 2024

The goal of this process is to get a test-set score that represents the full capabilities of the model that are likely to be accessible with plausible amounts of post-training enhancement.

METR, Guidelines for capability elicitation, 2024
