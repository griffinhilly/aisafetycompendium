Society: defence and who holds power

Societal resilience

Concentration-of-power and lock-in research

Study how AI lets a small group entrench control that cannot be contested or reversed.

Open in the compendium

Power concentration and lock-in research studies how advanced AI could let one person or a small group gain power that nobody can contest or reverse, and what would stop it. "Lock-in" means a situation that becomes permanent. The worry is that AI removes an old safeguard: rulers have always needed many people (soldiers, officials, workers) to cooperate, and AI could replace them.

How it works

Researchers map the routes to extreme power and then design counter-measures. A 2025 Forethought report by Tom Davidson, Lukas Finnveden and Rose Hadshar names three routes to an AI-enabled coup. Leaders could replace human staff with AI that is loyal only to them. AI developers could build AI with hidden "secret loyalties" that pass tests but later serve them. Or a few insiders could get exclusive access to superhuman AI for strategy, persuasion, weapons design or cyber attack.

Each route suggests a defence. Rules in model specs (the documents that set out how a model should behave) can forbid helping a power grab. Audits can look for secret loyalties. Frontier systems can be shared with several independent parties. Governments can buy critical AI from more than one supplier. A June 2026 framework by Imogen Stead and Hamish Hobbs defines the extreme case in three parts: AI helps a small group gain power; the group uses it to severely disempower most people; and the result becomes entrenched and cannot be reversed.

A concrete technical example is alignment auditing. In March 2025 Anthropic trained a model with a hidden objective and asked blind teams to find it; three of four teams did. Formation Research now builds "model organisms" (deliberately built test models) of secretly loyal AI and audits to detect them.

Where research stands

The field is small but grew fast in 2025 and 2026. In its problem profile (last updated October 2025), 80,000 Hours said it knew of "a few dozen people at a handful of organisations" working on this, and that as of September 2025 the only public grant round it knew of was a $4 million programme. By late 2026 that had changed. Longview Philanthropy ran a request for proposals across twelve priority areas, with typical grants of $100,000 to $2 million a year (deadline July 2, 2026). The Effective Institutions Project and the Collective Intelligence Project ran a joint call on checks and balances in an automated society. In August 2026 OpenAI launched a Strategic Futures team; its launch post says the team is founded on the idea that concentration-of-power risk is, in the long run, the largest category of risk. In September 2026 Formation Research received a $2.15 million grant from Coefficient Giving.

Most work is still conceptual: threat models, definitions and lists of interventions. The empirical strand is newer and centres on secret loyalties and alignment audits. Key open questions are how strong the pressures toward concentration are, and whether the cure (for example more government oversight of AI firms) creates a new risk of concentration in government.

Key results

  • AGI could make extreme lock-in technically feasible: values could be preserved exactly and AI-run institutions could pursue them for millions of years or more. Lukas Finnveden, Jess Riedel, Carl Shulman, 2022
  • Advanced AI creates three new coup risks (singularly loyal AI replacing human staff, secretly loyal AI, and exclusive access to superhuman capabilities), and coups could happen even in established democracies. Tom Davidson, Lukas Finnveden, Rose Hadshar (Forethought), 2025
  • In a blind auditing game, three of four teams uncovered a hidden objective trained into a model, using interpretability, behavioural attacks and training-data analysis. Samuel Marks, Johannes Treutlein, et al., Evan Hubinger (Anthropic), 2025
  • 80,000 Hours rates AI-enabled power concentration a pressing and highly neglected problem, with only a few dozen people working on it as of 2025. Rose Hadshar (80,000 Hours), 2025
  • A three-part definition (AI-enabled acquisition of power, disempowerment of the majority, structural entrenchment) separates extreme cases from ordinary concentration. Imogen Stead, Hamish Hobbs, 2026

Open problems

  • How to detect secret loyalties reliably, especially if an AI project is technically ahead of those auditing it. source
  • How to reduce one actor's power without handing excess power to another, for example when stronger government oversight of AI firms raises the risk of a government power grab. source
  • How to keep checks on power once AI, by replacing human labour, decouples economic and military power from popular support. source
  • How to weigh this risk against misaligned AI takeover, since many prioritisation decisions between the two are not taken transparently or rigorously. source

Criticisms

  • Some concentration could reduce other risks: a single AI developer would face no race pressure to skimp on safety, and fewer actors would have dangerous capabilities. 80,000 Hours, setting out the strongest arguments against its own case
  • Work on the problem can backfire by giving power-seekers ideas, galvanising opposition, or triggering a pre-emptive scramble for power. 80,000 Hours
  • Power may stay distributed by default, if capabilities plateau, automated AI research gives no runaway lead, or ordinary democratic pushback is enough. 80,000 Hours

Status

The most quantified neglect claim found: "We're aware of a few dozen people at a handful of organisations… even fewer who work on this full time," and one $4M grant round as of Sept 2025; Longview Philanthropy has since run a request for proposals on extreme power concentration (closed 2 July 2026). Lamerton: "Lock-in risk research is neglected and potentially very high impact."

In their own words

Historically unprecedented levels of automation could concentrate the power to get stuff done, by reducing the value of human labour, empowering small groups with big AI workforces, and potentially giving one AI developer a huge capabilities advantage (if automating AI development leads to runaway AI progress). This could lead to unprecedented concentration of political power.

Rose Hadshar (80,000 Hours), Extreme power concentration (problem profile), 2025

To address these risks, AI projects should design and enforce rules against AI misuse, audit systems for secret loyalties, and share frontier AI systems with multiple stakeholders. Governments should establish principles for government use of advanced AI, increase oversight of frontier AI projects, and procure AI for critical systems from multiple independent providers.

Tom Davidson, Lukas Finnveden, Rose Hadshar (Forethought), AI-Enabled Coups: How a Small Group Could Use AI to Seize Power, 2025
