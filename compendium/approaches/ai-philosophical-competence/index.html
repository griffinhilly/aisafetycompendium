Deciding what AI should value

Aligned models

AI philosophical competence (metaphilosophy)

Make AI systems better at reasoning about hard philosophical questions, so they help rather than mislead on them.

Open in the compendium

This approach aims to make AI systems good at philosophy: able to reason well about questions that have no settled method, such as what is valuable, how to make decisions, or what we owe future beings. Metaphilosophy is the study of what philosophy is and how it makes progress. The worry behind it is that a powerful AI, or a human-AI civilisation, guided by wrong philosophical ideas could cause 'astronomical' waste, in Wei Dai's word.

How it works

The idea starts from the observation that AI capability and AI wisdom may not grow together. A model could become excellent at coding and science while still reasoning badly about ethics, decision theory or the long-term future. Wei Dai's 2019 post 'Some Thoughts on Metaphilosophy' lays out several ways to view philosophy, for example as a way of answering confusing questions that lack an established method, or as reasoning about out-of-distribution situations where machine-learning systems tend to extrapolate unpredictably.

His main hope is to solve metaphilosophy: to understand philosophical reasoning well enough to build it into AI on purpose. Until then, he discusses two stopgaps. One is to protect the human process of philosophical reasoning, for example through global coordination to stop technological progress. The other is to train models to imitate human philosophical behaviour, which runs into the problem that much of the relevant reasoning is hidden state that cannot be observed, and there is no good way to check that the model has learned it correctly. A related research line on 'wise' AI argues that the missing ingredient is metacognition: strategies such as intellectual humility, taking other perspectives, and adapting to context.

Where research stands

This is an early area. Wei Dai has framed the problem in a series of LessWrong posts; in November 2025 he described AI philosophical competence as 'largely still illegible and hence neglected', and said that pausing AI should be plan A, with improving AI philosophical competence as part of plan B. Forethought's March 2025 report 'AI Tools for Existential Security' lists 'AI for philosophy' among the applications worth speeding up, to help humanity avoid subtle but catastrophic moral errors, and in June 2026 Forethought's podcast interviewed Wei Dai on whether the wisdom of frontier AIs will lag their raw capabilities.

Adjacent academic work is starting. A paper by Samuel Johnson, Yoshua Bengio, Melanie Mitchell, Igor Grossmann and others (first posted in November 2024) argues that AI is getting smarter but not wiser, and discusses how wise AI might be benchmarked and trained. Claude's 2026 constitution touches the issue: it asks the model to approach ethics 'nondogmatically', with calibrated uncertainty across ethical and metaethical positions.

Key results

  • An AI guided by wrong philosophical ideas would likely cause astronomical waste, and imitating human philosophy with machine learning faces the problem that the relevant reasoning is hidden and hard to verify. Wei Dai, 2019
  • AI philosophical competence is needed both for AI-assisted safety research and for avoiding catastrophic philosophical errors after AGI, and remains largely illegible and neglected. Wei Dai, 2025
  • AI for philosophy, helping with moral reflection and hard philosophical questions, is among the AI applications worth accelerating to avoid subtle but catastrophic moral errors. Lizka Vaintrob, Owen Cotton-Barratt, 2025
  • AI systems particularly struggle with metacognition; better metacognition would make them more robust in novel settings, more explainable, more cooperative and safer. Samuel G. B. Johnson, Amir-Hossein Karimi, Yoshua Bengio, Nick Chater, Tobias Gerstenberg and others, 2024

Open problems

  • Verifying that a model has learned good philosophical reasoning, when there is no agreed answer key to check it against. source
  • Benchmarking and training metacognitive skills such as intellectual humility and perspective-taking. source
  • Making the problem legible to researchers, funders, company leaders and policymakers outside a small online community. source
  • Knowing whether the wisdom of frontier AIs will lag their raw capabilities. source

Criticisms

  • Improving AI philosophical competence is only a fallback; Wei Dai, who frames the problem, says pausing AI should be plan A. Wei Dai
  • Humans themselves are not yet safe to serve as builders, overseers or alignment targets for powerful AI, because human judgment is still too flawed; a long process of correcting those flaws comes first. Wei Dai (The Long Self-Correction, July 2026)

Status

Developed mainly in Wei Dai's posts. He calls the area largely neglected. In a comment he names pausing AI as plan A and improving AI philosophical competence as part of plan B.

In their own words

A powerful AI (or human-AI civilization) guided by wrong philosophical ideas would likely cause astronomical (or beyond astronomical) waste. Solving metaphilosophy is one way in which we can hope to avoid this kind of disaster.

Wei Dai, Some Thoughts on Metaphilosophy, 2019

So I continue to think that pausing/stopping AI should be plan A (which legibilizing the problem of AI philosophical competence can contribute to), with actually improving AI philosophical competence as (part of) plan B.

Wei Dai, Problems I've Tried to Legibilize (comment), 2025
