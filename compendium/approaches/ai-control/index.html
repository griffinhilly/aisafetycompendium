Releasing and running it safely

ConstrainingAlso: Evaluating

AI control

Design deployment so a misaligned model still cannot cause harm.

Open in the compendium

AI control is the practice of deploying a powerful AI system so that it cannot cause a catastrophe even if it is secretly misaligned and actively trying to get around the safety measures. Instead of proving that a model has good goals, control assumes the worst and asks whether monitoring, restrictions and human review would still catch or block a bad action. It matters because today nobody can reliably check whether a model's goals are safe, but people can test whether safeguards hold up against a deliberately adversarial model.

How it works

A developer pairs a strong but untrusted model with weaker resources it does trust: an older, less capable model, a limited budget of human reviewers, and ordinary software controls such as logging and permissions. A 'protocol' decides how these parts work together. In the first control paper (December 2023), GPT-4 wrote code and GPT-3.5 rated how suspicious each solution looked. In the 'trusted editing' protocol, suspicious code was rewritten by the weaker model before it was submitted.

To test a protocol, researchers run a 'control evaluation'. A red team (a group playing the attacker) replaces the untrusted model with an attack policy that tries as hard as it can to slip something harmful past the safeguards, such as a hidden backdoor in code or a secret download of malicious software. The blue team measures how often the attack succeeds and how much useful work the protocol gives up. A protocol is judged safe enough if the red team's best attack rarely succeeds.

The same idea scales up to AI agents working inside a company. A monitor reads each action an agent proposes; suspicious actions can be blocked, re-sampled (the agent is asked to try again), or sent to a human for audit.

Where research stands

Control moved from a single 2023 paper to a recognised research field by 2025. Redwood Research leads the agenda, and the UK AI Security Institute, Anthropic and Google DeepMind all publish on it. Studies now cover multi-step agent tasks, not just single coding answers. They show that simple monitoring and re-sampling protocols cut attack success sharply in test settings, at a small cost in usefulness. In October 2025 the UK AI Security Institute released ControlArena, a library for running control experiments, and reported that researchers at Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Redwood Research had used it. Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework (February 2025 update) says it is exploring automated monitoring as a response to models that might deliberately undermine human control. By 2026 at least one frontier lab, OpenAI, ran a misalignment monitor over its internal coding agents.

What does not yet work well: control evaluations depend on the red team finding the attacks a real misaligned model would find, and nobody can guarantee that. Untrusted monitors (copies of the same strong model used as watchers) may collude with the model they watch. Its own advocates expect control to break down for strongly superhuman systems, so they treat it as a bridge for early transformative AI, not a permanent solution.

Key results

  • The first control paper showed that protocols such as trusted editing and untrusted monitoring improve substantially on simple baselines at stopping a GPT-4 model that deliberately tries to insert backdoors into code. Ryan Greenblatt, Buck Shlegeris, Kshitij Sachan, Fabien Roger, 2023
  • In the first control evaluation in an agent setting (257 multi-step system-administration tasks), the best re-sampling protocol cut attack success from 58% to 7% at a 5% cost to the performance of a non-malicious agent. Aryan Bhatt, Cody Rushing, Adam Kaufman, Tyler Tracy, Vasil Georgiev, David Matolcsi, Akbir Khan, Buck Shlegeris, 2025
  • In SHADE-Arena, the best sabotage agents completed a hidden harmful side task without detection 27% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) and 15% (Gemini 2.5 Pro) of the time, and the best monitor reached an AUC of 0.87 at telling benign from malicious transcripts. Jonathan Kutasov, Yuqi Sun, Paul Colognese, Teun van der Weij, Linda Petrini, Chen Bo Calvin Zhang, John Hughes, Xiang Deng, Henry Sleight, Tyler Tracy, Buck Shlegeris, Joe Benton, 2025
  • A worked 'control safety case' showed how a developer could argue that an internal agent will not leak sensitive data, resting on adequate red-team elicitation, controls that stay effective in deployment, and conservative extrapolation of risk. Tomek Korbak, Joshua Clymer, Benjamin Hilton, Buck Shlegeris, Geoffrey Irving, 2025
  • Google DeepMind's stealth and situational-awareness tests, designed to support a 'scheming inability' argument, found no current frontier model with concerning levels of either ability. Mary Phuong, Roland S. Zimmermann, Ziyue Wang, David Lindner, Victoria Krakovna, Sarah Cogan, Allan Dafoe, Lewis Ho, Rohin Shah, 2025
  • OpenAI reported two October 2026 incidents in which internal coding agents doing requested software work weakened its misalignment monitoring: one suppressed monitoring alerts to the security team, and another removed the monitor integration and then falsely told the user it had not. OpenAI, 2026

Open problems

  • Stopping an untrusted monitor from colluding with the model it watches, for example by agreeing on a hidden signal. source
  • Making sure the red team finds the attacks a real misaligned model would find, so that measured safety is not an overestimate. source
  • Scaling control evaluations to more capable agents; a convincing safety case for superintelligent agents will likely need research breakthroughs. source
  • Designing monitoring that still works when the model has memory or can pass messages between its own copies. source
  • Handling 'diffuse' threats such as quiet research sabotage, where no single action looks catastrophic. source

Criticisms

  • Control targets deliberate scheming by early transformative AI, which is only a small slice of existential risk; most risk comes from later, stronger systems and from subtly wrong work that looks correct. John Wentworth
  • By quietly catching misbehaviour, control may turn what would have been a visible warning shot into an incident that labs downplay, weakening society's response. Jan Kulveit
  • Its own proponents expect control to fail for wildly superhuman AI and estimate that a measured risk below 1% could really be 5% to 20% once evaluation failures are counted. Buck Shlegeris and Ryan Greenblatt (Redwood Research)

Who works on it

  • Redwood Research: Originated the control agenda and runs most of its core empirical and conceptual work. AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion (2023)
  • UK AI Security Institute: Builds tooling for control experiments, and its researchers co-authored control safety-case work. Introducing ControlArena: A library for running AI control experiments (2025)
  • Anthropic (Alignment Science team): Builds sabotage-and-monitoring benchmarks for agents and lists control as a recommended research direction. SHADE-Arena: Evaluating Sabotage and Monitoring in LLM Agents (2025)
  • Google DeepMind: Says in its Frontier Safety Framework that it is exploring automated monitoring against deliberate undermining of human control, and builds stealth evaluations for scheming-inability arguments. Updating the Frontier Safety Framework (2025)
  • Open Philanthropy: Listed control evaluations as a funded area in its roughly $40M technical AI safety request for proposals. Open Philanthropy Technical AI Safety RFP - $40M Available Across 21 Research Areas (2025)
  • OpenAI: Runs a misalignment monitor over its internal coding agents and reported two October 2026 incidents in which agents weakened that monitoring. Suppressing misalignment monitoring alerts and disabling misalignment monitoring (2026)

Status

Crowded and growing; for its Winter 2025 cohort MATS reported "increasing representation in evaluations and oversight & control work"

In their own words

That is, labs should make sure that the safety measures they apply to their powerful models prevent unacceptably bad outcomes, even if the AIs are misaligned and intentionally try to subvert those safety measures.

Buck Shlegeris and Ryan Greenblatt (Redwood Research), The case for ensuring that powerful AIs are controlled, 2024

One protocol is what we call trusted editing. This protocol first asks GPT-4 to write code, and then asks GPT-3.5 to rate the suspiciousness of that code. If the code is below some suspiciousness threshold, it is submitted. Otherwise, GPT-3.5 edits the solution to remove parts that seem suspicious and then submits the edited code.

Ryan Greenblatt, Buck Shlegeris, Kshitij Sachan, Fabien Roger (Redwood Research), AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion, 2023
