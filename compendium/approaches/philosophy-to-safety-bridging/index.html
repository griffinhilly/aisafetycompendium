Field-building

Conceptual-alignment literature bridging

Have someone read academic philosophy full-time and translate it for safety researchers.

Open in the compendium

Philosophy-to-safety bridging means having trained philosophers read the academic literature on concepts that AI safety relies on, such as corrigibility, impact, honesty and rational agency, and translate what they find for safety researchers. It matters because many safety arguments rest on philosophical claims about goals, rationality and value that philosophers have studied for decades. Bridging work can test those claims, find better formulations, and give people without deep technical skills a way to contribute.

How it works

The idea was set out by David Krueger in December 2018. He asked for at least one person to work full-time on reviewing philosophy for safety-relevant insights, and proposed three stages. First, list the concepts that matter for safety, for example corrigibility (an AI's willingness to be corrected or shut down), reduced impact (avoiding side effects) and interpretability, and explain how they relate. Second, review the relevant literature, mostly analytic philosophy, on each one and summarise the state of research. Third, carry the insights into technical safety and machine learning papers. Wei Dai published a matching list in August 2019 of problems philosophers could work on, from decision theory to the hidden metaethical assumptions (assumptions about what morality is) inside alignment proposals.

In practice the work has mostly taken the form of philosophers writing papers that take a well-known safety argument and test it with the tools of their field. For example, the philosopher J. Dmitri Gallow took the 'instrumental convergence' thesis (the claim that agents with almost any final goal will seek the same means, such as resources and self-preservation) and modelled it in decision theory. He found that rationality alone pushes agents toward only some of those means. Elliott Thornley took the 'shutdown problem' (how to build an agent that neither prevents nor causes the pressing of its off-switch) and proved theorems showing that agents meeting some seemingly harmless conditions will often try to prevent or cause the pressing of the button. These papers appear both on safety forums and in philosophy journals, so they serve both audiences.

Where research stands

Krueger's wider goal was partly met by programs that bring philosophers into safety. One was the Center for AI Safety (CAIS) Philosophy Fellowship. In 2023 it hosted a dozen academic philosophers for a seven-month research fellowship. CAIS reports they produced 21 research papers on topics such as power-seeking AI and the moral status of AI agents, and a special issue of the journal Philosophical Studies on AI safety received over 30 submissions. Fellows posted drafts on the AI Alignment Forum in June 2023 for feedback from safety researchers. DeepMind (now Google DeepMind) has also published bridging work, notably Iason Gabriel's 2020 paper on what alignment should aim at.

Smaller efforts continue. A reading group on AI safety and philosophy recruited on the safety forums in July 2026. Wei Dai argued in 2025 that the total effort on philosophical problems in safety is far from adequate. The approach is niche. Its weakest point is uptake. It is hard to show that a philosophy paper changed what a lab actually builds.

Key results

  • Proved three theorems showing that agents meeting seemingly innocuous conditions will often try to prevent or cause the pressing of a shutdown button, and that more patient agents will pay more to do so. Elliott Thornley, 2024
  • A decision-theory model found that rationality alone biases an agent with random goals toward only three things: leaving less to chance, preserving its desires, and keeping options open; the author concludes this at best weakly supports the case that superintelligence leads to catastrophe. J. Dmitri Gallow, 2023
  • Argued that no existing theorem, including the von Neumann-Morgenstern theorem, shows that an agent which does not maximise expected utility must pursue dominated strategies, weakening a common argument that advanced AI will be a goal-directed expected-utility maximiser. Dan H, Elliott Thornley, 2023
  • Argued that alignment needs a clearer target (instructions, intentions, preferences, interests or values) and that, instead of looking for the true moral theory, designers should seek principles that people can reflectively endorse despite disagreement. Iason Gabriel, 2020
  • Used conceptual engineering to argue that 'AI safety' is best defined as any research aimed at preventing or reducing harms from AI systems, joining work on bias and privacy with work on catastrophic risk. Jacqueline Harding, Cameron Domenico Kirk-Giannini, 2025

Open problems

  • Carrying out Krueger's plan in full: a list of safety-relevant concepts and a review of the philosophical literature on each. source
  • Many of the philosophical problems Wei Dai listed in 2019, such as metaphilosophy (what counts as correct philosophical reasoning and how to build it into an AI) and metaethical policing, remain open. source
  • There is no settled way to get philosophical findings into lab practice, so it is unclear whether bridging papers change what gets built.

Criticisms

  • Conceptual analysis as a method has failed on its own terms: philosophers refuted many proposed analyses but never found a single correct analysis of a concept such as knowledge. If so, expecting it to deliver precise definitions for safety concepts may be unrealistic. In 2020 Krueger linked a blog post making this argument under his own proposal. Michael Huemer
  • The deep philosophical problems in alignment are important but very intractable, and work on them can wait: if alignment succeeds, the first aligned AI will likely be a corrigible assistant without philosophical views of its own to act on, which can then help people solve these problems. Elliott Thornley (in reply to Wei Dai, November 2025)

Who works on it

  • Center for AI Safety: Ran the 2023 Philosophy Fellowship, which brought a dozen academic philosophers into conceptual AI safety research and seeded a Philosophical Studies special issue. CAIS Philosophy Fellowship 2023 (2023)
  • Google DeepMind: Published (as DeepMind) philosophical analysis of what alignment should aim at, arguing that its technical and normative sides are closely connected. Artificial Intelligence, Values and Alignment (2020)

Start reading

  • Conceptual Analysis for AI Alignment, David Scott Krueger (2018). The original proposal for a full-time philosophy-to-safety reviewer, with a three-stage plan.
  • Problems in AI Alignment that philosophers could potentially contribute to, Wei Dai (2019). A concrete list of open philosophical problems that safety work depends on.
  • CAIS Philosophy Fellowship Midpoint Deliverables, CAIS Philosophy Fellowship (posted by Dan H) (2023). Nine papers, mostly drafts, that show what philosopher-written safety research looks like in practice.
  • Artificial Intelligence, Values and Alignment, Iason Gabriel (2020). A bridge between moral philosophy and the question of what to align AI to.
  • The Failure of Analysis and the Nature of Concepts, Michael Huemer (2024). A clear case against expecting conceptual analysis to deliver definitions.

In their own words

Conceptual Analysis is highly relevant for AI alignment, and is also a way in which someone with less technical skills can contribute to alignment research. This suggests there should be at least one person working full-time on reviewing existing philosophy literature for relevant insights, and summarizing and synthesizing these results for the safety community.

David Scott Krueger, Conceptual Analysis for AI Alignment, 2018

A deep dive into the relevant literature (I imagine mostly in analytic philosophy) on each of these concepts (or sets of concepts). These should summarize the state of research on these problems in the relevant fields, and potentially inspire safety researchers, or at least help them frame their work for these audiences and find potential collaborators within these fields.

David Scott Krueger, Conceptual Analysis for AI Alignment, 2018
