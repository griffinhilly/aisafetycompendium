Shaping the model: data and training

Aligned models

Debate (two AI systems argue opposing sides for a judge)

Surface truth under complexity by making systems attack each other's answers.

Open in the compendium

Debate is a training and oversight method in which two AI systems argue for opposing answers and a judge, human or AI, decides which side was more truthful and useful. The bet is that it is harder to lie than to refute a lie, so a weaker judge can still reach the right answer. It is one of the main proposals for supervising AI that knows more than its overseers.

How it works

Picture a long story that the judge has not read. Two models have read it. Each argues for a different answer to a question about the story and quotes passages to support its case. The judge sees only the arguments and the quotes, then picks a winner. The models are trained to win, so the hope is that the winning strategy is to tell the truth, because a liar's claims can be attacked by an honest opponent.

The idea comes from a 2018 OpenAI paper by Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano and Dario Amodei. They argued that, in theory, debate with a fast judge can settle a far wider class of questions than a judge working alone. Later work designs protocols with better guarantees. One line, "prover-estimator debate" (June 2025), changes the rules so that an honest debater does not need much more computing effort than a dishonest one.

Where research stands

Debate has moved from theory to experiments with current models and humans. In 2024, a study that won a Best Paper award at the ICML conference found that debate between two strong models raised non-expert accuracy on hard reading questions: model judges went from 48% to 76%, human judges from 60% to 88%. A New York University study with human debaters found debate beat having a single expert argue one side, 84% to 74%. A larger Google DeepMind study in 2024 confirmed that debate beats a single arguer, but found the advantage over plain question answering was mixed when the judge was not missing key information.

The UK AI Security Institute's alignment team built its 2025 research agenda around debate and sketched how debate training could support a safety case, a structured argument that a system is safe enough to use. The authors list open problems that must be solved first, including better protocols, empirical tests and "exploration hacking" (a model deliberately avoiding the strategies training would reward). The best-known weakness is the obfuscated-arguments problem, described below.

Key results

  • In theory, debate with optimal play lets a polynomial-time judge answer a wider class of questions (PSPACE) than direct judging (NP); on an image task, debate raised a sparse classifier's accuracy from 59.4% to 88.9% with six pixels shown. Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, Dario Amodei, 2018
  • Debate between strong models helped non-expert judges: model judges reached 76% accuracy versus a 48% baseline, and human judges 88% versus 60%. Akbir Khan, John Hughes, Dan Valentine, Laura Ruis, Kshitij Sachan, Ansh Radhakrishnan, Edward Grefenstette, Samuel R. Bowman, Tim Rocktäschel, Ethan Perez, 2024
  • With human debaters, a non-expert judge found the right answer 84% of the time with debate versus 74% with a single expert arguing one side. Julian Michael, Salsabila Mahdi, David Rein, Jackson Petty, Julien Dirani, Vishakh Padmakumar, Samuel R. Bowman, 2023
  • Across many task types, debate beat a single arguer every time; it beat direct question answering on tasks where the judge lacked key information, with mixed results on other tasks. Zachary Kenton, Noah Y. Siegel et al. (Google DeepMind), 2024
  • A new recursive protocol lets an honest debater win without needing far more computation than a dishonest one, under stated stability assumptions. Jonah Brown-Cohen, Geoffrey Irving, Georgios Piliouras, Lijie Chen, Jiawei Li, Zhiyang Xun, 2025

Open problems

  • A dishonest debater can build an argument whose flaw is real but very hard to find, which the honest side cannot efficiently refute. source
  • Training may not reach the honest equilibrium if a model deliberately avoids strategies that training would reward (exploration hacking). source
  • Human judges may be systematically wrong on some questions, and protocols must stop debaters from exploiting those errors. source
  • The debate safety case covers only low-stakes settings, where a few wrong answers cannot by themselves cause severe harm; extending it to high-stakes settings is still an open question. source

Criticisms

  • Human experiments found that dishonest debaters can often "create arguments that have a fatal error, but where it is very hard to locate the error", and the authors had no fix. Beth Barnes and Paul Christiano
  • Debate only works if judges can reliably tell truth from skilled persuasion; it may need judges above an ability threshold that ordinary people may not meet, and it could amplify their biases. Geoffrey Irving and Amanda Askell
  • Stronger debaters raised judge accuracy more modestly than in earlier studies, and debate did not consistently beat simply asking the judge model directly. Zachary Kenton, Noah Y. Siegel et al. (Google DeepMind)

Who works on it

  • UK AI Security Institute (alignment team): Centres its alignment agenda on debate and sketched a debate-based safety case. An alignment safety case sketch based on debate (2025)
  • Google DeepMind: Ran large debate experiments with weak model judges and developed more efficient debate protocols. On scalable oversight with weak LLMs judging strong LLMs (2024)
  • Anthropic: Staff co-authored the debate study that showed more persuasive debaters lead judges to truer answers. Debating with More Persuasive LLMs Leads to More Truthful Answers (2024)
  • University College London: Researchers led the 2024 debate study with model and human judges. Debating with More Persuasive LLMs Leads to More Truthful Answers (2024)
  • New York University: Ran human debate experiments comparing debate with single-expert consultancy. Debate Helps Supervise Unreliable Experts (2023)
  • OpenAI: Published the original debate proposal in 2018 and the follow-up call for social-science experiments in 2019. AI safety via debate (2018)

In their own words

To help address this concern, we propose training agents via self play on a zero sum debate game. Given a question or proposed action, two agents take turns making short statements up to a limit, then a human judges which of the agents gave the most true, useful information.

Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, Dario Amodei (OpenAI), AI safety via debate, 2018

The utility of debate as an approach for AI alignment rests on the following central claim: Claim. In the debate game, it is harder to lie than to refute a lie.

Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, Dario Amodei (OpenAI), AI safety via debate, 2018
