International regulatory and inspection agency (IAEA-style)

An international body that sets AI standards and checks that states and developers follow them.

Open in the compendium

This approach would create an international agency that sets safety standards for the most powerful AI systems and checks that states and developers follow them. It is often called an 'IAEA for AI', after the International Atomic Energy Agency, which inspects nuclear facilities. Supporters argue that risks crossing borders need an international referee; critics argue that AI is much harder to inspect than nuclear material and that such bodies cannot enforce rules on great powers.

How it works

Proposals differ in how much power they give the agency. At the light end, the 'Advanced AI Governance Organization' sketched by Ho and colleagues (July 2023) would set international norms and standards, help states apply them, and perhaps monitor compliance. It draws on bodies such as the International Civil Aviation Organization and the Financial Action Task Force, which set standards that states then enforce at home. Haydn Belfield (July 2025) proposes an agency that harmonises and certifies national AI regulators, with states lacking a certified regime barred from importing advanced chips.

At the heavy end, the agency would inspect directly. In May 2023, OpenAI's leaders wrote that efforts above a capability or compute threshold should be subject to an international authority that can 'inspect systems, require audits, test for compliance with safety standards'. PauseAI's proposed International AI Safety Agency would approve major training runs and verify compliance through chip tracking, energy monitoring and inspections. MIRI's November 2025 draft treaty gives a Coalition Technical Body the power to monitor training runs and verify chip use, alongside challenge inspections approved by an Executive Council.

Where research stands

No such agency exists as of October 2026. In June 2023, UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he would be 'favorable' to an AI agency, but that only member states can create one. The UN High-level Advisory Body on AI, in its September 2024 final report, chose not to recommend an agency yet. It said one might become necessary 'if the risks of AI become more serious, and more concentrated'. The UN instead created a scientific panel and a global dialogue in 2025, which assess and discuss but do not regulate.

The research debate is about whether the nuclear model fits. Nuclear material can be counted and measured, but AI models are digital and can be copied, leaked or released openly. Frontier training does still need large, visible clusters of chips and power, which is the opening that verification research is trying to use. Analysts also stress that the IAEA itself cannot punish violators: it can only make recommendations to the UN Security Council and member states, so enforcement depends on great powers, as cases involving Iran and Syria show.

Key results

  • An Advanced AI Governance Organization could set norms and standards, help states implement them and monitor compliance, but standard-setting is slow and states may lack reasons to join. Lewis Ho, Joslyn Barnhart, Robert Trager, Yoshua Bengio, Miles Brundage and others, 2023
  • OpenAI's leaders proposed that superintelligence efforts above a capability or compute threshold be subject to an IAEA-like international authority, with tracking of compute and energy use as a practical lever. Sam Altman, Greg Brockman, Ilya Sutskever (OpenAI), 2023
  • The UN High-level Advisory Body did not recommend an international AI agency in 2024, but said one with monitoring, verification and enforcement powers might become necessary if risks grow and concentrate. UN High-level Advisory Body on Artificial Intelligence, 2024
  • An International AI Agency could harmonise, monitor and certify national frontier AI regulators, with chip imports tied to certification under a Secure Chips Agreement. Haydn Belfield, 2025
  • Case studies of the IAEA in Iran and the chemical weapons watchdog in Syria and Russia show that international bodies can verify, but enforcement depends on great-power politics. Akash Wasil (Lawfare), 2024

Open problems

  • What an inspector would measure, since AI has no equivalent of the declared nuclear material that safeguards start from. source
  • How to act when a state refuses to comply, given that the agency itself could not punish violators. source
  • How much warning an agency would get, since AI 'breakout' times could conceivably be very short. source
  • How to scope rules when AI is already widely deployed and the frontier moves quickly. source

Criticisms

  • It is not clear AI can be safeguarded like nuclear materials, because its inputs are digital, much of the work is done by private companies, and the paths to catastrophe are less clear. Ian J. Stewart (James Martin Center for Nonproliferation Studies), June 2023
  • The limits of the nuclear analogy are clear: nuclear weapons are expensive to build and hard to hide, while weaponising AI 'promises to be neither'. UN High-level Advisory Body on AI (2024)
  • Iran shows an IAEA-style body may be necessary but not sufficient: it would not tell anyone what to do when a party does not want to play by the rules. Miles Brundage, June 2025

Who works on it

  • OpenAI: Its leaders publicly proposed an IAEA-like authority for superintelligence efforts in May 2023. Governance of superintelligence (2023)
  • UN High-level Advisory Body on Artificial Intelligence: Weighed an international AI agency and chose not to recommend one yet. Governing AI for Humanity: Final Report (2024)
  • PauseAI: Campaigns for an International AI Safety Agency, modelled on the IAEA, to approve training runs and verify compliance. PauseAI Proposal
  • Machine Intelligence Research Institute (technical governance team): Its draft treaty creates a Coalition Technical Body with monitoring powers, backed by challenge inspections. An International Agreement to Prevent the Premature Creation of Artificial Superintelligence (2025)
  • ControlAI: Its plan A Narrow Path calls for an international AI oversight system that does not collapse over time. A Narrow Path (2024)
  • James Martin Center for Nonproliferation Studies: Its executive director in Washington, DC assessed the fit of the IAEA model for AI. Why the IAEA model may not be best for regulating artificial intelligence (2023)

Status

Proposal stage. The Ho et al. paper cites ICAO and the IAEA as models. It notes that some observers ask whether inspections of large data centres, partly like IAEA safeguards, might be needed. A Narrow Path calls for international institutions to keep controls from collapsing.

In their own words

An intergovernmental or multi-stakeholder Advanced AI Governance Organization ... could help internationalize and align efforts to address global risks from advanced AI systems by setting governance norms and standards, and assisting in their implementation. It may also perform compliance monitoring functions (either independently or in association with industry groups) for an international governance regime.

Lewis Ho, Joslyn Barnhart, Robert Trager, Yoshua Bengio, Miles Brundage, Allison Carnegie, Rumman Chowdhury, Allan Dafoe, Gillian Hadfield, Margaret Levi, Duncan Snidal, International Institutions for Advanced AI, 2023

Where states have incentives to undercut each other's regulatory commitments, international institutions may be needed to support and incentivize best practices. ... In the case of advanced AI, some observers have asked whether more intrusive forms of international oversight might be necessary, including detection and inspections of large data centers (partly analogous to IAEA safeguards).

Lewis Ho, Joslyn Barnhart, Robert Trager, Yoshua Bengio, Miles Brundage, Allison Carnegie, Rumman Chowdhury, Allan Dafoe, Gillian Hadfield, Margaret Levi, Duncan Snidal, International Institutions for Advanced AI, 2023
