Aligned models

Impact measures: relative reachability, attainable utility preservation

Penalise the AI for making large or irreversible changes to the world state.

Open in the compendium

Impact measures give an AI a penalty for changing the world more than its task needs. The aim is to avoid side effects: harms nobody thought to forbid, because no one can list everything an AI should leave alone. A good impact measure would make "don't break things" the default, even when the goal is written badly.

How it works

Picture a cleaning robot told to carry a box across a room. The shortest path runs through a vase. The goal says nothing about vases, so a plain reward-maximiser smashes it. An impact measure adds a cost for the difference between the world the robot leaves and some baseline world, typically the one where the robot did nothing.

The hard part is defining "difference". Counting changed objects punishes harmless changes and misses subtle harms. Two main proposals emerged in 2018 and 2019. Relative reachability, from Victoria Krakovna and colleagues at DeepMind, penalises the agent for making states of the world harder to reach than they would have been by default. A smashed vase can never be reached again, so smashing costs a lot. Attainable utility preservation (AUP), from Alexander Turner, Dylan Hadfield-Menell and Prasad Tadepalli, penalises changes in the agent's ability to achieve other goals. Surprisingly, those other goals can be randomly generated and still produce careful behaviour.

Both need a baseline. Choosing it is subtle. If the baseline is the world as it was at the start, the agent may be pushed to stop events that would have happened anyway, such as other agents' actions. If the baseline is "do nothing from the start", the agent may be pushed to undo the intended effects of its own task, which researchers call offsetting. A stepwise baseline, which compares each step with doing nothing from that step on, avoids both traps in DeepMind's tests.

Where research stands

The methods work in small test worlds. In gridworlds, relative reachability with a stepwise baseline avoided the bad incentives that simpler penalties created. In 2020 AUP was scaled to SafeLife, a set of large, randomly generated environments based on Conway's Game of Life. Built by the Partnership on AI, SafeLife scores agents on both task reward and side effects. AUP completed tasks while avoiding many side effects at modest cost. Also in 2020, DeepMind researchers proposed rewarding an agent for keeping possible future tasks achievable.

Most of this work appeared between 2016 and 2020, and the published tests of relative reachability and AUP cited here all use gridworlds or SafeLife. The problem itself has returned in practical form. A NeurIPS 2026 benchmark of tool-using language-model agents found collateral damage, such as writing to the wrong object or contacting an unintended recipient, in 15.2% of runs across 11 frontier models. The deep open question is whether any impact measure survives a capable agent. Stuart Armstrong showed in February 2020 that an agent can largely neutralise AUP by building a helper agent, while relative reachability holds up better.

Key results

  • Avoiding side effects was named as one of five concrete open problems in AI safety, arising from a wrong objective function. Dario Amodei, Chris Olah, Jacob Steinhardt, Paul Christiano, John Schulman, Dan Mané, 2016
  • A relative-reachability penalty with a stepwise inaction baseline avoided the bad incentives that simpler side-effect penalties created in gridworld tests. Victoria Krakovna, Laurent Orseau, Ramana Kumar, Miljan Martic, Shane Legg, 2018
  • Preserving the ability to pursue auxiliary goals, even randomly generated ones, produced conservative and effective behaviour. Alexander Matt Turner, Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Prasad Tadepalli, 2019
  • AUP scaled to large Game of Life-based environments, letting agents finish tasks while avoiding many side effects at modest overhead. Alexander Matt Turner, Neale Ratzlaff, Prasad Tadepalli, 2020
  • Rewarding an agent for keeping possible future tasks achievable, filtered by a do-nothing baseline, beat a penalty on irreversible actions in gridworlds. Victoria Krakovna, Laurent Orseau, Richard Ngo, Miljan Martic, Shane Legg, 2020
  • Across 2,211 model-task runs (201 tasks, 11 frontier language models) on email, calendar and ticket tasks, 15.2% caused collateral damage such as writing to the wrong object. Jiaju Huang, Shaobin Chen, Xinglong Liang, Xinyu Ma, Hao Yang, Yue Sun, Wei Ke, Tao Tan, 2026

Open problems

  • Stop a capable agent from escaping the penalty by building helper agents that act for it. source
  • Choose a baseline that does not reward undoing the intended effects of the task or blocking events that would happen anyway. source
  • Carry impact penalties from gridworlds to language-model agents acting in real software and the real world. source
  • Penalise harmful impact without blocking the large, intended impact that useful tasks require. source

Criticisms

  • An agent can build a subagent and then stay put while the subagent acts, which largely neutralises attainable utility penalties. Stuart Armstrong
  • Low-impact agents can still behave badly by pushing side effects toward zero, so impact measures need to be paired with mild optimisation. Eliezer Yudkowsky et al. (LessWrong wiki, formerly Arbital)

Who works on it

  • Google DeepMind (safety research): Developed relative reachability and the future-tasks approach to side effects. Penalizing side effects using stepwise relative reachability (2018)
  • Partnership on AI: Built and released SafeLife, an open benchmark that scores agents on side effects. SafeLife 1.0: Exploring Side Effects in Complex Environments (2019)

Status

Mature; tested in gridworlds and larger Game of Life-based environments, not on human life outcomes.

In their own words

There are various ways to make the notion of a "side effect" more precise - I think of it as a disruption of the agent's environment that is unnecessary for achieving its objective. ... For each state, we penalize the agent for making it less reachable than it would be from the default state.

Victoria Krakovna (DeepMind), Measuring and avoiding side effects using relative reachability, 2018

For example, a robotic factory assistant could break expensive equipment due to a reward misspecification; even if the designers immediately correct the reward function, the damage is done. To mitigate this risk, we introduce an approach that balances optimization of the primary reward function with preservation of the ability to optimize auxiliary reward functions.

Alexander Matt Turner, Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Prasad Tadepalli, Conservative Agency via Attainable Utility Preservation, 2019
