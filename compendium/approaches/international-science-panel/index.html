International scientific assessment panel (IPCC-style)

An independent expert panel that builds shared scientific evidence on AI risk for governments.

Open in the compendium

An international scientific panel gathers independent experts to agree on what the evidence says about AI's capabilities and risks, and reports to governments. It is modelled loosely on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC). It matters for safety because countries cannot coordinate on risks they do not agree exist, and a shared evidence base makes that agreement easier.

How it works

Two bodies now do this work. The International AI Safety Report was commissioned by the countries at the November 2023 AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park. Over 100 experts write it, led by Yoshua Bengio, with an advisory panel nominated by more than 30 countries and international organisations. It is published before each international AI summit: the first edition in January 2025, interim Key Updates in October and November 2025, and the second edition in February 2026.

The UN Independent International Scientific Panel on AI was created by the UN General Assembly in August 2025. Its 40 members come from every region and were appointed in February 2026; they elected Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa as co-chairs. The Panel writes an annual report for the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance. The first Dialogue took place in Geneva in July 2026 and the second is planned for New York in May 2027. Both bodies assess the evidence but do not recommend policy. The UN Panel, for example, states that it will not set rules, enforce standards or prescribe policy.

Where research stands

This approach has moved from proposal to practice. Ho and colleagues proposed a 'Commission on Frontier AI' in July 2023, citing the IPCC as a model. By 2026 two panels were publishing. The 2026 International AI Safety Report found that capabilities keep improving unevenly, that models increasingly tell test settings from real use, which makes safety testing harder, and that most risk management remains voluntary. The UN Panel's Preliminary Report of 1 July 2026 warned that safeguards are not keeping pace with capabilities, and that clear evidence may arrive only after it is too late to act.

The main open questions concern authority and independence. The United States declined to endorse the 2026 International AI Safety Report, though US officials commented on drafts. Researchers at Umeå University's AI Policy Lab argued in July 2026 that the UN Panel had not shown how it stays independent of governments and industry. Ho and colleagues had warned in 2023 that thin research and fast technical change make scientific assessment hard, and that the work should not become politicised.

Key results

  • A 'Commission on Frontier AI', modelled on the IPCC and IPBES, could help experts reach consensus on AI's opportunities and risks, but faces fast technical change and the risk of politicisation. Lewis Ho, Joslyn Barnhart, Robert Trager, Yoshua Bengio, Miles Brundage and others, 2023
  • The first International AI Safety Report, by 100 experts and backed by 30 countries, was published on 29 January 2025 as a shared evidence base before the Paris AI Action Summit. UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, 2025
  • The 2026 report found that models increasingly distinguish test settings from real deployment, so dangerous capabilities could go undetected before release, and that most risk management remains voluntary. Yoshua Bengio (lead) and over 100 experts, 2026
  • The UN General Assembly created the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI by consensus on 26 August 2025, with a mandate for annual evidence-based assessments in the non-military domain. United Nations, 2026
  • The UN Panel's Preliminary Report of 1 July 2026 warned that safeguards are not keeping pace with AI capabilities and that policymakers face an evidence gap. United Nations, 2026

Open problems

  • How a panel stays independent of the governments that appoint it and the companies that produce much of its evidence. source
  • How a slow, consensus-based assessment keeps up with a field that changes within months. source
  • How to assess proprietary systems whose capabilities companies do not fully disclose. source
  • How to keep the major AI powers engaged, after the United States declined to endorse the 2026 International AI Safety Report. source

Criticisms

  • The UN Panel's preliminary report does not explain how its independence from member states and industry is secured, smooths over disagreement, and leans on developer-produced evidence. Jason Tucker, Virginia Dignum and colleagues (AI Policy Lab, Umeå University), July 2026
  • An IPCC for AI would repeat what the author sees as climate assessment's flaws: pessimistic framing, pressure toward consensus and government influence over authors, and could pave the way for pre-market approval of AI models. James Broughel (Competitive Enterprise Institute), November 2023
  • Limited existing research and rapid technical change make scientific assessment of advanced AI hard, and a membership chosen for political representation could make meaningful consensus harder to reach. Lewis Ho and colleagues (2023)

Status

In place. The UN General Assembly created the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI by resolution A/RES/79/325, adopted by consensus on 26 August 2025. The Assembly appointed its 40 members on 12 February 2026, and the Panel released its Preliminary Report on 1 July 2026. The UN says the Panel will not set rules or prescribe policy. Separately, the International AI Safety Report 2026 was published on 3 February 2026 by over 100 experts, with an advisory panel nominated by over 30 countries and international organisations.
