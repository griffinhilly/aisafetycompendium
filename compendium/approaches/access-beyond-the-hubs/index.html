Building the field

Field-building

Reducing geographic constraints on AI-safety talent

Widen the talent pool by expanding where AI-safety work gets done. People who cannot move to the Bay Area, London or Washington DC can then contribute through remote and part-time programmes, paid entry roles and local hubs.

Open in the compendium

Reducing geographic constraints on AI-safety talent means letting people who could contribute do so without moving to the Bay Area, London or Washington DC. It works through remote and part-time programmes, local hubs in other cities, and hiring that does not require relocation. Advocates argue that relocation-heavy entry filters out mid-career talent with settled lives, shrinks the pool of people the field can draw on, and narrows the range of views in a field that shapes how AI systems treat everyone.

How it works

Most AI-safety jobs sit in a few cities. Anthropic expects all staff to be in one of its offices at least 25% of the time, and its careers page says most staff are in the Bay Area. OpenAI uses a hybrid model of three office days a week, and its alignment research roles, such as one on chain-of-thought monitorability, are based in San Francisco. In Berkeley, METR and Redwood Research list their research roles as on-site, and Apollo Research says it defaults to in-person work, with most staff in London or San Francisco. Full-time fellowships are often in person too: Pivotal, for example, is an in-person programme in London.

Remote and part-time routes are the main way around this. SPAR is a part-time, remote research fellowship. AI Safety Camp is a three-month online research programme. BlueDot Impact runs free online courses of 25 to 40 hours, and Apart Research runs weekend research hackathons online and at local sites. Some selective programmes allow remote work by exception. MATS runs in person in Berkeley and London, but decides on remote participation case by case, and many of its mentors are open to it, especially for people with family or other obligations. The Anthropic Fellows Program, which pays a weekly stipend, is open to remote fellows in the US, UK or Canada. The IAPS AI Policy Fellowship has a paid remote track, but fellows on its DC or London tracks get an extra $3,000 stipend. FAR.AI differs from the employers above: its senior research engineer role can be remote or in person in Berkeley or Singapore, and it says it can hire remotely in most countries.

Local hubs bring the work to people instead. Trajectory Labs aims to increase Toronto's contribution to reducing catastrophic risks from advanced AI, and BAISH in Buenos Aires runs in-person courses and a research lab for a community of over 400 people in Argentina. In July 2026 Seth Lifland proposed more such hubs, including spaces that house remote workers whose organisations are based elsewhere. The UK AI Security Institute lets some staff work from government offices in six UK cities outside London. For mid-career entrants, Successif offers free advising on career transitions.

Where research stands

The approach is small beside the hub model. In October 2026, 80,000 Hours' newsletter reported that more than half of the 623 AI-safety jobs on its job board were based in the Bay Area, though some could be done remotely. Many people in adjacent communities live outside the hubs: in the 2024 EA Survey, London (6.7%) and the Bay Area (5.7%) were the most common cities, and a majority of respondents lived outside the major hubs. Remote work already happens at some stages. In the spring 2024 MATS extension, 45% of the 36 scholars worked remotely. No representative survey reports the age, family situation or home location of people employed in AI safety. Adjacent groups skew young: respondents to the 2023 LessWrong survey had a median age of 29, and 85% had no children.

Evidence on the field's values comes only from surveys of nearby communities. Their respondents are forum readers and self-selected volunteers, not samples of AI-safety staff. Of the 558 people who answered the 2023 LessWrong survey, about half lived in the US; about 91% described themselves as atheist or agnostic, 2% as conservative, and 4.6% said they had signed up for cryonics, the freezing of bodies after death in hope of later revival. Among self-selected respondents to a 2024 AE Studio survey of alignment researchers, about 55% identified as progressive and 4 people as conservative. By contrast, Pew's 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study found that 62% of US adults identify as Christian and 29% as unaffiliated. On enhancement the US public is mixed: in 2016 Pew found that 66% of adults would not want a brain chip to improve their ability to concentrate and process information, but adults split 48% to 50% on whether they would want gene editing to cut a baby's risk of disease. In Pew's 2024 surveys, AI experts (authors and presenters at AI conferences, not AI-safety staff) were far more likely than US adults to expect AI to have a positive impact on the US over the next 20 years (56% vs. 17%). Writers such as Celia Ford (Transformer, December 2025) argue that concentration in a few cities creates an intellectual bubble prone to groupthink. No study yet tests whether location causes these differences or whether wider access would change them.

Open problems

  • Measuring who the field filters out: no representative survey reports the home location, age, family situation or values of people employed in AI safety.
  • Giving people outside the hubs the informal contacts that turn into jobs; an 80,000 Hours writer in New York says it is a lot easier to get an AI-safety job if people nearby already know a candidate before they read the application. source
  • Offering remote tracks on equal terms; the IAPS AI Policy Fellowship pays fellows in its DC or London hubs an extra $3,000. source
  • Building local hubs that can house remote workers and grow local jobs in cities without an AI-safety employer base. source
  • Supporting conceptual, early-stage research at a distance, where remote teams across science make fewer breakthroughs. source

Criticisms

  • Insiders see compelling reasons to avoid remote work on alignment research: the less settled the work, the more it needs high-bandwidth communication, and small alignment teams benefit from sharing an office. RobertM, replying to a question on jobs outside London and San Francisco (LessWrong, March 2023)
  • Across 20 million research articles and 4 million patent applications, remote teams were consistently less likely than on-site teams to make breakthrough discoveries, and remote members were less likely to share conceptual tasks such as conceiving new ideas. Yiling Lin, Carl Benedikt Frey and Lingfei Wu (Nature, November 2023)
  • Career advice still favours being in a hub: an 80,000 Hours advisor recommends a trip to a hub city, or moving if possible, and says several people who found AI-safety work called moving their most important decision. Matt Beard, 80,000 Hours advisor (guest post on the 80,000 Hours Substack, May 2026)

Status

Small beside the hub model: 80,000 Hours' newsletter reported in October 2026 that more than half of the 623 AI-safety jobs on its board were based in the Bay Area; remote routes are mostly part-time or entry programmes; no survey yet measures how many AI-safety workers have families or settled lives outside the hubs.

Start reading

  • On-ramps for mid-career, non-Anglophone entrants to AI safety, Aleksei Khvostov (2026). An account of how unpaid work, relocation-heavy opportunities and limited remote access filter out mid-career entrants, with proposals. The EA Forum tags the post "~Entirely AI-written".
  • There Should Be More AI Safety Hubs, Seth Lifland (2026). Where the talent sits, who cannot move, and what new local hubs could do.
  • The perils of AI safety's insularity, Celia Ford (Transformer) (2025). Journalism on how concentration in a few cities can narrow the field's ideas.
  • U.S. Public Wary of Biomedical Technologies to 'Enhance' Human Abilities, Cary Funk, Brian Kennedy and Elizabeth Podrebarac Sciupac (Pew Research Center) (2016). How the US public views gene editing, brain chips and synthetic blood, and how views vary with religious commitment.
  • How the U.S. Public and AI Experts View Artificial Intelligence, Colleen McClain, Brian Kennedy, Jeffrey Gottfried, Monica Anderson and Giancarlo Pasquini (Pew Research Center) (2025). Side-by-side surveys showing how far AI experts' hopes and worries about AI differ from the public's.

In their own words

But when funders and researchers live and work in the same handful of buildings scattered across a small handful of cities — two of which, Berkeley and San Francisco, are just 13 miles apart — it creates an intellectual bubble where groupthink may be mistaken for truth, and where a select few ideas can come to dominate the field's priorities.

Celia Ford (Transformer), The perils of AI safety's insularity, 2025

Right now, most AI safety talent is concentrated in just a few cities: San Francisco, Berkeley, London, Oxford, and Washington D.C. ... When you have an established life in a city, moving is not a trivial task, yet many AI safety jobs require (or at least heavily favor) relocation to an AI safety hub.

Seth Lifland, There Should Be More AI Safety Hubs, 2026
