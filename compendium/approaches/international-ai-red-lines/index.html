International AI red lines

Get countries to agree on specific AI behaviours and uses that are banned everywhere, such as AI that copies itself without human approval.

Open in the compendium

International AI red lines are specific limits on what AI systems may do, or what people may use them for, that countries agree to ban everywhere. Examples include AI that copies itself without human approval, and handing AI the decision to launch nuclear weapons. Supporters see red lines as the narrowest deal rival powers could reach, since no country gains from these disasters. Even supporters say the lines are hard to define precisely, verify and enforce, and in September 2025 the US government rejected global governance of AI.

How it works

A red line can target an AI behaviour (what a system can do) or an AI use (how people use it). In March 2024 scientists from China, the US and elsewhere met in Beijing as the International Dialogues on AI Safety and proposed five lines: no AI system should copy or improve itself without explicit human approval, seek undue power, substantially help design weapons of mass destruction, carry out serious cyberattacks on its own, or deceive its designers or regulators about its likelihood or ability to cross the other lines. Their roadmap called for domestic registration of large models and training runs, and for national rules aligned across countries. In September 2024 the same group added 'early-warning thresholds': capability levels showing that a model may be close to a line, which would trigger stricter testing.

The Global Call for AI Red Lines, launched at the UN General Assembly on 22 September 2025, asks governments for an agreement with 'robust enforcement mechanisms' by the end of 2026. It does not endorse specific lines. Its examples of uses to ban include giving AI nuclear launch authority, weapons that kill without meaningful human control, mass surveillance and social scoring, and AI that hides that it is not human. Its examples of behaviours to ban include autonomous self-replication and help with weapons of mass destruction. Enforcement would combine a treaty, national laws and, its organisers argued in September 2026, an international agency to log incidents, verify compliance and require a pause where safety cannot be shown.

Where research stands

As of the Call organisers' September 2026 letter there was no international agreement on AI red lines. Partial lines exist. The Call's organisers point to UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of AI, which calls for prohibiting AI social scoring and mass surveillance, and to the EU AI Act's ban on 'unacceptable risk' uses. In November 2024 Presidents Biden and Xi affirmed that humans, not AI, should control the decision to use nuclear weapons. At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, 16 companies (later 20) agreed to set thresholds at which severe risks, unless adequately mitigated, would be 'deemed intolerable'; those are voluntary company commitments, not law.

Support has grown but government action has not followed. By September 2026 the Call's website listed over 300 prominent figures as signatories, including 15 Nobel and Turing laureates, and over 90 organisations. Two working groups, one under UNESCO and one under the International Association for Safe and Ethical AI (IASEAI), are drafting definitions; the IASEAI group chose four priority domains: loss of control, chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear (CBRN) uplift, offensive cyber capabilities and harmful manipulation. The organisers wrote that governments had still not decided to negotiate. In September 2025 the US government rejected global governance of AI at the UN. The hardest open questions are how to define lines that can be measured, and how to verify that no one crosses them.

Key results

  • Leading scientists from China, the US and other countries agreed a non-exhaustive list of five red lines, on self-replication, power seeking, weapons help, cyberattacks and deception, plus a roadmap to enforce them. International Dialogues on AI Safety, 2024
  • Proposed that developers show authorities their systems will not cross red lines, using early-warning capability thresholds that trigger more rigorous assurance. International Dialogues on AI Safety, 2024
  • Sixteen AI companies, later twenty, committed to set thresholds at which severe risks from a model, unless adequately mitigated, 'would be deemed intolerable', and to say what they would do if a threshold were met. UK and Republic of Korea governments, 2024
  • The US and Chinese presidents agreed that humans, not AI, should control decisions to use nuclear weapons; NPR reported it was the first time China had made this statement. Asma Khalid (NPR), 2024
  • The Global Call, announced at the UN General Assembly with over 200 signatories including 10 Nobel Prize winners, asked governments to agree red lines by the end of 2026. Wikipedia, 2026
  • Set out principles and case studies (weapons of mass destruction, evaluation deception, misinformation) for defining intolerable-risk thresholds for industry and government. Deepika Raman, Nada Madkour, Evan R. Murphy, Krystal Jackson, Jessica Newman (UC Berkeley CLTC), 2025

Open problems

  • How to define lines precisely enough to measure; companies' own red lines are often 'weak and/or vague', which makes it easy to move the goalposts. source
  • How to verify that a trained model is not used secretly for a banned purpose, which CeSIA, a co-organiser of the Call, calls 'the most delicate point'. source
  • Who would enforce the lines, since there is no single global authority for AI. source
  • Which forum could reach agreement fastest: a UN Security Council or General Assembly resolution, the G7, or a coalition of willing states. source

Criticisms

  • The US rejects 'all efforts by international bodies to assert centralized control and global governance of AI', preferring national independence and sovereignty. Michael Kratsios, director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (UN Security Council, September 2025)
  • Any red line that requires slowing the improvement of models is likely to face intense political resistance. CeSIA, conceding the point in its defence of the Call (December 2025)
  • Red lines carry little weight without an institution able to verify and enforce them, and governments have not yet decided to build one. The Call's own organisers (September 2026)

Status

Campaign and expert statements; no international agreement. The Call asks governments to agree red lines by the end of 2026. On 22 September 2026 its organisers wrote that governments had not yet decided to negotiate, while working groups under UNESCO and IASEAI draft definitions. In November 2024 the US and Chinese presidents affirmed human control over the decision to use nuclear weapons.

In their own words

AI red lines are specific prohibitions on AI uses or behaviors that are deemed too dangerous to permit under any circumstances. They are limits, agreed upon internationally, to prevent AI from causing universally unacceptable risks.

Global Call for AI Red Lines (organised by CeSIA, The Future Society and CHAI), Global Call for AI Red Lines: FAQ, 2025

In this consensus statement, we propose red lines in AI development as an international coordination mechanism, including the following non-exhaustive list. ... No AI system should be able to copy or improve itself without explicit human approval and assistance.

International Dialogues on AI Safety (signatories include Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton, Andrew Yao, Ya-Qin Zhang, Stuart Russell), IDAIS-Beijing: Consensus Statement on Red Lines in Artificial Intelligence, 2024
