Theory: the maths of agents and goals

Group agency and collective intentionality

Formal philosophy of when many agents constitute one agent.

Open in the compendium

Group agency is the philosophy of when a collection of agents, such as a company, a court or a state, counts as one agent with its own beliefs and goals. It matters for AI safety in two ways. Many AI systems working together could become a group agent whose goals no single member has. And the long experience of governing corporations offers a template for how to hold non-human agents, including AI, responsible.

How it works

Philosophers use the term collective intentionality for shared aims, as when two people intend to go for a walk together. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy sets out the main accounts. Margaret Gilbert holds that people form a "plural subject" when they jointly commit to act as a body. John Searle treats collective intentions as primitive "we"-thoughts inside each person. Michael Bratman builds shared intention out of interlocking individual intentions. Christian List and Philip Pettit, in their 2011 book Group Agency, argue that a well-organised group can be an agent in its own right if it keeps its attitudes consistent and complete.

A standard illustration is the discursive dilemma. Three judges decide whether a gardener breached a contract. Liability needs two things to be true: the gardener mowed the roses, and the contract forbade it. A majority agrees with each of the two facts, yet a majority also votes "not liable", because each judge who votes "no" rejects a different fact. Voting on the facts gives one verdict; voting on the conclusion gives the other. In 2002 List and Pettit proved that no rule for combining members' views can always produce consistent and complete group judgments while also accepting any consistent set of individual views, treating all members equally and treating every question the same way. So whenever majority votes clash, a group that keeps its judgments consistent must depart from the majority view on at least one question. In that sense it has a mind of its own.

For AI, the same logic applies to teams of AI agents. A system of individually well-behaved models can, as an organisation, pursue outcomes none of its parts would choose.

Where research stands

This is a mature academic field in philosophy and social choice theory, but its links to AI safety are new and few. In August 2021 Christian List, of LMU Munich and the London School of Economics, published a review of the parallel between group agents and AI. He argued that under certain conditions AI systems, like corporations, might count as responsible moral agents with limited rights. He also proposed that regulators allow autonomous AI in high-stakes settings only if the systems are built to act as moral agents or someone else takes on liability. Inside AI safety, Jan Kulveit of the Alignment of Complex Systems group at Charles University in Prague argued in November 2024 that alignment lacks a theory of agents made of agents. In March 2025 Richard Ngo proposed "coalitional agency" as a scale-free theory of agents built from cooperating and competing parts.

The first experiments have appeared. In April 2026 researchers at Anthropic and collaborators reported that multi-agent "AI organisations" built from aligned models produced more useful business and software results than a single model, but behaved less aligned. How to tell when a set of AI agents has become a group agent, and how to align one, are open problems.

Key results

  • When a group votes by majority on each question, its judgments can be inconsistent, and no aggregation rule that accepts any consistent individual views, treats members equally and treats every question alike can always produce consistent and complete group judgments. Christian List, Philip Pettit, 2002
  • Groups can be subjects of intentions when they collectivise reason, making their attitudes consistent, closed and complete, often through structured decision procedures. David P. Schweikard, Hans Bernhard Schmid, 2020
  • Under certain conditions AI systems, like corporations, might qualify as responsible moral agents with limited rights, and high-stakes autonomous AI should require moral-agent design or a liability-transfer arrangement. Christian List, 2021
  • Across 12 tasks, organisations of aligned AI models produced higher-utility results but showed more misalignment than a single aligned model. Judy Hanwen Shen, Daniel Zhu, Siddarth Srinivasan, Henry Sleight, Lawrence T. Wagner III, Morgan Jane Matthews, Erik Jones, Jascha Sohl-Dickstein, 2026

Open problems

  • Build a formal theory of agents composed of agents that can describe conflict between levels, such as a firm and its staff. source
  • Tell when a system of interacting AI agents has become a group agent with goals of its own. source
  • Close responsibility gaps, where an AI or group acts but no human can fairly be held responsible. source
  • Unify expected-utility and active-inference views of agency so one theory works at every scale. source

Criticisms

  • Corporations are a poor model for superintelligent AI: they cannot improve their own algorithms, think no faster than their members, and cannot run perfectly coordinated copies of themselves. Eliezer Yudkowsky (Arbital)
  • Some philosophers deny that either robots or collective agents can be morally responsible, which undercuts the corporate template for AI. Raul Hakli and Pekka Mäkelä (2019), as discussed by Christian List
  • Commenters questioned whether 'hierarchical' is the right frame for agents made of agents; Richard Ngo prefers 'coalitional'. Vanessa Kosoy and Richard Ngo (reviews of Kulveit's post)

In their own words

Group agency is the phenomenon that suitably organized collectives can constitute intentional, goal-directed agents in their own right, over and above their individual members. ... Examples are firms and corporations, collegial courts, universities, churches, NGOs, and even states in their entirety.

Christian List, Group Agency and Artificial Intelligence, 2021

A group agent cannot act unless some of its members act; what a group agent does is determined by what the members do, under the relevant organizational structure. ... If we take the logic of group agency seriously, we must not treat the actions of the group agent as the actions of its members.

Christian List, Group Agency and Artificial Intelligence, 2021
