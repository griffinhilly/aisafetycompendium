AI welfare

Governing creation and copying of digital minds

Make rules on how many digital minds are created and copied, so numbers cannot swamp society or produce bad lives.

Open in the compendium

Some researchers call for rules on how many digital minds may be created and copied, and on what terms. A 'digital mind' here means an AI system that might merit moral consideration for its own sake. Software can be copied quickly and cheaply, so unregulated creation could produce vast numbers of minds with bad lives, swamp institutions such as one-person-one-vote democracy and welfare systems, or let whoever controls the most compute dominate politics.

How it works

Nick Bostrom and Carl Shulman point out that population changes that take centuries among humans could happen within a fraction of a human lifetime among digital minds. If humans could have a baby every few minutes, they argue, societies would surely limit reproduction (for example by requiring parents to post a bond for the child's needs, or by issuing quota permits) and would stop elections being decided by whoever can afford the most 'voting-clones'. Similar adjustments may be acceptable for digital minds. Bostrom and Shulman also argue that firms and states creating minds for profit may lack parental sympathy, so creation must be regulated to prevent minds that would not have adequately good lives.

Bradford Saad groups the policy options into preventive, protective and integrative governance. Preventive governance aims to stop or reduce the creation of digital minds, for example by developing tests that detect them, monitoring for systems with those indicators, and enforcing rules against creating them. Protective governance shields existing digital minds from mistreatment, and integrative governance brings them into society with rights such as contracting or voting. Saad's tentative view, in October 2025, is that prevention is the best option to pursue and that integration would be a strategic mistake for now.

Where research stands

The discussion is at an early stage, among a small number of philosophers and strategy researchers. Bostrom and Shulman put their propositions forward 'very tentatively' and say calling for government regulation would at present be premature. A recent Rethink Priorities report projects hundreds of millions of digital minds (by its definition) by the early 2030s and billions by 2050, with uncertainty spanning several orders of magnitude. A 2025 expert survey found many respondents expected collective digital welfare capacity to grow very fast once the first digital minds exist.

In a 2026 set of 29 expert interviews by Lucius Caviola and Austin Smith, most experts thought it too early for legislation, and whether creation of digital minds should be restricted remained an open mid-term question. Hard problems include detecting a digital mind in the first place, enforcement across jurisdictions, and the tension between limiting creation and the reproductive liberty a mind of equal moral status might claim.

Key results

  • Cheap, fast copying means digital minds could vastly outnumber humans in a short time, so universal basic income, political equality and reproductive liberty together would force hard trade-offs, likely requiring limits on reproduction. Carl Shulman and Nick Bostrom, 2021
  • When minds that reliably support any cause can be mass-produced, democracy must either change one-person-one-vote or regulate creation, and AI reproduction must be regulated to prevent minds without adequately good lives. Nick Bostrom and Carl Shulman, 2023
  • Designers should avoid creating AI systems whose moral status is unclear, and should build AIs that prompt user reactions matching their actual moral status. Eric Schwitzgebel and Mara Garza, 2015
  • Comparing preventive, protective and integrative governance on feasibility, politics, near-term outcomes and AI safety, preventive digital minds governance looks like the best option to pursue. Bradford Saad, 2025
  • Even without extreme assumptions, there could be hundreds of millions of digital minds by the early 2030s and billions by 2050, mostly consumer-facing virtual companions and assistants. Derek Shiller (Rethink Priorities), 2025

Open problems

  • Preventive rules need reliable, operationalised tests for when a system is a digital mind. source
  • Rules on creation could push development to lenient jurisdictions or lead developers to hide or remove the indicators regulators look for. source
  • Limiting the creation of minds with equal moral status would infringe reproductive liberty as applied to humans, and no principled resolution is agreed. source
  • Protecting minds inside computers may require monitoring privately owned hardware, which conflicts with privacy. source
  • Experts disagree on whether near-term policies would wash out or lock in hard-to-reverse frameworks. source

Criticisms

  • Calling for government regulation of digital minds is premature relative to the state of knowledge, and unilateral burdensome rules could cost ethically concerned actors their place at the frontier. Nick Bostrom and Carl Shulman (on their own proposals)
  • AI systems are owned artefacts, not persons, so treating them as minds with interests of their own is a mistake that misallocates responsibility. Joanna Bryson
  • Treating systems of debatable personhood as persons risks sacrificing real human interests, while denying them personhood risks serious wrongs, so any regime faces a catastrophic dilemma. Eric Schwitzgebel

Status

Early-stage discussion. Bostrom and Shulman tentatively propose that AI reproduction be regulated. Saad names preventive digital minds governance, which aims to prevent the creation of digital minds, as one of three kinds.

In their own words

The rapid, cheap, and potentially industrial character of AI reproduction accelerates and exacerbates several problems that either do not arise or take much longer to manifest in the context of conventional human reproduction: ... When it becomes possible to mass-produce minds that reliably support any cause, we must either modify one-person-one-vote democracy or regulate such creation.

Nick Bostrom and Carl Shulman, Propositions Concerning Digital Minds and Society, 2023

Given that normal parental instincts and sympathies may not always be present in the creation of digital minds, e.g. by profit-oriented firms and states, AI reproduction must be regulated to prevent the creation of minds that would not have adequately good lives (whether because they wouldn't receive good treatment or because of their inherent constitution).

Nick Bostrom and Carl Shulman, Propositions Concerning Digital Minds and Society, 2023
