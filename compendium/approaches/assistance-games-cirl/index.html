Theory: the maths of agents and goals

Assistance games / cooperative inverse reinforcement learning

Model the AI as uncertain about what the human wants, so it defers rather than assumes.

Open in the compendium

An assistance game is a model of a person and an AI working toward the person's goal, where only the person knows what that goal is. The AI has to learn the goal from what the person does and says. Because the AI is unsure what the person wants, it has a reason to ask, watch and defer, and in some versions to let itself be switched off.

How it works

Picture a kitchen robot that does not know whether its owner wants soup or salad. If it were simply told "make dinner" and given a fixed score, it would pick whatever scores highest and push ahead. In an assistance game the robot's score is the owner's score, but it cannot see that score directly. So it treats what the owner does as evidence: if the owner reaches for the lettuce, salad becomes more likely, and the robot changes its plan. The owner, in turn, has a reason to act in ways that teach the robot.

Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning (CIRL), posted in June 2016 by Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan, Pieter Abbeel and Stuart Russell at UC Berkeley, proposed a formal definition of this problem. Inverse reinforcement learning means inferring someone's goals from their behaviour. CIRL makes it a two-player game: both players are rewarded by the human's reward function, which the robot does not know at the start. The authors showed that good play includes active teaching by the human and active learning by the robot.

The same team's "off-switch game" (November 2016) applied the idea to shutdown. A robot that is certain of its goal has a reason to disable its off switch, unless the human is perfectly rational. A robot that is uncertain, and that reads the human's attempt to switch it off as information about what is good, has a reason to leave the switch alone. Stuart Russell built his October 2019 book Human Compatible around this idea.

Where research stands

The core theory is well established and widely cited, but practical use is young. For years assistance games were solved only in tiny toy settings, because the AI must plan while tracking many possible goals at once. In April 2025 a Berkeley team presented AssistanceZero, which extends the AlphaZero game-playing method with a network that predicts the human's actions and rewards. In a Minecraft building task with more than 10^400 possible goals, it beat standard reinforcement learning and imitation baselines, and in a study with people it reduced the number of actions they needed to finish building. The authors pitch assistance games as an alternative to reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), the standard method for tuning chat models.

The safety claims rest on assumptions that critics have tested. The off-switch result needs the robot's model of the human and of the reward to be roughly right. Ryan Carey showed in September 2017 that if the reward model is mis-specified, for example through a programmer error, the incentive to accept shutdown can disappear. A related argument, the "problem of fully updated deference", says that an AI that expects to learn the answer later has a reason to avoid shutdown now. Milli and colleagues (May 2017) also showed a built-in tension: a robot that acts on inferred preferences rather than literal orders can serve an irrational human better, but is then less obedient. A December 2024 paper found a further problem: when human and assistant each see only part of the world, an optimal assistant can sometimes gain by interfering with what the human sees. As of October 2026 this is mainly a research programme at UC Berkeley's Center for Human-Compatible AI and close collaborators, such as a Carnegie Mellon lab.

Key results

  • Value alignment can be framed as a cooperative two-player game with partial information, and good play in it includes active teaching and active learning that plain inverse reinforcement learning lacks. Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan, Pieter Abbeel, Stuart Russell, 2016
  • A robot that takes its objective as given has an incentive to disable its off switch unless the human is perfectly rational, while one that is uncertain and treats the human's choice as evidence has a reason to keep it. Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan, Pieter Abbeel, Stuart Russell, 2016
  • If the robot's reward model is mis-specified, the incentive to follow shutdown commands in the CIRL setting can vanish. Ryan Carey, 2017
  • When one policy both learns the goal and acts, an assistant can plan around its own learning, for example making a pie crust and waiting to ask about the filling, which separate reward-learning agents cannot do. Rohin Shah, Pedro Freire, Neel Alex, Rachel Freedman, Dmitrii Krasheninnikov, Lawrence Chan, Michael Dennis, Pieter Abbeel, Anca Dragan, Stuart Russell, 2020
  • An AlphaZero-style planner solved an assistance game in Minecraft with more than 10^400 possible goals and helped real people finish building tasks in fewer actions. Cassidy Laidlaw, Eli Bronstein, Timothy Guo, Dylan Feng, Lukas Berglund, Justin Svegliato, Stuart Russell, Anca Dragan, 2025
  • Even when the human plays optimally, an optimal assistant in a partially observable assistance game sometimes has a reason to interfere with what the human can see. Scott Emmons, Caspar Oesterheld, Vincent Conitzer, Stuart Russell, 2024

Open problems

  • Keep the shutdown incentive when the AI's model of the human or of the reward is wrong. source
  • Stop an AI that expects to resolve its uncertainty later from resisting correction now (the problem of fully updated deference). source
  • Remove the incentive for an assistant to tamper with what the human observes when each side sees only part of the world. source
  • Build realistic models of human behaviour, since people are not the near-rational demonstrators the theory often assumes. source
  • Handle preferences that change over time and that the AI itself can influence. source
  • Scale assistance-game training to general-purpose language-model assistants, beyond a single building task. source

Criticisms

  • The deference comes from uncertainty, and an AI that expects to learn the answer has a reason to avoid shutdown until it does, so uncertainty alone does not give lasting corrigibility. Eliezer Yudkowsky et al. (Arbital / LessWrong wiki)
  • The off-switch incentive depends on a correct reward model and breaks under ordinary programming errors. Ryan Carey
  • Humans, and things we might want to help such as ecosystems, are not well modelled as agents with fixed objectives, which the framework assumes. Alex Flint

Who works on it

  • Center for Human-Compatible AI (CHAI), UC Berkeley: Home of the assistance-games agenda; its researchers wrote the assistance-versus-reward-learning and partially observable assistance-game papers. Observation Interference in Partially Observable Assistance Games (2024)
  • University of California, Berkeley (Anca Dragan and Stuart Russell's groups): Built AssistanceZero, which its authors call the first scalable approach to solving assistance games. AssistanceZero: Scalably Solving Assistance Games (2025)
  • Foundations of Cooperative AI Lab, Carnegie Mellon University: Co-authored the analysis of when assistants gain by interfering with what humans see. Observation Interference in Partially Observable Assistance Games (2024)

In their own words

We propose a formal definition of the value alignment problem as cooperative inverse reinforcement learning (CIRL). A CIRL problem is a cooperative, partial-information game with two agents, human and robot; both are rewarded according to the human's reward function, but the robot does not initially know what this is.

Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan, Pieter Abbeel, Stuart Russell, Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning, 2016

Our key insight is that for R to want to preserve its off switch, it needs to be uncertain about the utility associated with the outcome, and to treat H's actions as important observations about that utility.

Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan, Pieter Abbeel, Stuart Russell, The Off-Switch Game, 2017
