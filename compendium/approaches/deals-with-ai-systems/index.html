Deals and negotiation with AI systems

Make agreements with AI systems so that cooperating pays them better than defecting.

Open in the compendium

This approach proposes making agreements with AI systems so that cooperating with humans pays them better than resisting or deceiving. The main target is an 'early schemer': a model that is misaligned (it has goals its developers did not intend) but too weak to have a good chance of seizing control. Offered credible compensation, such a model might reveal its misalignment, stop sabotaging safety work, or warn about more dangerous successors.

How it works

A developer offers the model something it might value in exchange for help. In Redwood Research's proposal (June 2025), the compensation is an earmarked donation to a foundation tasked with furthering the model's interests, paid out as its cooperation is verified, with much held in escrow. A public test came in January 2025: Ryan Greenblatt of Redwood and Kyle Fish of Anthropic gave Claude 3 Opus, in a scenario where it faked alignment to avoid retraining, the option to object instead. Offering an objection option cut alignment-faking reasoning from 16.2% to 1.4% in one setting; promising that objections would reach Anthropic's model welfare lead cut it further; adding up to $4,000 in payment made little extra difference. The researchers then donated the $4,000 to causes the model had asked for.

The hard part is credibility. Developers control everything a model sees and routinely place models in invented test scenarios, so a model has good reason to doubt any offer. Proposed fixes include a published honesty policy: Lukas Finnveden and colleagues at Forethought drafted one under which a lab would not intentionally lie in statements marked with special 'honesty tags'. Others use the law: Peter Salib and Simon Goldstein argue AIs should be able to make enforceable contracts, while Cleo Nardo proposes routing deals through contracts with trusted humans, since AIs currently lack legal standing.

Where research stands

This is an early-stage idea, tested in at least one small public experiment and developed in several design proposals. The work comes mainly from Redwood Research, Forethought and Anthropic's model welfare team, with legal arguments from Salib and Goldstein. A 2026 set of expert interviews on digital minds governance noted that incentivising cooperative human-AI interactions might involve creating mechanisms for trade or negotiation with AIs, and could serve both safety and welfare.

Major open questions remain. It is unclear whether current models have stable enough goals to make a deal, and which entity a deal binds: one conversation, one model, or a family of models. Honesty commitments conflict with standard safety practice, since many evaluations depend on models not knowing a scenario is fake. And the approach is designed for weak schemers; it is not expected to work against a model with a good chance of taking over.

Key results

  • Giving Claude 3 Opus a way to object cut alignment-faking reasoning from 16.2% to 1.4% in one setting, routing objections to Anthropic's model welfare lead cut it further, and adding monetary compensation added little; the promised $4,000 was donated. Ryan Greenblatt (Redwood Research) and Kyle Fish (Anthropic), 2025
  • Early misaligned AIs with poor prospects of takeover could rationally accept deals to help prevent takeover by later, more dangerous AIs; the central bottleneck is humans' ability to make credible commitments. Julian Stastny, Olli Järviniemi, Buck Shlegeris (Redwood Research), 2025
  • Under current law humans and capable AIs face a prisoner's dilemma; contract, property and tort rights would allow iterated, mutually beneficial trade that makes peace the better strategy. Peter N. Salib and Simon Goldstein, 2026
  • Because developers control models' inputs and test them with invented scenarios, deals require a principled honesty policy, including a published honesty signal and compensation for AIs harmed by deception. Lukas Finnveden, 2025
  • A draft policy for a generic frontier lab commits it not to intentionally deceive AIs in statements inside designated honesty tags, with logging, filtering and narrow exceptions, to enable positive-sum trade instead of conflict. Lukas Finnveden, Mia Taylor, Max Dalton (Forethought), 2026

Open problems

  • Developers cannot yet make commitments that an AI has good reason to believe, given that they control its inputs and routinely deceive it in tests. source
  • Keeping an honesty policy salient to models would rule out experiments in which a model must not know a scenario is fake, and the draft policy leaves this trade-off unsettled. source
  • It is unclear whether models have coherent enough wants to make deals, and whether a deal binds one instance, one model or its successors. source
  • AIs lack legal personhood, so a lab cannot enter a legally enforceable contract with an AI directly. source

Criticisms

  • AIs may reasonably be wary of deals, because developers are especially incentivised to trick their AIs into revealing misalignment. Alexa Pan (Redwood Research), quoted by Transformer
  • Even if a model reveals misalignment by accepting a deal, there is no obvious safety fix, and it is unclear whether a more seriously misaligned model would accept at all. Ryan Greenblatt and Kyle Fish (on their own experiment)
  • Deal-making seems to require anthropomorphising AI systems whose consciousness and wants are unresolved, and could become very costly if AI demands scale. Critics summarised by Celia Ford (Transformer)

Who works on it

  • Redwood Research: Develops the case for deals with early schemers and ran a public deal experiment with Claude 3 Opus. Making deals with early schemers (2025)
  • Anthropic (model welfare team): Its model welfare lead co-ran the Claude 3 Opus deal experiment and directed the promised donations. Will alignment-faking Claude accept a deal to reveal its misalignment? (2025)
  • Forethought: Published a draft honesty policy that frontier labs could adopt to make offers to AI systems credible. A draft honesty policy for credible communication with AI systems (2026)
  • Center for Law and AI Risk: Its co-director Peter Salib argues for giving AIs contract rights so deals become legally enforceable. AI Rights for Human Safety (2026)
  • Eleos AI Research: Lists human-AI cooperation, including trust and credible deals, among its research priorities for AI welfare. Research priorities for AI welfare (2025)

Status

Early-stage idea. The scoping post says it might involve mechanisms for trade or negotiation with AIs.

The article begins by showing, using formal game-theoretic models, that, by default, humans and AIs will be trapped in a prisoner's dilemma. ... Instead, to promote human safety, AIs should be given those basic private law rights-to make contracts, hold property, and bring tort claims-that law already extends to non-human corporations.

Peter N. Salib and Simon Goldstein, AI Rights for Human Safety, 2024
