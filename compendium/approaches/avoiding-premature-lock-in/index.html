Avoiding premature lock-in (the long reflection)

Keep key choices reversible, and avoid irreversible steps until humanity has had time to work out what it wants.

Open in the compendium

This approach says humanity should avoid making permanent choices about its values and institutions before it understands what it really wants. 'Lock-in' means a state of affairs, such as a set of values written into powerful AI systems or a regime that cannot be removed, that becomes practically impossible to change. The 'long reflection' is the name for a hoped-for period in which people work out, slowly and carefully, how best to use the future. It matters for AI because advanced AI could make extreme forms of lock-in technically feasible.

How it works

The idea runs in stages. Toby Ord's book The Precipice (2020) puts it as a sequence: first reach existential security (a state where the risk of catastrophe is low and stays low), then hold a long reflection about what the best future looks like, and only then try to achieve it. William MacAskill was already using the term in an interview published in January 2018, saying 'I call it the long reflection'.

In practice the approach turns into policies that keep options open. MacAskill's 2025 essay 'How to Make the Future Better' gives examples: prevent autocracies from entrenching themselves with AI, slow the grab for resources in space, and make major new laws and institutions explicitly temporary, so that they must be reauthorised after a set period. For AI values, it suggests that alignment should aim at good processes of reflection, not just at a fixed set of values.

Where research stands

This is a philosophical proposal, not a technical programme. The research group Forethought treats value lock-in as one of the 'grand challenges' of an intelligence explosion and studies what a good intermediate state, which it calls 'viatopia', would look like. A key supporting argument, by Lukas Finnveden, Jess Riedel and Carl Shulman (2022), is that AGI could make extreme lock-in technically feasible, because AI systems could preserve goals far into the future without degradation.

There is no agreed plan for how a long reflection would actually be governed, and critics doubt it can be. The concept was framed before the current AI boom, and recent writers have tried to update it. In July 2026, for example, Wei Dai proposed a 'long self-correction' that focuses on fixing human flaws in judgment rather than only giving humans more time.

Key results

  • AGI could make extreme forms of lock-in technologically feasible, letting values or goals be preserved without degradation 'for at least millions, and plausibly trillions, of years'; the claim is about feasibility, not likelihood. Lukas Finnveden, Jess Riedel, Carl Shulman, 2022
  • An intelligence explosion could let some groups lock in particular views and values through permanent AI values, commitment technology, preference-shaping technology or a global government. William MacAskill, Fin Moorhouse, 2025
  • Practical ways to keep options open include preventing post-AGI autocracy, delaying off-world resource extraction, and making major new institutions temporary by default. William MacAskill, 2025
  • Because we may hold morally monstrous beliefs today, keeping moral options open has value, which motivates a long reflection. Robert Wiblin, Keiran Harris, William MacAskill, 2018

Open problems

  • Describing a reachable intermediate state ('viatopia') that would reliably lead to a good future, which MacAskill flags as needing research. source
  • Preventing irreversible change for a long period without the strong, intrusive global coordination that critics say this would require. source
  • Deciding which philosophical questions cannot wait for a long reflection because they shape which risks to reduce now. source
  • Avoiding lock-in through early choices about AI rights and the allocation of space resources. source

Criticisms

  • Stopping irreversible change for centuries would need global coordination close to a world government, and such a regime might never end. Robin Hanson (Overcoming Bias, October 2021)
  • Some ethical work cannot be deferred to a long reflection, because it is needed now to decide which risks to reduce. Theron Pummer (Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews, 2020)
  • Giving humans more time is not enough; humans are not safe as builders, overseers or alignment targets for powerful AI until their flaws are corrected, so existential security comes after that correction, not before. Wei Dai (LessWrong, July 2026)

Who works on it

  • Forethought: Publishes research on value lock-in, keeping options open, and the 'Better Futures' series. How to Make the Future Better (2025)

Status

Philosophical proposal. In a January 2018 interview, MacAskill describes a "long reflection": once extinction risks are near zero, humanity would spend "tens of thousands of years" working out what is of value before building civilization in one particular way.

A key way to keep our options open is to prevent the emergence or dominance of autocracy. ... In order to keep our options open, we might want to delay the point of time at which widespread resource extraction or space settlement occurs, and advocate for restrictions on offworld resource grabs.
