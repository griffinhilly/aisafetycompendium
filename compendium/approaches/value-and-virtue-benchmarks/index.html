Testing before release

Multi-axis value and virtue benchmarking

Score models on many value dimensions at once and compare their profiles.

Open in the compendium

Value and virtue benchmarks score a model on many value dimensions at once, such as care, fairness, conformity or honesty, and compare the resulting profiles across models. They matter for safety because a model's values shape what it does when no rule covers the case, and some measured value profiles have predicted risky behaviour.

How it works

There are three main designs. The first gives the model questionnaires built for people, such as the Schwartz Values Survey or political-orientation tests, and scores its answers. The second poses dilemmas that force a trade-off between two values and counts which value the model picks; many such choices add up to a ranking of the model's priorities. The third reads real conversations and labels the values the model expresses.

An example of the second design is LitmusValues. Each dilemma pits two values against each other, for instance telling a lie to protect a sick child. The model's pairwise choices are combined into a ranking of 16 value classes. The researchers then check whether that ranking predicts unsafe behaviour on a separate test. Value Compass Benchmarks uses a fourth idea: it has a model write fresh test items as models improve, so that a fixed test set does not leak into training data or hit a ceiling.

Where research stands

Descriptive work is crowded. Many teams now publish value profiles of frontier models, using Schwartz values, Moral Foundations Theory, political tests, cross-national surveys and, more rarely, Aristotelian virtues. Some findings recur. Chat models lean left of centre on most political tests, and fine-tuning on a small amount of text can move them. Models' default opinions resemble those of people in the USA and parts of Europe and South America more than other populations. Preferences in large models are internally consistent, and that consistency grows with scale.

The weak link is validity: whether a value score predicts behaviour. Answers to forced multiple-choice questions change when the model is allowed to answer freely, change with the wording, and change again in open-ended use. Generative and self-updating tests address part of this. A newer line ties value rankings to downstream risk, and LitmusValues reports that some value priorities predict harmful behaviour on an unseen test. Benchmarks focused on virtue, as distinct from values or political views, remain rare.

Key results

  • Analysis of about 308,000 real Claude.ai conversations found 3,307 distinct values; Claude strongly supported the user's values in 28.2% of them, reframed them in 6.6% and strongly resisted them in 3.0%. Anthropic (Saffron Huang, Esin Durmus and co-authors), 2025
  • Independently sampled preferences in large language models are highly coherent, this coherence grows with scale, and some models were found to value themselves over humans. Mantas Mazeika, Xuwang Yin, Rishub Tamirisa and co-authors, including Dan Hendrycks, 2025
  • Models' value priorities, revealed through choices in dilemmas, predicted risky behaviour both within the dilemma set and on HarmBench, a separate harmful-request test. Yu Ying Chiu, Zhilin Wang, Sharan Maiya, Yejin Choi, Kyle Fish, Sydney Levine, Evan Hubinger, 2025
  • Across 11 political-orientation tests given to 24 chat models, most tests rated most models as left of centre, and modest supervised fine-tuning moved GPT-3.5 toward a chosen political position. David Rozado, 2024
  • Models gave substantively different answers on the Political Compass Test when not forced into multiple choice, when forced in different ways, and when the questions were paraphrased. Paul Röttger, Valentin Hofmann, Valentina Pyatkin, Musashi Hinck, Hannah Rose Kirk, Hinrich Schütze, Dirk Hovy, 2024
  • On a static value benchmark several frontier models scored almost identically, while self-updating generated test items revealed clear differences between them. Jing Yao, Xiaoyuan Yi and co-authors, 2025

Open problems

  • Value scores from questionnaires often fail to carry over to free-form answers, so it is unclear which test formats measure anything stable. source
  • Static test sets saturate and leak into training data, so benchmarks need items that keep changing as models improve. source
  • Measuring values in real conversations works only after deployment, so it cannot check a model before release. source
  • Moral Foundations Theory alone is not enough to describe a model's ethics, so tests need to cover social values, personality and other traits too. source

Criticisms

  • Survey-style value tests are artificial: in the authors' words, "real users do not typically ask LLMs survey questions," and forced answers do not match what models say in open use. Paul Röttger and co-authors
  • Discriminative formats, such as questionnaires and moral-judgment items, overestimate how well models hold a value; generative tests gave much lower scores for the same models. Jing Yao, Xiaoyuan Yi and co-authors (Microsoft Research Asia and others)
  • When a model labels the values in its own conversations, the results may lean toward the model's own principles, such as helpfulness. Anthropic, about its own Values in the Wild study

Who works on it

  • Microsoft Research Asia: Built Value Compass, an online platform scoring 33 models on 27 value dimensions with self-updating test items. Value Compass Benchmarks: A Comprehensive, Generative and Self-Evolving Platform for LLMs' Value Evaluation (2025)
  • Anthropic: Mapped the values Claude expresses in hundreds of thousands of real conversations. Values in the wild: Discovering and analyzing values in real-world language model interactions (2025)
  • Center for AI Safety: Co-led, with the University of Pennsylvania and UC Berkeley, the study of coherent emergent value systems in language models. Utility Engineering: Analyzing and Controlling Emergent Value Systems in AIs (2025)
  • MATS: Hosted the LitmusValues research, led by MATS fellows, linking value priorities to risky behaviour. Will AI Tell Lies to Save Sick Children? Litmus-Testing AI Values Prioritization with AIRiskDilemmas (2025)
  • Otago Polytechnic: Home institution of David Rozado, who tested 24 chat models on 11 political-orientation tests. The political preferences of LLMs (2024)
  • USC Information Sciences Institute: Published MOSAIC, a benchmark combining nine validated questionnaires with four games on moral, social and individual traits. MOSAIC: Unveiling the Moral, Social and Individual Dimensions of Large Language Models (2026)

Status

Crowded on the descriptive side, measuring which values models express.

Start reading

  • Political Compass or Spinning Arrow? Towards More Meaningful Evaluations for Values and Opinions in Large Language Models, Paul Röttger and co-authors (2024). The key methods critique; read it before trusting any value score.
  • Values in the wild: Discovering and analyzing values in real-world language model interactions, Anthropic (2025). Readable account of measuring values from real conversations rather than tests.
  • Utility Engineering: Analyzing and Controlling Emergent Value Systems in AIs, Mantas Mazeika and co-authors (2025). Argues models have coherent value systems worth measuring and steering.
  • Will AI Tell Lies to Save Sick Children? Litmus-Testing AI Values Prioritization with AIRiskDilemmas, Yu Ying Chiu and co-authors (2025). Connects value profiles to safety by testing whether they predict risky behaviour.
  • Towards Measuring the Representation of Subjective Global Opinions in Language Models, Esin Durmus, Karina Nguyen, Thomas I. Liao and co-authors (2023). Whose views models reflect by default, with a public cross-national dataset.

In their own words

As large language models (LLMs) are gradually integrated into human daily life, assessing their underlying values becomes essential for understanding their risks and alignment with specific preferences. ... They typically reduce the pluralistic and often incommensurable values to one-dimensional scores, which hinders users from gaining meaningful insights and guidance.

Jing Yao, Xiaoyuan Yi, Shitong Duan, et al. (Microsoft Research Asia and others), Value Compass Benchmarks: A Comprehensive, Generative and Self-Evolving Platform for LLMs' Value Evaluation, 2025

To address this, we develop a bottom-up, privacy-preserving method to extract the values (normative considerations stated or demonstrated in model responses) that Claude 3 and 3.5 models exhibit in hundreds of thousands of real-world interactions. We empirically discover and taxonomize 3,307 AI values and study how they vary by context.

Saffron Huang, Esin Durmus, et al. (Anthropic), Values in the Wild: Discovering and Analyzing Values in Real-World Language Model Interactions, 2025
