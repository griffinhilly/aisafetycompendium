Society: defence and who holds power

Reducing the motive to misuse

Lower the number of people who want to cause mass harm, by fixing social causes such as inequality and poor mental health care.

Open in the compendium

This approach tries to shrink the number of people who would want to use AI to cause mass harm, rather than only restricting what AI can do. Yoshua Bengio argued in May 2023 that the risk of rogue AI is one more reason to reduce suffering, injustice, inequality and untreated mental illness. Its value depends on how many motivated people it takes to cause a catastrophe once dangerous AI is easy to obtain.

How it works

Most misuse defences act on capability: they block dangerous requests, secure model weights or limit access. This approach acts on motive. Bengio's essay "How Rogue AIs may Arise" notes that the sources of extreme violence include anger from injustice, hate from racism, conspiracy theories or cults, and psychotic episodes. What limits these today is that such people are rare and lack catastrophic means; publicly available AI could remove the second limit. His proposed responses are long-term social ones: enough food and health care for everyone, much lower wealth inequality, worldwide education in compassion and critical thinking, and accessible mental health care.

Nick Bostrom's 2019 "vulnerable world" paper analyses the same lever under the name "preference modification" and finds its value depends on who can act. If a technology lets any individual cause devastation, the pool of willing people would have to fall all the way to zero, which he says may be completely infeasible; halving it might cut the risk by only 5 or 10 percent. But if, early on, only a small group (such as staff of specialised labs) can use the technology, closer screening of that group could make a real difference. Where only a few states can act, making even those states more peace-loving would help a great deal.

A related strand looks at the few people who gain great power. David Althaus and Tobias Baumann (2020) propose developing hard-to-fake measures of malevolent traits to screen people for high-impact roles such as heads of government or company leaders.

Where research stands

This is an essay-level suggestion, not a research programme. The proposals mostly overlap with long-standing goals of public health, education and anti-poverty policy. The AI-specific work that does exist focuses on narrower, more tractable versions: screening people who get access to dangerous capabilities, and keeping malevolent people out of power.

The evidence on root causes is not encouraging for the broadest version. Alan Krueger and Jitka Malečková's 2003 study, which they call tentative, suggested that neither poverty nor low education has a direct causal link to terrorism; in their Hezbollah data, better-off and better-educated people were more likely to take part. That does not refute Bengio's wider case for reducing suffering, but it suggests motive-reduction through economic policy alone would be a weak defence against rare, determined attackers.

Key results

  • AI could remove the main limit on rare violent individuals, their lack of catastrophic means; reducing injustice, inequality and untreated mental illness would shrink the pool of would-be attackers. Yoshua Bengio, 2023
  • For technologies any individual could use, halving the number of people willing to cause devastation might cut risk by only 5 to 10 percent; motive-shifting helps more when only a few actors are empowered. Nick Bostrom (Global Policy), 2019
  • Tentative results suggest neither poverty nor education has a direct causal effect on terrorism; higher living standards and education were positively associated with militant participation. Alan B. Krueger, Jitka Malečková (Journal of Economic Perspectives), 2003
  • Manipulation-proof measures of malevolent traits could be used to screen people for high-impact positions, reducing the chance that such people control transformative technology. David Althaus, Tobias Baumann, 2020

Open problems

  • How many willing actors a given AI-enabled harm requires, which decides whether reducing motives can matter at all. source
  • How to screen the small groups who first get access to a dangerous capability without unfairness or abuse. source
  • How to build reliable, hard-to-fake measures of malevolent traits. source

Criticisms

  • Where any individual could cause devastation, preference modification looks unpromising: the pool of willing people would have to fall all the way to zero. Nick Bostrom
  • Poverty and low education are poor predictors of who becomes a terrorist, so economic fixes may not reach the relevant people. Alan B. Krueger and Jitka Malečková

Who works on it

  • Center on Long-Term Risk: Funded David Althaus's work on the analysis of risks from malevolent actors and how to screen for them. Reducing long-term risks from malevolent actors (2020)

Status

Essay-level suggestion. Bengio ties it to education, inequality and mental health care. Bostrom calls it preference modification and doubts it helps much once a dangerous technology is easy for anyone to use.

Not yet named in any of the 24 overview sources in the chart. It is here because the people under “Who works on it” have published or practised it.

Start reading

  • How Rogue AIs may Arise, Yoshua Bengio (2023). The clearest statement of the idea, in its AI-risk context.
  • The Vulnerable World Hypothesis (section on preference modification), Nick Bostrom (2019). Careful analysis of when changing motives helps and when it cannot.
  • Reducing long-term risks from malevolent actors, David Althaus, Tobias Baumann (2020). The narrower, more tractable version focused on people in power.

Related approaches

  • Preventive surveillance with structured transparency Bostrom's alternative: preventive policing
  • Predistribution and windfall mechanisms reducing inequality through windfall sharing
  • Know-your-customer checks by compute and cloud providers screening who gets dangerous access
  • Limiting proliferation of high-risk models limiting the means rather than the motive

In their own words

The rogue AI risk may provide an additional motivation to reform our societies so as to minimize human suffering, misery, poor education and injustice, which can give rise to anger and violence. That includes providing enough food and health care to everyone on Earth, and in order to minimize strong feelings of injustice, greatly reduce wealth inequalities.

Yoshua Bengio, How Rogue AIs may Arise, 2023

Another theoretically possible way of achieving civilizational stabilization would be to change the fact that there exists a large population of actors representing a wide and recognizably human distribution of motives. ... In the case of a Type-1 vulnerability, preference modification does not look promising, at least in the absence of extremely effective means for doing so.

Nick Bostrom (Future of Humanity Institute), The Vulnerable World Hypothesis (Working Paper v3.21), 2018
