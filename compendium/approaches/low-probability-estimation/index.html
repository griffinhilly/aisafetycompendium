Low-probability estimation (estimating rare catastrophic outputs)

Estimate how likely a model is to produce a rare catastrophic output, without having to find an input that triggers it.

Open in the compendium

Low-probability estimation tries to put a number on how likely a model is to do something catastrophic, even when that event is far too rare to observe in testing. Ordinary testing only catches failures it actually sees, and red-teaming only catches failures someone finds an input for. A model deployed to millions of users, or one that misbehaves only in rare situations, can fail in ways no test run ever shows, so a method that estimates the rate without finding the trigger would fill a real gap.

How it works

The problem is posed formally: take a model, a precisely specified distribution of inputs, and a yes-or-no property of the output (for example, "the model emits this target token"). Estimate how often the property holds. When the true rate is one in a billion and the budget allows only about 65,000 model runs, random sampling almost never sees a single case and returns an estimate of zero. That is the gap these methods aim to close.

There are two families of methods. Black-box methods, such as importance sampling, search for inputs that make the rare output more likely and then correct for the bias of that search. White-box (mechanistic) methods use the model's weights directly: they push a simple description of the input distribution through the network layer by layer and calculate the chance of the bad output, without running the model on many inputs. The Alignment Research Center (ARC) pursues the white-box route, because a capable model might not fall for test inputs, while an estimate built from its internals would not depend on finding them. A related, more empirical approach from Anthropic measures how risk rises with the number of queries in a small test and extrapolates it to deployment scale.

Where research stands

This is a small, mostly theoretical area led by one organization, ARC, with a few related efforts elsewhere. Results so far come from small or artificial models. In ARC's October 2024 study on small transformers, importance sampling beat ARC's own extrapolation method, and both beat naive sampling. In May 2026 ARC reported that a mechanistic method beat sampling for estimating the average output of wide, randomly initialised networks, but not yet for trained networks. In June 2026 it launched a public contest, the ARC White-Box Estimation Challenge, with a prize pool of at least $100,000 for later rounds.

Funders list the problem as a priority: the UK AI Security Institute's Alignment Project names low-probability estimation as a research area, the 2025 technical safety call from Coefficient Giving (then Open Philanthropy) included "White-box estimation of rare misbehavior", and Schmidt Sciences' Science of Trustworthy AI call asks about tail risk. Whether the methods will scale to frontier models, and over which input distribution they should estimate risk, remain open. A former ARC researcher has published a pessimistic assessment.

Key results

  • On small transformers, importance sampling estimated rare-output probabilities better than activation extrapolation, and both beat naive sampling; minimising the estimated probability of a bad behaviour generalises adversarial training. Gabriel Wu and Jacob Hilton, 2024 (ICLR 2025)
  • A mechanistic method (cumulant propagation) estimated the expected output of wide random networks about a factor of the width faster than sampling, and beat sampling across seven orders of magnitude of compute in tests with 4 hidden layers. Jacob Hilton, Wilson Wu, George Robinson, Mike Winer, Victor Lecomte, Paul Christiano, 2026
  • ARC set its working goal as the Matching Sampling Principle: a mechanistic estimator should match random sampling's accuracy in comparable time, with the expectation that it will beat sampling on structured, trained models. Eric Neyman, Victor Lecomte, Wilson Wu, Michael Winer, Jacob Hilton, George Robinson, 2025
  • Fitting a power law to the riskiest queries in a small test forecast worst-case risk at 100 times more queries to within one order of magnitude in 86% of misuse forecasts. Erik Jones, Meg Tong, et al. (Anthropic Alignment Science), 2025
  • Neural-network robustness can be measured as the probability that a property is violated over an input distribution, estimated with a rare-event Monte Carlo method, an early version of the same idea. Stefan Webb, Tom Rainforth, Yee Whye Teh, M. Pawan Kumar, 2018 (ICLR 2019)

Open problems

  • Extending mechanistic estimates from randomly initialised networks to trained ones, and to deep networks where depth grows with width. source
  • Choosing the input distribution: estimates over a fixed, formal distribution may not reflect real deployment, and a deceptive model could exploit inputs nobody considered. source
  • Layer-by-layer methods can lose correlations between layers and give poor estimates even for simple networks. source
  • The approach assumes a catastrophe detector that has a reasonable chance of catching every catastrophe and that a useful model almost never triggers, which may fail if the model is much smarter than the detector. source
  • Extending results to "eliciting bad contexts": working out whether a system holds enough information to construct situations where it would behave maliciously. source

Criticisms

  • Good low-probability estimation looks extremely hard and may not become useful: the real deployment distribution is hard to model, and for strategic behaviour the problem reduces to eliciting the model's latent knowledge. David Matolcsi (former ARC researcher)
  • In the author's words, "a strategic model can condition its defection on an astronomically rare trigger", pushing the true probability below any feasible threshold. Ondřej Kubů (LessWrong)
  • Exact estimation is at least NP-hard in general, so the realistic goal is estimates good enough to detect risk; as of September 2024 ARC's white-box methods had not beaten importance-sampling baselines in experiments. Mark Xu (ARC), stating the agenda's own limits

Who works on it

  • Alignment Research Center (ARC): Runs the main research programme on estimating rare model behaviour, mostly with white-box mechanistic methods. Estimating the Probabilities of Rare Outputs in Language Models (2024)
  • Anthropic (Alignment Science team): Forecasts rare harmful behaviours at deployment scale by extrapolating from small tests. Forecasting rare language model behaviors (2025)
  • UK AI Security Institute (The Alignment Project): Lists low-probability estimation as a research area in its Alignment Project funding programme. Research Areas in Probabilistic Methods (The Alignment Project by UK AISI) (2025)
  • Coefficient Giving (formerly Open Philanthropy): Included "White-box estimation of rare misbehavior" among the 21 research areas of its 2025 technical AI safety call. Open Philanthropy Technical AI Safety RFP - $40M Available Across 21 Research Areas (2025)
  • Schmidt Sciences: Its Science of Trustworthy AI call asks whether we can estimate the probability of "consequential but low-frequency failures". Schmidt Sciences' request for proposals on the Science of Trustworthy AI (2026)

Status

Active research area at ARC; funders list rare-failure estimation among priorities (Open Philanthropy 2025 RFP, Schmidt Sciences)

In their own words

We consider the problem of low probability estimation: given a machine learning model and a formally-specified input distribution, how can we estimate the probability of a binary property of the model's output, even when that probability is too small to estimate by random sampling?

Gabriel Wu and Jacob Hilton (Alignment Research Center), Estimating the Probabilities of Rare Outputs in Language Models, 2024

Current techniques for estimating the probability of tail events are based on finding inputs on which an AI behaves catastrophically. ... We are excited about techniques to estimate the probability of tail events that do not rely on finding inputs on which an AI behaves badly, and can thus detect a broader range of catastrophic behavior.

Mark Xu (Alignment Research Center), Estimating Tail Risk in Neural Networks, 2024
