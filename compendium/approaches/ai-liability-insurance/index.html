Holding AI companies to account

Constraining

Commercial AI liability insurance

Actually sell cover against model underperformance and deviation from expected behaviour.

Open in the compendium

AI liability insurance pays a company's costs when its AI system fails and causes loss, for example when a chatbot gives false information that a customer relies on. Insurers price each policy by how risky the system looks, and can demand safety practices before they will cover it. The safety hope is that insurers become private regulators: they reward safer systems with cheaper cover and require tests and standards that buyers would otherwise skip.

How it works

A specialist firm tests or reviews an AI system, then sells a policy that pays for losses from defined failures. Armilla AI's policy, launched in April 2025 with Lloyd's underwriters including Chaucer, covers hallucinations (false or misleading outputs), model drift (performance falling over time) and other deviations from expected behaviour, plus legal defence costs. In January 2026 Armilla raised its limit to $25 million per organisation. Munich Re's aiSure line backs AI vendors' performance guarantees, so a vendor can indemnify a customer for losses caused by its model's errors.

The Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company (AIUC), launched in July 2025, ties all three pieces together: a standard (AIUC-1) for AI agents, technical testing of agents against that standard, and insurance whose terms depend on the audit. Its founders call this an 'incentive flywheel': insurers price risk, standards define safe practice, and audits check compliance.

A push from the other side matters too. In November 2025 AIG, Great American and WR Berkley asked US state regulators to approve exclusions that would remove AI-related claims from ordinary corporate policies. If general policies stop covering AI, companies that use AI need dedicated cover, which gives specialist insurers more say over how AI is built and used.

Where research stands

The product is live but the market is small: a handful of specialists (Armilla, AIUC, Munich Re) sell dedicated cover, mostly for business losses from errors by deployed systems. Mainstream carriers are moving the other way, seeking to add AI exclusions to ordinary corporate policies. Insurers worry less about one large loss than about thousands of correlated claims caused by a single provider's error.

Research asks when insurance actually makes things safer. Cristian Trout's 2025 framework finds that insurance regulates best for catastrophic, non-product accidents, and that frontier AI has 'significant potential' for this effect that will not appear without policy, such as a mandate encouraging a specialised insurer or mutual. The Center for Democracy & Technology launched a research agenda in 2026 on how insurers decide what is insurable.

Key results

  • A standalone AI liability policy at Lloyd's covers hallucinations, model drift and other deviations from expected behaviour, plus legal defence. Chaucer Group, 2025
  • Armilla expanded its standalone AI liability policy to limits up to $25 million per organisation in January 2026. Armilla AI, 2026
  • AIG, Great American and WR Berkley asked US regulators to approve AI exclusions; an Aon executive said the industry could handle a single $400 million loss but not 1,000 or 10,000 correlated claims caused by one AI provider's error. The Decoder (reporting the Financial Times), 2025
  • Insurance reduces harm most where incentives are already distorted and risks are catastrophic; for frontier AI the potential is large but needs a well-designed mandate to be realised. Cristian Trout, 2025
  • Proposes an 'incentive flywheel' in which insurance, standards and audits give AI developers a financial stake in managing risk faster than regulation can. Rune Kvist, Rajiv Dattani, Brandon Wang, 2025

Criticisms

  • Insurance can create moral hazard: firms that are covered may take more risk, so coverage can become a 'safety net' that weakens incentives instead of strengthening them. Center for Democracy & Technology AI Governance Lab (summarising the concern)
  • Without policy intervention the insurance market will not realise its regulatory potential for frontier AI, and pure captive insurers owned by the insured should be barred. Cristian Trout
  • Mainstream carriers are retreating from AI risk through exclusions, which suggests private markets cannot yet carry large correlated AI losses. Kevin Kalinich (Aon), reported via the Financial Times
