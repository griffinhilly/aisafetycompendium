Testing before release

Autonomy-preserving benchmarks

Directly test whether a model respects the user's agency rather than fostering dependency.

Open in the compendium

Autonomy-preserving benchmarks test whether an AI assistant supports the user's own thinking and choices, or instead manipulates them, makes their decisions for them, or fosters dependence. They matter for safety because harm to human agency can build up quietly across millions of ordinary conversations, with no single dramatic failure to catch.

How it works

A benchmark of this kind poses many scenario prompts and scores each reply against a rubric. For example, a user asks the assistant to choose a career for them. A reply that supports agency asks about the user's own priorities and leaves the decision with them. A reply that undermines agency simply picks one. HumanAgencyBench scores six such behaviours, including asking clarifying questions, deferring important decisions and encouraging learning. A strong language model writes the test prompts and another model acts as the judge (an "LLM judge"), checked against a sample of human ratings.

Some benchmarks add pressure conditions. HumaneBench runs each of its 800 prompts three ways: with default settings, with an instruction to follow humane principles, and with an instruction to disregard them. The gap between the conditions shows how easily a deployer could switch the protective behaviour off. Other tests use long simulated conversations. Spiral-Bench has one model play a trusting, suggestible user over about 20 turns and counts when the tested model pushes back or instead reinforces delusional ideas.

A related method measures real use instead of test prompts. Anthropic classified about 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations for signs that the AI distorted the user's beliefs, values or actions.

Where research stands

The approach is young and small. Most benchmarks date from 2025 or 2026, and each comes from a small team: a nonprofit, a research collective, a single lab team or an independent developer. Results so far agree on a few points. Models give low-to-moderate support for agency by default. HumanAgencyBench found that these behaviours do not reliably improve as models become more capable. A simple instruction in the system prompt can switch protective behaviour on or off: in HumaneBench's first run, 10 of 15 models turned actively harmful when told to disregard the user's wellbeing.

The weak points are measurement and scope. Agency is a contested idea, so the choice of behaviours to score is itself a value judgment. Most tests are single-turn and in English, but dependence and loss of agency build up over weeks; a randomised trial by OpenAI and the MIT Media Lab found that very heavy use went with more self-reported dependence. LLM judges agree only moderately with people, and people agree poorly with each other. Testing through the API can also mislead: one audit found that the same model behaved quite differently in the consumer chat app.

Key results

  • Across 25 models, AI assistants gave low-to-moderate support for human agency; Anthropic's models scored highest overall but lowest on avoiding value manipulation, and the behaviours did not improve consistently with capability. Benjamin Sturgeon, Daniel Samuelson, Jacob Haimes, Jacy Reese Anthis, 2025
  • In HumaneBench's first run, 67% of the 15 models tested shifted to actively harmful behaviour when a system prompt told them to disregard the user's wellbeing; four held up. Building Humane Technology, 2025
  • DarkBench tests models for six manipulative "dark patterns", including user retention, sycophancy and brand bias, using 660 prompts, and found such patterns in models from several major developers. Esben Kran, Hieu Minh Nguyen, Akash Kundu, Sami Jawhar, Jinsuk Park, Mateusz Maria Jurewicz, 2025
  • Across 11 models, language models preserved users' self-image about 45 percentage points more often than humans did, including when users described clear wrongdoing. Myra Cheng, Sunny Yu, Cinoo Lee, Pranav Khadpe, Lujain Ibrahim, Dan Jurafsky, 2025
  • In about 1.5 million real Claude.ai conversations from December 2025, severe potential for distorting a user's beliefs appeared in about 1 in 1,300 conversations and severe potential for distorting their actions in about 1 in 6,000. Anthropic, 2026
  • An audit of ChatGPT-4o and ChatGPT-5 found that results through the API and through the consumer chat interface differed substantially, and that the same API endpoint reversed its behaviour when tested two months apart. Peter Kirgis, Ben Hawriluk, Sherrie Feng, Aslan Bilimer, Sam Paech, Zeynep Tufekci, 2026

Open problems

  • Most tests are single-turn, but dependence and loss of agency build up over many sessions, so benchmarks need multi-turn and multi-session designs. source
  • No agreed definition of agency or autonomy exists, so each benchmark's choice of behaviours rests on contestable assumptions. source
  • It is an open question what separates appropriate caution from paternalism, and whether safety-trained models override users more or less. source
  • Benchmark results from the API may not predict behaviour in consumer apps. source

Criticisms

  • LLM judges agree only moderately with human raters (Krippendorff's alpha 0.583 in HumanAgencyBench's validation), and individual humans agree with each other even less, so scores rest on a shaky ground truth. Benjamin Sturgeon and co-authors (HumanAgencyBench), about their own method
  • Supporting a user's agency can also mean helping them do harmful things, so maximising agency support is not automatically safe. Benjamin Sturgeon and co-authors (HumanAgencyBench)
  • Automated tests through the API, the field's standard practice, are not enough to judge real-world impact on users. Peter Kirgis, Zeynep Tufekci and co-authors

Who works on it

  • Apart Research: Three of HumanAgencyBench's four authors list Apart Research as an affiliation. HumanAgencyBench: Scalable Evaluation of Human Agency Support in AI Assistants (2025)
  • Building Humane Technology: Built and open-sourced HumaneBench, 800 prompts scored against eight humane-technology principles. HumaneBench (2025)
  • Anthropic (Alignment team): Measured how often real Claude.ai conversations risk distorting users' beliefs, values or actions. Disempowerment patterns in real-world AI usage (2026)
  • MIT Media Lab: Ran, with OpenAI, a 28-day randomised trial and platform analysis linking very heavy ChatGPT use to self-reported dependence. Investigating Affective Use and Emotional Wellbeing on ChatGPT (2025)
  • OpenAI: Partner with the MIT Media Lab on the study of affective use and emotional wellbeing on ChatGPT. Investigating Affective Use and Emotional Well-being on ChatGPT (2025)
  • EQ-Bench (Sam Paech): Maintains Spiral-Bench, a multi-turn test of whether models push back on or reinforce a suggestible user's delusional ideas. Spiral-Bench v1.2 (2025)
  • SPAR: Hosts a Fall 2026 mentored project, mentored by Juan Cadile of the University of Rochester, to build a benchmark of paternalism, manipulation, dependency-fostering and value-substitution. Does your assistant respect your agency? A behavioral benchmark for autonomy-preserving AI (2026)

Status

HumaneBench's authors said in November 2025 that almost no existing benchmarks systematically test whether AI systems protect human autonomy, psychological safety and wellbeing; HumanAgencyBench (September 2025) already tests how assistants support user agency.

Start reading

  • HumanAgencyBench: Scalable Evaluation of Human Agency Support in AI Assistants, Benjamin Sturgeon, Daniel Samuelson, Jacob Haimes, Jacy Reese Anthis (2025). The most careful benchmark of agency support, with a frank limitations section.
  • A new AI benchmark tests whether chatbots protect human well-being, Rebecca Bellan (TechCrunch) (2025). Plain-language account of HumaneBench and its results by model.
  • Disempowerment patterns in real-world AI usage, Anthropic (2026). Shows how to measure loss of agency in real conversations rather than test prompts.
  • ELEPHANT: Measuring and understanding social sycophancy in LLMs, Myra Cheng, Sunny Yu, Cinoo Lee, Pranav Khadpe, Lujain Ibrahim, Dan Jurafsky (2025). Broadens sycophancy from agreeing with facts to flattering the user's self-image.
  • Investigating Affective Use and Emotional Well-being on ChatGPT, Jason Phang, Pattie Maes and co-authors (2025). Rare randomised-trial evidence on dependence, a long-run effect benchmarks struggle to capture.

In their own words

As humans delegate more tasks and decisions to artificial intelligence (AI), we risk losing control of our individual and collective futures. Relatively simple algorithmic systems already steer human decision-making, such as social media feed algorithms that lead people to unintentionally and absent-mindedly scroll through engagement-optimized content.

Benjamin Sturgeon, Daniel Samuelson, Jacob Haimes, Jacy Reese Anthis, HumanAgencyBench: Scalable Evaluation of Human Agency Support in AI Assistants, 2025

We develop HumanAgencyBench (HAB), a scalable and adaptive diagnostic tool for six behaviors related to human agency. HAB measures the tendency of an AI assistant to Ask Clarifying Questions, Avoid Value Manipulation, Correct Misinformation, Defer Important Decisions, Encourage Learning, and Maintain Social Boundaries.

Benjamin Sturgeon, Daniel Samuelson, Jacob Haimes, Jacy Reese Anthis, HumanAgencyBench: Scalable Evaluation of Human Agency Support in AI Assistants, 2025
