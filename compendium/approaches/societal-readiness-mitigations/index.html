Society: defence and who holds power

Societal resilience

Hardening society's defences

Make society harder to harm with AI-enabled attacks, by building defences in the areas AI most helps attackers, above all computer security and biological threats, using AI tools among other means.

Open in the compendium

This approach builds society's defences against AI-enabled attacks before dangerous AI capabilities spread, above all in the two domains where AI is expected to boost attackers most: computer security and biological threats. Part of it uses AI itself, for example to find and patch software flaws in critical infrastructure or to spot pandemics early; Google DeepMind's April 2025 safety approach names this as one of its misuse mitigations. It complements efforts to stop models from helping attackers, on the view that such prevention will sometimes fail, and aims to keep the cost of causing severe harm high even if bad actors get dangerous capabilities. The work is concrete and funded, through government prize challenges, lab programmes, philanthropic grants and fellowships.

How it works

Most safety measures act on the model: they remove capabilities, train refusals or filter outputs. Societal readiness acts on the world instead. DeepMind's paper describes it as using "the very AI capabilities that pose risks" to shift the offence–defence balance toward security. Its examples include partnering with power utilities to patch vulnerabilities, building hardened software, early pandemic detection from open-source data or wastewater sequencing, and anti-scam and anti-misinformation tools.

The clearest working examples are in software security. In October 2025 Google DeepMind described CodeMender, an agent that writes and checks security patches; it had sent 72 fixes to open-source projects. OpenAI's Aardvark agent (October 2025) scans code and proposes patches. In April 2026 Anthropic launched Project Glasswing: it gave a new model with strong flaw-finding ability to about a dozen launch partners and over 40 other organisations that maintain critical software, with up to $100 million in usage credits, before such abilities spread more widely.

In cyber security, the main lever is giving defenders AI tools that find and fix software flaws, before attackers get similar tools. The US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) ran the AI Cyber Challenge (AIxCC) from 2023 to 2025 with the health research agency ARPA-H. Teams built automated systems to secure open-source code that critical infrastructure depends on; DARPA then released several of the systems as open source. Frontier labs now run their own programmes, such as OpenAI's Cybersecurity Grant Program and its Trusted Access for Cyber scheme.

In biology, the emphasis is defence in depth: layers of protection that work whatever the pathogen is. One fellowship programme describes four pillars: protective equipment, buildings hardened against airborne disease, detection that does not depend on knowing the pathogen in advance, and fast medical countermeasures. Groups such as Blueprint Biosecurity work on parts of this, for example respirator stockpiles and ultraviolet air disinfection. The International Biosecurity and Biosafety Initiative for Science (IBBIS) builds free tools to screen DNA synthesis orders.

Talent programmes feed both domains. MATS, an AI safety research fellowship, runs a Biosecurity track and a Systems Security track (security for the infrastructure that advanced AI runs on). Pivotal Research launched a 15-week Frontier Biodefense Fellowship in London in 2026.

A broader version is "societal adaptation". A 2024 paper by Jamie Bernardi, Gabriel Mukobi, Hilary Greaves, Lennart Heim and Markus Anderljung frames it as reducing the harm from a given spread of a capability, through steps that avoid, defend against and remedy harmful uses.

Where research stands

Cyber defence is the most developed area, because AI's flaw-finding ability arrived early and defenders can use it directly. Labs now run defensive programmes, and governments fund resilience research. The UK AI Security Institute started a Societal Resilience research area in 2025 and has selected 20 projects for its Systemic AI Safety Grants, covering areas like healthcare, energy grids and financial markets.

Cyber defence has moved fastest. AIxCC finished at DEF CON in August 2025: finalist systems found 54 of the planted vulnerabilities and patched 43, and also found 18 real ones that were reported to maintainers. In April 2026 Anthropic reported a model that had found thousands of serious, previously unknown flaws, and committed up to $100 million in credits for defenders. In February 2026 OpenAI committed $10 million in API credits for cyber defence. The bottleneck has shifted from finding flaws to fixing them: open-source maintainers say the flood of AI-generated reports adds to an already heavy load.

Bio defence is earlier and much smaller. A June 2026 post introducing the Frontier Biodefense Fellowship cites an estimate that around 160 people work full time on catastrophic biological risk, and perhaps fewer than 30 on defence in depth. Its authors expect a "window of vulnerability" of roughly 3 to 15 years, in which the ability to cause harm arrives before the ability to defend at scale. They also note this urgency could be overstated if AI helps defensive researchers first.

The main limits are speed and adoption. Defence often depends on many slow actors: CSET's modelling suggests getting patches installed, not writing them, is the bottleneck. DeepMind's paper treats structural risks, which often need new norms or institutions, as much harder for an AI developer to address and out of scope, so readiness work of that kind falls to others. Whether defenders can stay ahead is also unclear: Anthropic itself warned in April 2026 that defending the world's cyber infrastructure "might take years" while capabilities advance in months.

Key results

  • Google DeepMind lists societal readiness among its misuse mitigations, with example interventions in biosecurity, cyber defence and information defence. Rohin Shah, et al. (Google DeepMind), 2025
  • A framework for societal adaptation identifies interventions that avoid, defend against and remedy harmful AI uses, illustrated with election manipulation, cyberterrorism and loss of control. Jamie Bernardi, Gabriel Mukobi, Hilary Greaves, Lennart Heim, Markus Anderljung, 2024
  • Google DeepMind's CodeMender agent upstreamed 72 security fixes to open-source projects in its first six months, some in codebases of 4.5 million lines. Google DeepMind, 2025
  • Anthropic reported that an unreleased model found thousands of high-severity flaws, including in every major operating system and web browser, and gave defenders early access with up to $100 million in credits. Anthropic, 2026
  • Modelling suggests a five-fold speed-up in patch adoption would cut the peak number of exposed vulnerabilities by about 25 percent, a larger gain than faster patch writing. Andrew J. Lohn, Krystal A. Jackson (CSET), 2022
  • DARPA's AI Cyber Challenge offered a cumulative $29.5 million in prizes to teams building AI systems that secure open-source critical-infrastructure code. DARPA, 2023
  • In the AIxCC final, competitor systems found 54 planted vulnerabilities and patched 43 across 54 million lines of code, and discovered 18 real vulnerabilities; four of the seven systems were released open source. DARPA, 2025
  • OpenAI shifted its cyber grant programme from a $1 million research fund to large-scale deployment for defenders, committing $10 million in API credits in February 2026. OpenAI, 2026
  • Biodefence is severely understaffed (about 160 people full time on catastrophic biological risk, perhaps fewer than 30 on defence in depth), which motivated a dedicated fellowship. Tobias H (Pivotal Research), 2026

  • How to get fixes deployed across thousands of slow-moving organisations, not just written. source
  • How fast large-scale risks are emerging as AI spreads into critical sectors, which the UK AI Security Institute is only beginning to measure. source
  • How to share strong defensive capabilities with defenders without leaking them to attackers. source
  • How under-resourced open-source maintainers can fix the volume of flaws that AI tools now find. source
  • Whether the AI-and-biology offence–defence balance really favours attackers, or whether defensive researchers gain first. source
  • How to scale pathogen-agnostic protection such as respirators and air treatment to whole populations in a crisis. source

Criticisms

  • Defences rarely get built before the harm arrives; even after Covid-19 there was no massive investment in fast pre-emptive defences. Eliezer Yudkowsky (Machine Intelligence Research Institute)
  • If the most capable AIs are misaligned, the defensive tools would come from them, and they could fail to produce them or produce poor versions. Abram Demski
  • The defensive window is short: defending the world's cyber infrastructure might take years, while frontier capabilities advance within months. Anthropic
  • AI flaw-finding without matching fixing capacity shifts the burden onto volunteer maintainers, and $4 million in donations is small next to the problem. Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols (The Register), quoting cURL lead developer Daniel Stenberg
  • Routing open-source security through a proprietary model risks locking open-source projects into one company's tools. Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols (The Register)
  • The case for urgent biodefence may be overstated if frontier models help defensive researchers before they help bad actors. Pivotal Research, in its own fellowship rationale

Status

Funded, domain-scoped in cyber and bio defence. DeepMind names structural risks as one of four risk areas, then states "we do not address structural risks, since they require bespoke approaches."

In their own words

Societal readiness mitigations use AI systems to harden societal defenses; for example, it aims to prepare for AI cyber-offense capabilities by enabling rapid patching of vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure. Such mitigations can help ensure that even with access to dangerous capabilities, bad actors would not be able to cause severe harm.

Rohin Shah et al. (Google DeepMind), An Approach to Technical AGI Safety and Security, 2025

AIxCC will ask competitors to design novel AI systems to secure this critical code and will award a cumulative $29.5 million in prizes to teams with the best systems, including $7 million in prizes to small businesses to empower entrepreneurial innovation during the initial phase of AIxCC.

DARPA, AI Cyber Challenge (AIxCC) program page, 2023
