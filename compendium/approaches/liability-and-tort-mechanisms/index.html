Holding AI companies to account

Constraining

Liability and tort mechanisms

Make developers legally bear the cost of catastrophic AI harm.

Open in the compendium

Liability means that a company whose AI causes harm must pay for it through the courts. Tort law is the part of civil law that lets injured people sue for compensation. The safety idea is that if developers expect to pay for the harm their systems cause, they will spend more on making them safe before release, without a regulator having to write detailed rules first.

How it works

Under ordinary negligence law, a victim must show the developer failed to take reasonable care. Under product liability, a victim must show the product was defective. Under strict liability, the developer pays for harm whatever care it took; courts already apply this to 'abnormally dangerous activities' such as blasting. Proposals for AI pick among these rules. In September 2025, for example, US Senators Durbin and Hawley introduced the AI LEAD Act, which would classify AI systems as products so that product liability claims can be brought against their makers.

The hard case is catastrophe. Gabriel Weil argues that most of the expected harm from advanced AI lies in disasters so large that no one could be paid compensation afterwards. His fix is to 'pull forward' that liability: when a system causes a smaller, compensable harm that reveals catastrophic risk (a near miss), courts would add punitive damages sized to the risk the developer ran. He pairs this with other measures, including strict liability and a requirement to carry liability insurance for training and deploying advanced AI systems.

Courts are already testing the basic questions. In May 2025 a US federal judge in Garcia v. Character Technologies, a wrongful-death suit over a chatbot, refused to dismiss the case on free-speech grounds and let product liability and negligence claims go forward.

Where research stands

This is an active area of legal scholarship and early lawmaking, with no settled rule in any major jurisdiction. In the United States, the AI LEAD Act (S.2937) was referred to the Senate Judiciary Committee on 29 September 2025; Congress's official bill-status record (last updated 10 August 2026) shows no action since, and its latest recorded event is a new cosponsor on 19 March 2026. State bills such as Rhode Island's H 8052, introduced on 27 February 2026, which would make AI developers liable for certain injuries 'regardless of the degree of care they exercised', drew organised industry opposition; Rhode Island Current reported on 15 July 2026 that it failed in that year's session. In July 2026 the Future of Life Institute published model legislation making developers of models trained with more than 10^26 operations strictly liable for catastrophic harm, defined to include 100 or more deaths or $1 billion or more in property damage.

In the European Union, the Commission announced on 11 February 2025 that it would withdraw its proposed AI Liability Directive. The revised Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 instead treats software, including AI, as a product; member states must transpose it by 9 December 2026, and it applies to products placed on the market after that date. Known weak points remain: proving which design choice caused a given harm, and the fact that the worst outcomes may leave no one to sue.

Key results

  • Most expected harm from advanced AI lies in catastrophes where compensation is not feasible, so ordinary tort law under-deters; punitive damages in near-miss cases, strict liability and mandatory insurance could close the gap. Gabriel Weil, 2024
  • Existing US tort law already reaches frontier developers through a general duty of reasonable care, but it struggles with causation, catastrophic harms, slow case law and technical complexity, so it should complement regulation, not replace it. Matthew van der Merwe, Ketan Ramakrishnan, Markus Anderljung, 2024
  • A US federal court let product liability and negligence claims against a chatbot company go forward in May 2025, rejecting at that stage the argument that chatbot output is protected speech. Tech Justice Law Project, 2026
  • The EU's revised Product Liability Directive covers stand-alone software as a product and presumes defect or causation where technical complexity makes proof excessively difficult; it applies to products placed on the market after 9 December 2026. Gibson Dunn, 2026
  • The European Commission dropped its proposed AI Liability Directive in its 2025 work programme, published 11 February 2025, saying it would assess whether to table another proposal. The Stack, 2025

Criticisms

  • Strict liability for a general-purpose technology is 'unprecedented and inappropriate', would chill open-source releases, and removes the reward for taking care; a negligence standard would balance accountability with innovation. TechNet (testimony on Rhode Island H 8052)
  • Judges and state legislators lack the expertise and uniformity to manage AI harms, and relying on tort law now is premature; 'both existing tort law and new state-level regulations are flawed solutions at this stage.'. Kevin Frazier (AI Frontiers)
  • Tort law acts after harm occurs and handles catastrophic, hard-to-trace harms poorly, so it cannot be the only governance tool. Matthew van der Merwe, Ketan Ramakrishnan, Markus Anderljung (Lawfare)

Who works on it

  • Institute for Law & AI: Its non-resident senior fellow Gabriel Weil developed the main proposal for liability against catastrophic AI risk. Tort Law as a Tool for Mitigating Catastrophic Risk from Artificial Intelligence (2024)
  • Future of Life Institute: Published model legislation making frontier developers strictly liable for catastrophic harm. Holding AI Companies Accountable for Catastrophic Harm (2026)
  • Center for Humane Technology: Proposed a framework combining product liability with product-safety rules for the riskiest AI systems. A Framework for Incentivizing Responsible Artificial Intelligence Development and Use (2024)
  • Tech Justice Law Project: Represents the family, with the Social Media Victims Law Center, in Garcia v. Character Technologies, a test case on whether chatbots are products under liability law. Garcia v. Character Technologies (case page) (2026)
  • Alliance for Secure AI Action: Campaigns for treating AI as a product under liability law and backs the federal AI LEAD Act. Issues
  • Ada Lovelace Institute: Analyses UK regulatory options, including liability that reaches developers and hosting platforms, not only adopters. Making sense of the current options for AI regulation in the UK (2026)
  • Gladstone AI: Its action plan for the US State Department recommends a criminal and civil liability regime for advanced AI. An Action Plan to increase the safety and security of advanced AI (2024)

Start reading

  • Tort Law and Frontier AI Governance, Matthew van der Merwe, Ketan Ramakrishnan, Markus Anderljung (2024). Balanced explainer of what US tort law can and cannot do for frontier AI.
  • Your AI Breaks It? You Buy It., Gabriel Weil (2024). Short, non-technical case for making AI companies pay.
  • Tort Law as a Tool for Mitigating Catastrophic Risk from Artificial Intelligence, Gabriel Weil (2024). The core academic proposal: strict liability, punitive damages for near misses, insurance.
  • We're Not Ready for AI Liability, Kevin Frazier (2025). The strongest recent argument against leaning on tort law now.
  • Catastrophic Liability: Managing Systemic Risks in Frontier AI Development, Aidan Kierans, Kaley Rittichier, Utku Sonsayar, Avijit Ghosh (2025). Draws on nuclear, aviation and healthcare liability regimes.

In their own words

Thankfully, just such a policy is available: Make AI companies pay for the harm they cause. The principle is quite simple. If people and companies don't need to bear the costs of the harms and risks they generate, they feel more free to engage in risky behavior.

Gabriel Weil (Noema Magazine), Your AI Breaks It? You Buy It., 2024

... most of the expected harm from AI systems comes in truly catastrophic scenarios where compensation would not be feasible. To address this, I propose a form of punitive damages designed to pull forward this expected liability into cases of practically compensable harm.

Gabriel Weil, Tort Law as a Tool for Mitigating Catastrophic Risk from Artificial Intelligence, 2024
