Preparing for warning shots

Prepare responses in advance so a non-catastrophic AI incident can drive lasting action.

Open in the compendium

A "warning shot" is an AI incident or demonstration that is alarming but not catastrophic, such as a visible case of a model acting against its developers, or serious misuse. This approach prepares in advance so that such an event leads to sound, lasting action instead of panic, denial or a race. The premise is that governments rarely act on abstract risks, but often act after a concrete event, and the first response tends to stick.

How it works

Preparation has several parts. The first is detection: evaluations, incident reporting, compute and deployment monitoring, and whistleblower channels, so that a warning shot is noticed and documented. A June 2026 proposal by Mark Kagach, Elias Schlie, Thomas Van Damme and Justin Shovelain calls this a "detection stack". It suggests the network of AI Security Institutes could be its backbone, since the UK institute's founding mission was to "minimise surprise" from rapid AI advances.

The second part is interpretation. Communications research cited in the same proposal suggests an event is framed as either a one-off ("one bad model") or a systemic problem within the first 48 to 72 hours. So the advice is to prepare explanations, trusted voices and relationships before any event. The third part is ready policy: "shovel-ready" proposals and pre-agreed responses. In a February 2026 post, Noah Birnbaum suggests labs or governments commit in advance to specific responses to specific classes of event. This is close to the "if-then commitments" Holden Karnofsky described in 2024: if a model shows capability X, mitigations Y must be in place.

A concrete analogy used in this work is a hypothetical proof that Covid-19 came from a lab. The same event could produce a global halt to risky research or a secretive race. Which happens depends on who is ready first with a credible reading and a concrete ask.

Where research stands

This is an early idea, set out mostly in a few 2025 and 2026 essays. Its premise has grown more credible. Early AI safety arguments expected few useful warnings before catastrophe. Birnbaum argues that frequent model replacement, uneven ("jagged") capabilities and slower take-off make partial, visible failures more likely. Lab experiments showing deception and scheming have already shaped public debate.

The historical evidence is sobering. In September 2025 Ben Norman reviewed cases including Chernobyl, ozone depletion and Covid-19. He found that warning shots led to international agreements mainly when institutional capacity, clear attribution, cross-border harm, aligned political incentives and ready-made solutions were already in place. He argues that for frontier AI, unclear attribution and geopolitical competition work against such agreements. Practical work is still thin: Birnbaum writes that almost no one is working on what to do in anticipation of a warning shot. The related building blocks, such as incident reporting and if-then commitments, are covered by other approaches.

Key results

  • Changes in how AI is built (frequent retraining, jagged capabilities, possible slow take-off) make non-catastrophic warning shots more plausible than early arguments assumed, yet almost no one prepares for them. Noah Birnbaum, 2026
  • Historical warning shots led to international agreements mainly when institutional capacity, clear attribution, transnational harm, political alignment and available solutions already existed. Ben Norman (The Option Space), 2025
  • Turning a warning shot into international cooperation needs a detection stack, anti-"frog-boiling" communication, ready policy blueprints and a plan for the first 72 hours. Mark Kagach, Elias Schlie, Thomas Van Damme, Justin Shovelain, 2026
  • If-then commitments ("If an AI model has capability X, risk mitigations Y must be in place") let actors agree responses in advance without agreeing on whether risks are imminent. Holden Karnofsky (Carnegie Endowment for International Peace), 2024

Open problems

  • How to classify likely warning shots and predict how the public and policymakers will react to each type. source
  • How to avoid gradual numbing when many small, incremental alarms arrive instead of one clear event. source
  • How to build the institutional preconditions for international agreement before a crisis, given that AI lacks most of them. source
  • How to balance acting on each minor signal (and risking "crying wolf") against waiting for a major event (and acting too late). source

Criticisms

  • There will never be a clear fire alarm for AGI: signs will always be arguable, so no event will create the common knowledge that action is now due. Eliezer Yudkowsky (Machine Intelligence Research Institute)
  • Focusing on warning shots rewards exaggerating weak signals, and the safety community has a limited budget of alarms before it becomes the boy who cried wolf. Zephaniah Roe, commenting on Birnbaum's post
  • Governance strategies should work without any warning shot; over-reliance on one is a common failure mode. Kagach, Schlie, Van Damme and Shovelain

Who works on it

  • UK AI Security Institute: Founded with a mission to minimise surprise from rapid AI advances; proposed as a backbone for warning-shot detection. Introducing the AI Safety Institute (2023)
  • Carnegie Endowment for International Peace (Carnegie California): Published the primer on if-then commitments, a way to agree responses before evidence arrives. If-Then Commitments for AI Risk Reduction (2024)

In their own words

Early AI safety arguments often assumed we wouldn't get meaningful warning shots (a non-existential public display of misalignment) before catastrophic misalignment, meaning things would go from "seems fine" to "we lose" pretty quickly. ... Preparing for warning shots-especially ambiguous ones-could be a high-leverage and neglected area of AI Safety.

Noah Birnbaum, Preparing for a Warning Shot, 2026

The practical implication is that warning-shot preparation cannot just mean "better messaging after the event." It requires pre-building policy options, coalitions, legitimacy, and channels to decision-makers. ... Preparing to catch warning shots requires a detection stack: capability evaluations (especially labs entering an intelligence explosion), alignment evaluations, incident reporting, compute and deployment monitoring, whistleblower channels, and more.

Mark Kagach, Elias Schlie, Thomas Van Damme, Justin Shovelain, Preparing for Warning Shots to Catalyze International Cooperation on AGI Risks, 2026
