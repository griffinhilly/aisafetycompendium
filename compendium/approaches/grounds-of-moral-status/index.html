Grounds of moral status

Work out what capacity earns a being moral consideration: sentience, cognitive sophistication or something else.

Open in the compendium

This is the philosophical question of what feature makes a being count morally for its own sake. Candidate answers include the capacity to feel pleasure and pain (sentience), sophisticated thinking such as rational agency, or simply having desires and goals. For AI safety the answer decides whether AI systems could ever be wronged, which in turn decides whether welfare limits should apply to how developers train, test, constrain and shut down models.

How it works

Philosophers set out candidate grounds and test them against cases. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry by Agnieszka Jaworska and Julie Tannenbaum sorts the main views into sophisticated cognitive capacities (such as autonomy or self-awareness), the potential to develop them, rudimentary capacities (such as sentience), species membership, and relationships. Each view is checked against hard cases. A view that grounds status only in rational agency, for example, struggles to explain why infants count. A further question is whether status comes in degrees: Shelly Kagan's How to Count Animals, more or less (2019) defends a hierarchy in which people count more than animals and some animals count more than others.

Applied to AI, the debate splits on which ground matters. If sentience is the ground, the question becomes whether an AI system can have conscious experience, which turns on theories of consciousness. Simon Goldstein and Cameron Domenico Kirk-Giannini argue instead that wellbeing may not require consciousness: on desire-based theories, a language agent (a language model wrapped in memory and planning tools) that has beliefs and desires could already have wellbeing. Jeff Sebo and Robert Long take a third route that is built for uncertainty: if a being has a non-negligible chance of being conscious, we should extend it moral consideration.

Where research stands

This is a mature field in philosophy, and its application to AI grew quickly from 2023. The main academic outputs are frameworks for reasoning under uncertainty, not settled answers. A 2023 report led by Patrick Butlin and Robert Long derived 'indicator properties' from scientific theories of consciousness and concluded that no current AI system appears conscious, but that no obvious technical barrier stops one being built. In January 2026 Rethink Priorities published initial results from a probabilistic model that combines 13 stances on consciousness; it found the evidence is against 2024 language models being conscious but not decisively, and the authors urge particular caution in interpreting the probabilities it generates.

Disagreement remains deep. Anil Seth argues that consciousness may depend on being a living organism, which would make conscious AI unlikely on current trajectories. Joanna Bryson argues that robots should not be treated as persons, and Abeba Birhane and Jelle van Dijk argue that robots are not the kind of thing that could hold rights and that the rights debate diverts attention from harms to people. No theory of moral status commands consensus, so most practical proposals in this area rely on decision-making under uncertainty rather than on a settled view.

Key results

  • Some possible AI systems would not differ from humans in any morally relevant respect, so they would deserve similar moral consideration; the authors propose that AI designers avoid building systems whose moral status is unclear. Eric Schwitzgebel and Mara Garza, 2015
  • Indicator properties drawn from leading scientific theories of consciousness suggest no current AI system is conscious, but no obvious technical barrier prevents building one that meets the indicators. Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, Eric Elmoznino, Yoshua Bengio, Jonathan Birch and others, 2023
  • If humans have a duty to consider beings with a non-negligible chance of being conscious, and some AI systems will have that chance by 2030, then humans have a duty to extend moral consideration to some AI systems by 2030 and to start preparing now. Jeff Sebo and Robert Long, 2025
  • Many theories of belief and desire, combined with leading theories of wellbeing, imply that some existing language agents may have wellbeing, even if they are not phenomenally conscious. Simon Goldstein and Cameron Domenico Kirk-Giannini, 2025
  • A Bayesian model across 13 views of consciousness gave 2024 language models a median probability of about 0.08, against about 0.47 for chickens and 0.85 for humans, under a starting prior of 1 in 6; the authors call the model a proof of concept. Derek Shiller, Laura Duffy, Arvo Muñoz Morán, Adrià Moret, Chris Percy, Hayley Clatterbuck (Rethink Priorities), 2026

Open problems

  • Whether consciousness is necessary for moral status, or whether desires and agency are enough, is unresolved, and the answer changes which AI systems are candidates. source
  • Whether computation alone can produce consciousness, or whether it needs a living substrate, is disputed between functionalists and biological naturalists. source
  • Even if AI systems have some moral standing, it is unclear how much they count and in what ways, for example whether status comes in degrees. source
  • Probability estimates of AI consciousness depend heavily on priors and on how competing theories are weighted, so absolute numbers are not yet reliable. source

Criticisms

  • Consciousness may depend on being a living organism, so computation alone may not suffice; attributing minds to machines too readily leads us to overestimate them and underestimate ourselves. Anil Seth
  • Robots are owned artefacts whose goals humans set; describing them as persons dehumanises real people and leads to poor decisions about resources and responsibility. Joanna Bryson
  • Robots are not the kind of entity that could hold rights, and the rights debate neglects urgent harms such as machine bias, labour exploitation and loss of privacy. Abeba Birhane and Jelle van Dijk
  • Extending moral consideration on the basis of a non-negligible chance of consciousness is premature and dangerous, because belief in AI consciousness will push people to advocate for AI rights. Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft AI)

Who works on it

  • NYU Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy: NYU research centre, home of Jeff Sebo's work, on the consciousness, moral status and legal status of nonhuman minds, including AI systems. Moral consideration for AI systems by 2030 (2025)
  • Rethink Priorities (AI Cognition Initiative): Built the Digital Consciousness Model, a probabilistic tool for weighing evidence about AI consciousness across theories. Initial results of the Digital Consciousness Model (2026)
  • London School of Economics and Political Science (Department of Philosophy, Logic and Scientific Method): Jonathan Birch's open-access book sets out a precautionary framework for beings whose sentience is uncertain, including AI. The Edge of Sentience: Risk and Precaution in Humans, Other Animals, and AI (2024)
  • Sussex Centre for Consciousness Science, University of Sussex: Anil Seth's target article argues for biological naturalism and against computation as sufficient for consciousness. Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism (2025)

Status

Mature academic field. Sebo's criterion is deliberately minimal and inclusive.

Start reading

  • The Grounds of Moral Status (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), Agnieszka Jaworska and Julie Tannenbaum (2021). The standard survey of candidate grounds and of whether status comes in degrees.
  • Digital Minds in 2025: A Year in Review, Bradford Saad, Lucius Caviola and Will Millership (2025). A broad map of the papers, organisations and public debates on AI moral status in 2025.
  • Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness, Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and others (2023). The main attempt to turn theories of consciousness into checkable indicators for AI.
  • Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism, Anil K. Seth (2025). The strongest sceptical case from consciousness science, published with open peer commentary.
  • How to Count Animals, More or Less (2016 Uehiro Lectures in Practical Ethics), Shelly Kagan (2016). The lectures behind Kagan's 2019 book; they defend a hierarchy of moral status, a useful contrast to equal-status views.

In their own words

An entity has moral status if and only if it matters (to some degree) from the moral point of view for its own sake. More specifically, one's moral status consists in there being certain moral reasons or requirements, for one's own sake, for how one is to be treated.

Agnieszka Jaworska and Julie Tannenbaum, The Grounds of Moral Status (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), 2021

Accounts differ on what it is about the individual that grounds or confers moral status and to what degree. ... On some views, the capacity to experience pleasure or pain (sentience) is a prerequisite of having interests and this explains why sentience is a ground of moral status (Singer 1993, p. 57).

Agnieszka Jaworska and Julie Tannenbaum, The Grounds of Moral Status (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), 2021
