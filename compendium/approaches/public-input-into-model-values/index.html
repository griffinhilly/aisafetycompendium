Public input into model values (collective constitutions, assemblies)

Run deliberation and voting with members of the public to set the principles a model is trained to follow.

Open in the compendium

This approach asks members of the public, not only company staff, to help decide the values and rules an AI model is trained to follow. Organisers run surveys, votes or small deliberative meetings, turn the results into written principles or rubrics, and then use those to train or evaluate the model. It matters for safety because a model's values are set by a small group of developers today, and public input is one way to make those choices more legitimate, more visible and less tied to one company's views.

How it works

Most projects follow three steps: ask people, turn their answers into a usable target, and train or test a model against it. In Collective Constitutional AI (Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project, October 2023), about 1,000 Americans used Polis, an open-source online platform for group deliberation, to propose and vote on rules for an AI system. Participants wrote 1,127 statements and cast 38,252 votes. Polis found two opinion groups. The organisers kept the statements that passed a consensus threshold within both groups and turned them into a 'constitution' (a written list of principles) and trained a model on it with Constitutional AI, a method in which the model critiques and revises its own answers against those principles. The public constitution overlapped roughly 50% in concepts and values with Anthropic's own. It put more weight on objectivity, impartiality and accessibility, for example a principle that the model should be adaptable and accessible to people with disabilities.

Other projects collect input in different ways. OpenAI (August 2025) showed about 1,000 people in 19 countries four possible answers to value-sensitive prompts and asked them to rank the answers and explain why. It then compared their rankings with a model that ranked the same answers by the rules in OpenAI's Model Spec, the public document that sets out how its models should behave. Where the two disagreed, researchers drafted changes to the Spec. The Meaning Alignment Institute's 'Democratic Fine-Tuning' uses a chatbot to interview each person about the value behind their view and writes it up as a 'values card'. Participants then vote on which value is wiser in a given situation, and the votes form a 'moral graph' meant to serve as a training target.

Smaller, deeper formats also exist. Google DeepMind's STELA study held focus groups with people from four historically marginalised communities in the United States and coded the discussions into a 38-rule ruleset. Meta, with Stanford's Deliberative Democracy Lab, ran a 'Deliberative Polling' forum in which 1,545 participants in Brazil, Germany, Spain and the United States answered survey questions about AI chatbots before and after deliberating together.

Where research stands

The approach is at the pilot stage, with one small production use. Since 2023 a handful of organisations have run processes with between a few dozen and 15,000 participants each. In September 2025 OpenAI's Model Spec changelog recorded 'important clarifications gathered from public input via a Collective Alignment process'. In January 2026 OpenAI released CoVal, a dataset of crowd-written rubrics (lists of what a good answer should do or avoid) drawn from the same study. The Collective Intelligence Project has run its 'Global Dialogues' survey about every few months since November 2024, each time with roughly 1,000 to 1,300 people from 63 to 80 countries. The field is small: most of the work comes from the Collective Intelligence Project, OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google DeepMind and the Meaning Alignment Institute, plus academic groups studying the methods.

What works: people from different political camps often agree on how a model should behave even when they disagree on the underlying issue, and most participants say they feel well represented. What does not yet work: the measured effect on model behaviour is small. Anthropic's public-constitution model differed from its standard model mainly on one measure, lower social bias. OpenAI found that participants already agreed with its Spec-based rankings about 80% of the time, and in January 2026 its crowd rubrics picked the answer a new rater preferred a little over 60% of the time, against 50% by chance. Every project also relies on many judgement calls by the organisers: who takes part, how answers are moderated and merged, and how they are turned into rules. The company keeps the final say. OpenAI did not adopt crowd preferences for more tailored political content, citing the risk of large-scale political targeting.

Key results

  • A model trained on a constitution written by about 1,000 Americans showed lower bias across nine social dimensions than a model trained on Anthropic's own constitution, and matched it on language, maths, helpfulness and harmlessness tests. Saffron Huang, Divya Siddarth, Liane Lovitt, Thomas I. Liao, Esin Durmus, Alex Tamkin, Deep Ganguli, 2024
  • About 1,000 people in 19 countries agreed with a Model Spec-based ranker about 80% of the time; disagreements clustered on political content, sexual or graphic content, and critiques of pseudoscience or conspiracies, and some led to Model Spec changes. Tyna Eloundou, Mitchell Gordon, Eddie Zhang, Sandhini Agarwal (OpenAI), 2025
  • In a test of Moral Graph Elicitation with a representative sample of 500 Americans on deliberately divisive prompts such as abortion advice, 89.1% of participants said they felt well represented by the process. Oliver Klingefjord, Ryan Lowe, Joe Edelman, 2024
  • AI-assisted 'collective dialogues' with over 1,500 US participants, run in two weeks for about $10,000, produced guidelines for AI assistants on medical advice, vaccine information, and wars and conflicts that won 75-81% support overall and at least 70-75% across age, gender, religion, race, education and party. Andrew Konya, Lisa Schirch, Colin Irwin, Aviv Ovadya, 2023
  • Rules drawn from focus groups with 44 people from four marginalised US communities stressed impartiality, factual sourcing and intellectual humility, while developer rulesets for Claude, Sparrow and Llama 2 focused far more on avoiding harmful content. Stevie Bergman, Nahema Marchal, John Mellor, Shakir Mohamed, Iason Gabriel, William Isaac, 2024
  • In a study of 15,000 people across five countries, humans varied far more in their preferences than the answers of 21 leading language models did, which suggests that one aggregated target can hide real disagreement. Lily Hong Zhang, Smitha Milli, Karen Jusko and others (Meta, New York University, Ecole polytechnique), 2025

Open problems

  • How to combine conflicting input from many people into one consistent target is unsolved; social choice theory (the maths of voting and aggregation) offers tools but no settled answer. source
  • Turning public answers into training rules needs many discretionary steps, and OpenAI did not check its final rule proposals back with participants, so the output may not match what people meant. source
  • Online recruitment misses people across the digital divide and skews toward people who are optimistic about AI, and views can shift from day to day, so it is unclear who should take part and how often to repeat the process. source
  • Standard alignment training may flatten the range of views a model expresses, so it is open whether public input should set one default, several defaults, or a model that can be steered to different value systems. source
  • Current evaluations detect only small differences between publicly aligned and developer-aligned models, so better tests are needed to show whether public input changes behaviour that matters. source

Criticisms

  • OpenAI's Democratic Inputs programme rested on shared but largely unspoken assumptions: that participation must scale, that its object is a single model, that it should yield consensus principles, and that the public must be representative. The authors call for other forms of participation in AI, perhaps not run by tech companies. David Moats and Chandrima Ganguly
  • A foundation model is built to be used almost everywhere, so affected communities cannot meaningfully shape it; participation works better at layers closer to specific domains and uses. Harini Suresh, Emily Tseng, Meg Young, Mary L. Gray, Emma Pierson, Karen Levy
  • The developer keeps the final say: crowd proposals pass through internal review, changes to the highest-priority rules are least likely to be adopted, and a process with many automated steps may not deliver the legitimacy it aims for. OpenAI (self-assessment of its own study)
  • Participation can slide into 'participation-washing'; the machine-learning community needs to watch for exploitative and extractive forms of community involvement and move away from approaches meant to scale across every context. Mona Sloane, Emanuel Moss, Olaitan Awomolo, Laura Forlano

Who works on it

  • Collective Intelligence Project: Ran 'Alignment Assemblies' (public input processes tied to AI decisions) in 2023-24, starting in March 2023, and runs the recurring Global Dialogues survey of roughly 1,000 to 1,300 people from 63 to 80 countries per round. Global Dialogues (2026)
  • Anthropic: Partnered with the Collective Intelligence Project to source a constitution from about 1,000 Americans and trained and evaluated a model on it. Collective Constitutional AI: Aligning a Language Model with Public Input (2023)
  • OpenAI (Collective Alignment team): Surveyed about 1,000 people in 19 countries, fed resulting clarifications into the September 2025 Model Spec, and released the CoVal dataset of crowd-written rubrics in January 2026. CoVal: Learning values-aware rubrics from the crowd (2026)
  • OpenAI: Launched ten $100,000 grants in May 2023 to fund experiments in democratic processes for deciding what rules AI systems should follow (Democratic Inputs to AI). Democratic inputs to AI (2023)
  • Meaning Alignment Institute: Developed Democratic Fine-Tuning and Moral Graph Elicitation, which interview people about their values and build a 'moral graph' as an alignment target. OpenAI x DFT: The First Moral Graph (2023)
  • Meta: Ran a deliberative Community Forum on principles for AI chatbots in four countries, and its researchers released the Community Alignment preference dataset from five countries. Leading the Way in Governance Innovation With Community Forums on AI (2024)
  • Stanford Deliberative Democracy Lab: Designed and analysed the Deliberative Polling forum on AI chatbots with 1,545 participants in Brazil, Germany, Spain and the United States. Meta Community Forum Results Analysis [April 2024] (2024)
  • Behavioural Insights Team: Partnered with Meta and Stanford's Deliberative Democracy Lab on the Community Forum on generative AI chatbots, held in October 2023, with results released in April 2024. Leading the Way in Governance Innovation With Community Forums on AI (2024)
  • Google DeepMind: Developed STELA, a community-centred method that turns focus-group deliberation into rules for language models. STELA: a community-centred approach to norm elicitation for AI alignment (2024)
  • Ministry of Digital Affairs (Taiwan): Partnered with the Collective Intelligence Project from May 2023 on alignment assemblies, including deliberative workshops in August and September 2023 and, in March 2024, an online citizens' assembly on using AI to promote information integrity, with participants chosen by stratified random sampling. Alignment Assemblies (2024)
  • Remesh: Provides the deliberation and consensus-building platform used in the Collective Intelligence Project's Global Dialogues. Global Dialogues (2025)

Status

Pilots, now feeding into one lab's published model rules. In Collective Constitutional AI about 1,000 Americans cast 38,252 votes on Polis. The public constitution overlapped about 50% with Anthropic's own. On 27 August 2025 OpenAI reported input from about 1,000 people living in 19 countries; its Model Spec of 12 September 2025 adds clarifications from that process.

Start reading

  • Collective Constitutional AI: Aligning a Language Model with Public Input, Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project (2023). The clearest end-to-end example, from public vote to trained model, with frank notes on what was hard.
  • Collective alignment: public input on our Model Spec, OpenAI (2025). Shows how a lab turns crowd rankings into changes to a deployed behaviour document, and which changes it refused.
  • Democratising AI: Multiple Meanings, Goals, and Methods, Elizabeth Seger, Aviv Ovadya, Ben Garfinkel, Divya Siddarth, Allan Dafoe (2023). Separates the different things 'democratising AI' can mean and argues that democratising governance is the key one.
  • Social Choice Should Guide AI Alignment in Dealing with Diverse Human Feedback, Vincent Conitzer, Rachel Freedman, Jobst Heitzig and others (2024). Explains why combining many people's input is a voting-theory problem and what that field can offer.
  • A Roadmap to Pluralistic Alignment, Taylor Sorensen, Jared Moore, Jillian Fisher and others (2024). Defines ways a model can reflect many views instead of one, and how to measure them.
  • What are human values, and how do we align AI to them?, Oliver Klingefjord, Ryan Lowe, Joe Edelman (2024). A worked alternative to voting on rules: elicit values, then let people judge which are wiser.

Choices that are consequential for all of us are already being made, from how and when to release models, what constitutes appropriate risk, and how to determine underlying principles for model behavior. By default, these decisions fall to a small percentage of those likely to be affected.

Collective Intelligence Project, Alignment Assemblies, 2023
