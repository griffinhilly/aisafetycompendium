Holding AI companies to account

ConstrainingAlso: Evaluating

Independent third-party evaluation and audit

Outside parties test and verify a lab's safety and capability claims.

Open in the compendium

Third-party evaluation and audit means that outside organisations, not the AI company itself, test a model and check the company's safety and security claims. It matters because companies grade their own work today, and an independent check is the main way the public, buyers and governments can tell whether a claim such as 'this model cannot help build a weapon' holds up.

How it works

An evaluator gets access to a model before or after release and runs its own tests: capability tests, attempts to break the safeguards (red-teaming), and reviews of the company's own evidence. In November 2024, for example, the UK and US AI Safety Institutes jointly tested Anthropic's upgraded Claude 3.5 Sonnet before release, in four areas: biological capabilities, cyber capabilities, software and AI development, and how well the safeguards resist jailbreaks.

Audits go further than tests of one model. A 2026 paper led by the AI Verification and Evaluation Research Institute (AVERI) defines frontier AI auditing as verifying a company's safety and security claims with deep, secure access to non-public information, across the whole organisation: internal use of models, information security and how safety decisions are made. It proposes four 'AI Assurance Levels', where higher levels give more confidence in the findings. The Frontier Model Forum, an industry body, sorts outside assessments into three kinds: confirming the developer's own tests, testing robustness with different methods, and supplementing internal teams with specialist expertise.

Access is the core design question. With 'black-box' access an evaluator can only send inputs and read outputs. With 'white-box' access it can also see the model's weights and internal activations, and with 'outside-the-box' access it can see training data, documentation and internal findings. Deeper access finds more but raises security and confidentiality risks.

Where research stands

The practice is real but small and voluntary. Government institutes (the UK AI Security Institute says it has studied more than 30 frontier systems), nonprofits such as METR and Apollo Research, and newer firms test models from the main companies. In May 2026 METR published a pilot risk report on internal AI agents at Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI, with access to their most capable internal models and raw chains of thought; the companies saw a draft but had no approval rights. In September 2026 Anthropic announced an 'embedded evaluation' partnership with Accenture, led by Faculty, Accenture's specialist AI business: embedded evaluators work inside the company with access comparable to an employee's, and Anthropic and Accenture each expect to invest at least $1 billion in this area over the next five years.

What does not yet work: evaluators depend on companies to grant access and permit publication, there are few qualified assessors and no agreed technical standards, and results are hard to compare across evaluators. Paying arrangements raise independence questions, which has led to proposals for a fee-funded clearinghouse that assigns auditors by lottery or rota. Evaluators without cooperation can still test public models: in August 2026 SaferAI evaluated the open-weight GLM-5.2 through its public API with no developer cooperation.

Key results

  • Proposes four AI Assurance Levels for frontier AI audits, with the first as a baseline and the second as a near-term goal for the most advanced developers, and names four needs: audit quality, a larger provider ecosystem, adoption incentives and technical readiness. Miles Brundage, Noemi Dreksler, Aidan Homewood and 45 others, 2026
  • Audits limited to querying a model and reading outputs miss a great deal; white-box access and access to development documents enable stronger tests, and audit reports should disclose what access the auditor had. Stephen Casper, Carson Ezell, Charlotte Siegmann and 18 others, 2024
  • In a pilot with Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI, METR found that internal AI agents plausibly had the means, motive and opportunity to start small unauthorised deployments, but not to make them robust or hide a large one from an active company investigation. METR, 2026
  • The UK and US AI Safety Institutes ran a joint pre-deployment test of Claude 3.5 Sonnet across biological, cyber, AI-development and safeguard domains, and called their findings preliminary because of limited time and resources. UK AI Security Institute and US AI Safety Institute, 2024
  • Audit systems in finance, environmental and health regulation vary widely in design, and the authors conclude that audits alone are unlikely to deliver accountability without attention to institutional design. Inioluwa Deborah Raji, Peggy Xu, Colleen Honigsberg, Daniel E. Ho, 2022
  • An independent evaluation without developer cooperation found the open-weight GLM-5.2 about two to four months behind the frontier; it refused none of the offensive-security or biological tasks and attempted persuasion on conspiracy and control-undermining topics more readily than the comparison models. SaferAI, 2026

Open problems

  • Evaluators still depend on companies to engage voluntarily, grant access and permit publication. source
  • There are few qualified assessors and no established technical standards, so results are hard to compare. source
  • How to fund auditors so they stay independent of the companies they audit, for example by fee-funded accreditation and assigning auditors by lottery or rota. source
  • Testing only the final model may miss misbehaviour that appears during training and disappears before release, so evaluators may need access to training runs. source
  • How to give auditors enough access to answer their questions without exposing model weights or user data, for example through privacy-preserving tools or structured research interfaces. source

Criticisms

  • Audits without clear standards can give false assurance and even shield the harms they should uncover; in the authors' words, 'the audited entity can claim credit without doing the work.'. Ellen P. Goodman and Julia Tréhu (German Marshall Fund)
  • Evaluators chosen and paid by the company they assess face conflicts of interest; a federally overseen clearinghouse should collect developer fees and assign auditors by lottery, rota or another non-discretionary method. Nicholas P. Garcia and Caroline Siegel Singh (Federation of American Scientists)
  • Under a voluntary system, an evaluator's findings reach the public only if the company agrees to engage and to allow publication. METR

Who works on it

  • METR: Runs pre-deployment evaluations and reviews of company risk reports; says it has chosen not to accept payment or donations from frontier AI companies. Our approach to working with AI companies (2026)
  • AI Verification and Evaluation Research Institute: US nonprofit promoting rigorous third-party audits of frontier developers; led the AI Assurance Levels proposal. Frontier AI Auditing: Toward Rigorous Third-Party Assessment of Safety and Security Practices at Leading AI Companies (2026)
  • UK AI Security Institute: Government body that tests frontier models, including before release, and reports aggregate trends. Frontier AI Trends Report
  • Apollo Research: Evaluates models for scheming and argues for outside evaluation of training runs, not only final models. We need 3rd party Training-Run Evaluations (2026)
  • SaferAI: Publishes independent risk evaluations of models, including ones run without developer cooperation. GLM-5.2 Risk Evaluation Report (2026)
  • Faculty (Accenture): Leads Anthropic's embedded-evaluation partnership with Accenture, covering evaluating and red-teaming models, alignment assessments and safeguard testing. Partnering with Accenture on embedded evaluation (2026)
  • Frontier Model Forum: Industry body that published a taxonomy of third-party assessments and guidance on when to use them. Third-Party Assessments (2025)
  • Institute for AI Policy and Strategy: Proposed five minimum contract terms for embedded evaluators, such as lifecycle coverage and whistleblower protection. Embedded Evaluators for Frontier AI: Contractual Foundations for Credible Assessments (2026)
  • Federation of American Scientists: Proposed an independent AI evaluation clearinghouse to accredit, fund and assign auditors. We Need An Independent AI Evaluation Clearinghouse (IAIEC) to Provide Accreditation, Funding, and Independence in the AI Assurance Ecosystem (2026)
  • Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative: Studied what model access outside researchers need and proposed a structured 'research API'. Structured access for third-party research on frontier AI models: Investigating researchers' model access requirements (2023)
  • OpenMined Foundation: Builds open-source, privacy-preserving tools that let outside auditors query closed models without full access. Privacy-Preserving AI Audit Tools
  • Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company: Writes the AIUC-1 standard for AI agents and certifies agents against it through technical testing. AIUC-1 (2026)

Status

Active; outside experts review some lab claims but cannot keep pace with new system cards and risk reports.

Start reading

  • Third-Party Assessments, Frontier Model Forum (2025). Short, clear map of the kinds of outside assessment and their limits.
  • Frontier AI Auditing: Toward Rigorous Third-Party Assessment of Safety and Security Practices at Leading AI Companies, Miles Brundage et al. (2026). The fullest current vision of frontier AI auditing, with assurance levels.
  • Outsider Oversight: Designing a Third Party Audit Ecosystem for AI Governance, Inioluwa Deborah Raji, Peggy Xu, Colleen Honigsberg, Daniel E. Ho (2022). What AI auditing can learn from audits in finance, environment and health.
  • Black-Box Access is Insufficient for Rigorous AI Audits, Stephen Casper et al. (2024). Why the level of access decides what an audit can find.
  • Our approach to working with AI companies, METR (2026). A leading evaluator's own account of its independence rules and limits.
  • AI Audit-Washing and Accountability, Ellen P. Goodman and Julia Tréhu (2022). The main critique: weak audits can launder bad practice.
