Holding AI companies to account

ConstrainingAlso: Evaluating

Model cards, system cards and transparency reporting

Publish a standard document with each model on its data, evaluations, limits and risks, so outsiders can check the developer's claims.

Open in the compendium

A model card is a short document published with an AI model that says what it is for, how it was tested, how well it performs and where it fails. Frontier labs now publish longer 'system cards' that also report safety evaluations and the safeguards put in place. These documents let outsiders check developers' claims and spot risks, and laws in the EU and California now require some of this disclosure.

How it works

The idea comes from a 2019 paper by Margaret Mitchell and colleagues at Google. They proposed that every released model come with a card that states its intended uses and reports its performance across different groups of people, so users do not deploy it where it works badly. A companion idea, 'datasheets for datasets' (Gebru and others, 2018), does the same for training data. Hugging Face, a leading public model hub, builds a model card into every model page.

A frontier system card goes further. Before or at launch, the developer describes the model's capabilities, the dangerous-capability tests it ran, the results, and the protections it applied. Anthropic, for example, lists system cards for its Claude models going back to July 2023, and OpenAI publishes system cards and addenda on its Deployment Safety Hub. Some disclosure is now required: the EU AI Act requires general-purpose model providers to keep technical documentation for regulators and give key information to downstream developers, and California's SB 53 requires frontier developers to publish a transparency report, including summaries of catastrophic-risk assessments, before deploying a new frontier model.

Where research stands

Publishing cards is common, but their depth is uneven and has been falling by one measure. Stanford's Foundation Model Transparency Index scored 13 developers in December 2025: the average fell from 58 out of 100 in 2024 to 40, though the index changed its criteria that year. IBM scored 95; xAI and Midjourney scored 14. Developers were least open about training data, training compute, and how models are used after release. A 2024 study of 32,111 model cards on Hugging Face found the sections on evaluation, limitations and environmental impact were the least often filled in.

Timing and detail are recurring problems. In 2025, Google released Gemini 2.5 Pro in late March and published a short model card three weeks later, which critics said fell short of its 2023 White House and 2024 Seoul summit commitments; OpenAI released GPT-4.1 without a safety report, saying it was not a frontier model. Work is under way to standardise what cards contain. In September 2025, GovAI published STREAM, a reporting standard and template for chemical and biological evaluation results in model reports, developed with 23 experts from government, civil society, academia and labs. The deeper limit is that cards are self-reported, so their value depends on outside checks.

Key results

  • Mitchell and co-authors proposed model cards that report a model's performance across demographic groups and its intended uses, illustrated with a smile detector and a toxic-comment classifier. Margaret Mitchell, Simone Wu, Andrew Zaldivar, Parker Barnes, Lucy Vasserman, Ben Hutchinson, Elena Spitzer, Inioluwa Deborah Raji, Timnit Gebru, 2019
  • The 2025 index found average developer transparency fell from 58 to 40 out of 100, with IBM at 95 and xAI and Midjourney at 14, and opacity concentrated on training data, compute and post-deployment use. Alexander Wan, Kevin Klyman, Sayash Kapoor, Nestor Maslej, Shayne Longpre, Betty Xiong, Percy Liang, Rishi Bommasani, 2025
  • Across 32,111 Hugging Face model cards, evaluation, limitations and environmental-impact sections had the lowest fill-out rates, while training sections were most often complete. Weixin Liang, Nazneen Rajani, Xinyu Yang, Ezinwanne Ozoani, Eric Wu, Yiqun Chen, Daniel Scott Smith, James Zou, 2024
  • Google shipped Gemini 2.5 Pro in March 2025 without a model card, apparently against its White House, G7 and Seoul transparency commitments. Beatrice Nolan, Jeremy Kahn (Fortune), 2025
  • STREAM gives developers best-practice guidance and a three-page template for reporting chemical and biological evaluation results, built with 23 experts from government, civil society, academia and labs. Tegan McCaslin, Jide Alaga, Samira Nedungadi, Seth Donoughe et al., 2025

Open problems

  • Standardising which evaluations a system card must report and in how much detail, so cards can be compared. source
  • Making developers disclose training data, compute and post-deployment use, the areas where they are least open. source
  • Ensuring cards are published before or at release rather than weeks later, and for every significant model version. source
  • Verifying self-reported claims, since a card is only as reliable as the developer that writes it. source

Criticisms

  • Seeing inside a system is not the same as understanding or governing it; transparency alone is inadequate for algorithmic accountability. Mike Ananny and Kate Crawford, New Media & Society
  • Thin, late model cards tell 'a troubling story of a race to the bottom on AI safety and transparency'. Kevin Bankston, Center for Democracy and Technology (April 2025)
  • Voluntary transparency leaves leading developers in the middle of the pack, avoiding reputational harm without strong incentives to lead, so stronger policy is needed. Stanford CRFM authors of the 2025 Foundation Model Transparency Index

Who works on it

  • Stanford Center for Research on Foundation Models (CRFM), Stanford HAI: Publishes the annual Foundation Model Transparency Index scoring developers' disclosures. Transparency in AI is on the Decline (2025)
  • Centre for the Governance of AI: Led STREAM, a standard for reporting evaluation results in model reports. STREAM (ChemBio) (2025)
  • Anthropic: Publishes system cards documenting capabilities, safety evaluations and deployment decisions for each Claude model. System cards (2026)
  • OpenAI: Publishes system cards and safety addenda for its models on its Deployment Safety Hub. Deployment Safety Hub (2026)
  • Amazon (Amazon AGI): Publishes technical reports and model cards for its Nova models. Amazon Nova Premier: Technical report and model card (2025)
  • Hugging Face: Builds model cards into every model page on its hub, with a template and metadata standard. Model Cards (Hub documentation) (2026)
  • Center for Democracy and Technology: Publicly assessed the adequacy of frontier model cards. Comments quoted in Fortune on the Gemini 2.5 Pro model card (2025)

Status

Common practice, uneven depth. The 2025 Foundation Model Transparency Index found developers' average transparency score fell from 58 in 2024 to 40 out of 100. Stanford reports the fall with a caveat: the 2025 edition was the first to update the Index's original indicators. Scores ranged from 14 (xAI, Midjourney) to 95 (IBM).

In their own words

In order to clarify the intended use cases of machine learning models and minimize their usage in contexts for which they are not well suited, we recommend that released models be accompanied by documentation detailing their performance characteristics. In this paper, we propose a framework that we call model cards, to encourage such transparent model reporting.

Margaret Mitchell et al. (Google), Model Cards for Model Reporting, 2019

While the societal impact of foundation models is growing, transparency is on the decline, mirroring the opacity that has plagued past digital technologies (e.g. social media). Reversing this trend is essential: transparency is a vital precondition for public accountability, scientific innovation, and effective governance.

Rishi Bommasani et al. (Stanford CRFM), The Foundation Model Transparency Index, 2023
