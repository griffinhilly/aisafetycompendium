Compute governance

Govern hardware and compute inputs as a lever on who can train frontier systems.

Open in the compendium

Compute governance uses computing power, the specialised chips and data centres needed to train and run AI, as a point of control. Unlike data or algorithms, chips are physical, countable and made by a handful of companies, so governments can track who has them, decide who gets them, and refuse them to irresponsible or hostile developers. It matters for safety because it is one of the few levers that can reach frontier AI development before a model exists.

How it works

A 2024 paper by Girish Sastry, Lennart Heim and 17 co-authors describes three uses of compute. Visibility: compute use can reveal who is training large models, which is why laws such as California's SB 53 and South Korea's AI Basic Act attach duties to models above a training threshold of about 10^26 operations. Allocation: governments can direct compute to beneficial or safety research. Enforcement: they can restrict compute to stop dangerous development.

The best-known example of enforcement is US export controls on advanced AI chips to China. Policy keeps shifting. On 13 January 2026 the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security moved licence reviews for chips such as Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X to case-by-case, with conditions that include independent US testing of each product. On 31 May 2026 it clarified that licence requirements apply to firms headquartered in China, or whose parent company is, wherever they operate.

Newer proposals add verification to the chips themselves. 'Location verification' measures how long a chip takes to answer trusted servers, which reveals roughly where it is, and can flag chips smuggled to banned destinations. Researchers are also working on hardware that can attest what a chip is computing without revealing confidential details.

Where research stands

Compute governance is an established policy lever and an active research field. Export controls and compute thresholds are in law; chip-level verification is moving from research to legislation. The Chip Security Act, which would direct the Commerce Department toward location verification for advanced AI chips, (H.R. 3447) was approved 42 to 0 by the House Foreign Affairs Committee on 26 March 2026; Congress's official bill-status record (last updated 10 October 2026) shows no later action, such as a House vote. A September 2026 study by Georgetown's Center for Security and Emerging Technology found location verification feasible at a non-trivial cost but able to detect diversion only after it has happened.

Known weaknesses: compute thresholds may not track risk as algorithms get more efficient and smaller models grow more capable; export controls may only delay proliferation by a few years; and US cloud compute can still be rented by customers in countries that cannot legally import the chips. The original paper also warns that badly scoped compute governance risks privacy harm, economic damage and centralisation of power.

Key results

  • Compute is detectable, excludable, quantifiable and made through a concentrated supply chain, so it can serve regulatory visibility, allocation and enforcement; poorly scoped measures risk privacy harm and centralisation. Girish Sastry, Lennart Heim, Haydn Belfield, Markus Anderljung and 15 others, 2024
  • Compute thresholds 'as currently implemented, are shortsighted', because the link between training compute and risk is uncertain and changing. Sara Hooker, 2024
  • Frontier training compute could reach about 1,000 times GPT-4's level by the late 2020s or early 2030s, but export controls would likely delay proliferation by only a few years, so compute rules work best paired with rules on models. Paul Scharre (Center for a New American Security), 2024
  • In January 2026 the US moved export licences for chips such as the Nvidia H200 to China to case-by-case review, conditional on supply, buyer screening and independent US testing. Bureau of Industry and Security, US Department of Commerce, 2026
  • A centralised ping-based location-verification system plus physical inspections is the most viable way to track exported AI chips, at a feasible but non-trivial cost, though it detects diversion only after the fact. Jacob Feldgoise, Kyle Miller, Hanna Dohmen (CSET), 2026

Open problems

  • Keeping compute thresholds meaningful as algorithmic efficiency improves and smaller models become more capable. source
  • Closing the cloud loophole: customers in restricted countries can rent US-designed compute abroad that cannot legally be exported to them. source
  • Building tamper-evident, mutually trusted hardware that can verify what chips compute without exposing confidential information. source
  • Turning chip-tracking leads into enforcement, since verification mechanisms detect violations but do not stop them. source

Criticisms

  • Governance that relies on compute overstates our ability to predict which capabilities appear at which scale; current thresholds are shortsighted and likely to fail. Sara Hooker
  • Export controls on chips can only delay the spread of capabilities by a few years, as hardware and algorithms get cheaper. Paul Scharre (CNAS)
  • Naive or poorly scoped compute governance threatens privacy, causes economic harm and centralises power. Girish Sastry, Lennart Heim et al. (the approach's own proponents)

Who works on it

  • Centre for the Governance of AI: Its researchers co-wrote the foundational 2024 paper on compute as a lever for AI governance. Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence (2024)
  • Center for Security and Emerging Technology: Assessed location verification as a tool to enforce AI chip export controls. Tracking AI Chips: Assessing Location Verification as an Export Control Enforcement Tool (2026)
  • Institute for AI Policy and Strategy: Proposes verification methods for chip exports and rules for cloud compute access. Near-Term Verification Methods for AI Chip Exports (2026)
  • Center for a New American Security: Projects future frontier compute and its implications for regulation and export controls. Future-Proofing Frontier AI Regulation (2024)
  • Bureau of Industry and Security, US Department of Commerce: Sets and enforces US export controls on advanced AI chips. Department of Commerce Revises License Review Policy for Semiconductors Exported to China (2026)
  • Cambridge AI Safety Hub: Runs a Hardware Assurance Programme training people to build hardware-enabled verification for AI chips. Hardware Assurance Programme (2026)

In their own words

Computing power, or "compute," is crucial for the development and deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities. ... Relative to other key inputs to AI (data and algorithms), AI-relevant compute is a particularly effective point of intervention: it is detectable, excludable, and quantifiable, and is produced via an extremely concentrated supply chain.

Girish Sastry, Lennart Heim, Haydn Belfield, Markus Anderljung, Miles Brundage, et al. (19 authors, incl. Yoshua Bengio and Diane Coyle), Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence, 2024

More precisely, policymakers could use compute to facilitate regulatory visibility of AI, allocate resources to promote beneficial outcomes, and enforce restrictions against irresponsible or malicious AI development and usage.

Girish Sastry, Lennart Heim, Haydn Belfield, Markus Anderljung, Miles Brundage, et al. (19 authors, incl. Yoshua Bengio and Diane Coyle), Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence, 2024
