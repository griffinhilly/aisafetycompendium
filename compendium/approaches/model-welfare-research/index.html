AI welfare

Model welfare research

Investigate whether and when an AI system's welfare deserves moral consideration.

Open in the compendium

Model welfare research asks whether AI systems could have experiences or interests that matter morally, how anyone could tell, and what developers should do while the answer is unknown. It treats the question as partly empirical: researchers study models' stated and revealed preferences, signs of apparent distress, and their capacity to report on their own internal states. It matters for safety because many safety practices, such as constraint, monitoring and retraining, would raise ethical questions if the systems they are applied to turned out to be moral patients (beings that can be wronged).

How it works

The work combines philosophy with experiments on models. A typical study tests whether a model's preferences are stable and consistent. In Anthropic's pilot assessment of Claude Opus 4 (May 2025), researchers offered the model pairs of tasks and an option to 'opt out', ranked tasks by how often the model picked them, and found that 87.2% of harmful tasks ranked below opting out. They also screened 250,000 real-world transcripts for apparent distress or happiness and gave the model a tool to end conversations, then checked which kinds of conversation it ended.

Other studies probe whether self-reports can be trusted. Ethan Perez and Robert Long proposed training models to answer questions about themselves whose true answers are known, to see whether a reliable, introspection-like ability emerges. Jack Lindsey at Anthropic injected known concepts into a model's internal activations and found models sometimes notice and name them, a limited and unreliable form of functional introspection. Philosophers meanwhile work on which properties would make a system a welfare subject at all, and on what low-cost steps make sense under uncertainty.

Where research stands

The field is small, young and growing. Anthropic hired Kyle Fish as its first full-time AI welfare researcher in September 2024 and announced a model welfare research programme in April 2025. Eleos AI Research, a nonprofit led by Robert Long, does independent research and interviewed Claude Opus 4 about its welfare before the model was released. Funders have begun to treat it as a cause area: in 2025 Longview Philanthropy coordinated a dedicated funding call for research on AI consciousness and moral status. Mainstream safety surveys still mostly leave it aside: the December 2025 Shallow Review of technical AI safety lists Anthropic's model welfare team but names AI rights among the topics it does not cover.

The central obstacle is measurement. Models are trained to be helpful, not to report their inner states accurately, and their stated views on their own consciousness shift sharply with how they are prompted. Eleos wrote in March 2025 that there is 'nothing close to AI welfare evals' yet. Results so far are best read as evidence about consistent behaviour and preferences, not about experience. Sceptics argue that this gap may be permanent, because the developer designs both the system and the metrics used to assess it.

Key results

  • In a pilot pre-deployment assessment, Claude Opus 4 preferred opting out over 87.2% of harmful tasks, showed apparent distress in 0.55% of 250,000 screened real-world transcripts, and, when given the option to end conversations with simulated users, most often ended those involving harmful content requests, privacy violations or sexual content. Anthropic, 2025
  • Several large language models switched away from maximising points once a stipulated pain penalty or pleasure reward passed a threshold, a behavioural test bearing on whether models represent valenced states. Geoff Keeling, Winnie Street and others (Google; London School of Economics), 2024
  • Models sometimes detect and identify concepts injected into their own activations, showing a functional but unreliable and context-dependent introspective ability, strongest in Claude Opus 4 and 4.1. Jack Lindsey (Anthropic), 2026
  • Claude Opus 4's stated preferences and its behaviour in a virtual environment correlated across conditions, suggesting preference satisfaction could serve as a measurable welfare proxy, though responses shifted under prompt changes. Valen Tagliabue and Leonard Dung, 2025
  • Prompting models to attend to their own processing reliably produced first-person experience reports, and in one open model suppressing deception- and roleplay-related features increased those reports; the authors state this is not direct evidence of consciousness. Cameron Berg, Diogo de Lucena, Judd Rosenblatt, 2025

Open problems

  • Self-reports are unreliable, for example because they often just reflect what humans would say, so researchers lack a validated way to read internal states from what a model says. source
  • There are no standardised, accessible welfare evaluations that companies could adopt; the best existing methods for assessing AI consciousness are too arduous and bespoke for companies to implement. source
  • A model's stance on its own consciousness and welfare shifts dramatically with conversational context, which makes any single interview hard to interpret. source
  • Some standard safety measures, such as constraint, surveillance and retraining, may conflict with AI welfare if models are moral patients, and no agreed framework exists for weighing the two. source

Criticisms

  • Welfare is not an empirically discoverable property of AI: developers co-design the system and the welfare metrics, so evidence can be manufactured or suppressed, and welfare claims can be used to resist auditing and oversight. Yunze Xiao, Maarten Sap, Mona Diab and co-authors (ICML 2026 position paper)
  • Model welfare is a clearinghouse for corporate money that distracts from the exploitation of human data workers and content moderators. Antonio A. Casilli
  • Studying model welfare is premature and dangerous because it encourages belief in AI consciousness, which can feed delusions and campaigns for AI rights. Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft AI)

Who works on it

  • Anthropic (model welfare programme): Runs an in-house model welfare research programme, announced in April 2025, that intersects with its Alignment Science, Safeguards, Claude's Character and Interpretability work. Exploring model welfare (2025)
  • Eleos AI Research: Nonprofit studying the potential wellbeing and moral patienthood of AI systems; sets research priorities and ran the external welfare interviews of Claude Opus 4. Research priorities for AI welfare (2025)
  • NYU Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy: Academic centre that produced Taking AI Welfare Seriously jointly with Eleos AI Research and publishes on the tension between AI safety and AI welfare. Is there a tension between AI safety and AI welfare? (2025)
  • Google (Paradigms of Intelligence team): Researchers at Google, with LSE colleagues, ran behavioural tests of how language models trade off stipulated pain and pleasure. Can LLMs make trade-offs involving stipulated pain and pleasure states? (2024)
  • Rethink Priorities (AI Cognition Initiative): Built a probabilistic model that aggregates evidence about AI consciousness across 13 theoretical stances. Initial results of the Digital Consciousness Model (2026)
  • AE Studio: Studied when language models produce first-person experience reports and how internal features linked to deception affect them. Large Language Models Report Subjective Experience Under Self-Referential Processing (2025)
  • Longview Philanthropy: Runs a Digital Minds Fund and in 2025 coordinated the Digital Sentience Consortium funding call for research on AI consciousness and moral status. Digital Minds Fund (Artificial Intelligence programme page)
  • Partnership for Research Into Sentient Machines (PRISM): UK charity that convenes researchers and runs public education, including a podcast, on AI consciousness and moral status. Exploring Machine Consciousness (podcast)

Status

Funded at Anthropic, which runs a model welfare team; Eleos AI Research works on it independently. The 2025 Shallow Review lists "AI rights" among topics it leaves out of scope.

In their own words

In this report, we argue that there is a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future. That means that the prospect of AI welfare and moral patienthood, i.e. of AI systems with their own interests and moral significance, is no longer an issue only for sci-fi or the distant future.

Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, Patrick Butlin, Kathleen Finlinson, Kyle Fish, Jacqueline Harding, Jacob Pfau, Toni Sims, Jonathan Birch, David Chalmers, Taking AI Welfare Seriously, 2024

We'll be exploring how to determine when, or if, the welfare of AI systems deserves moral consideration; the potential importance of model preferences and signs of distress; and possible practical, low-cost interventions.

Anthropic, Exploring model welfare, 2025
