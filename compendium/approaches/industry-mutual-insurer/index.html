Holding AI companies to account

Constraining

Industry mutual insurer (nuclear-industry model)

Frontier labs jointly own an insurer that pools liability and sets safety commitments. It checks members through incident reporting and third-party evaluations.

Open in the compendium

An industry mutual insurer is an insurance company owned by the firms it covers. Under this proposal, the frontier AI companies would each put capital into a shared fund that pays out when any member causes harm. Because every member loses money when another member is careless, members have a reason to set and enforce safety rules on each other.

How it works

The model is borrowed from commercial nuclear power. In the United States, nuclear plant operators insure through a mutual that sends engineers to inspect members' sites and can suspend a member's coverage if it finds a dangerous condition that is not fixed promptly. Medical and legal malpractice mutuals work in a similar way: they pool incident data, review each other's failures and spread better practice.

Applied to AI, a lab that joins the mutual would pay in capital and accept conditions on its cover. Those conditions could include reporting incidents to the mutual, passing third-party evaluations and meeting shared safety standards. A lab that broke the rules could lose its cover. In a September 2026 Lawfare article, Cristian Trout, Rune Kvist and Rajiv Dattani argue that such a mutual would turn the risk of catastrophic liability into incident reporting, standards, evaluations and safety research. They also argue it could give labs an antitrust-compliant way to coordinate the pace of frontier development, and that it needs no new laws.

Where research stands

This is a proposal, not an existing body. As of October 2026, no AI mutual insurer has been formed. The idea comes mainly from one group of authors connected to the Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company (AIUC), a San Francisco start-up that writes standards for AI agents and insures them. Cristian Trout's earlier papers set out the nuclear precedent (2024, revised 2025) and the conditions under which insurance reduces harm rather than adding to it (December 2025). A July 2026 report with 37 authors, led by Trout, lists a frontier-developer mutual among several tools for catastrophic AI risk, next to catastrophe bonds and government backstops.

The main open question is whether insurance can work for this kind of risk at all. Insurers do well with frequent, measurable losses. Frontier AI catastrophes would be rare, very large and correlated: one model failure could hit many policyholders at once. In November 2025, several large insurers asked US regulators for permission to exclude widespread AI losses from corporate policies. Legal scholars have also argued that the claims made for 'regulation by insurance' are generally overstated. The literature on this approach is small, and almost all of it comes from its proponents.

Key results

  • Trout argues that frontier AI developers should face limited, strict and exclusive liability for catastrophic events, backed by mandatory insurance, and that insurers would then do risk modelling, monitoring and loss-prevention work, as they do in nuclear power. Cristian Trout, 2024
  • Trout finds that regulation by insurance can be particularly effective for catastrophic non-product accidents where market discipline is weak, and proposes a mandate that encourages a specialised insurer or mutual and bars pure captives (insurers owned by a single firm). Cristian Trout, 2025
  • The authors propose a mutual owned by frontier AI companies that would develop and enforce shared safety commitments, and say it requires no new laws or regulations. Cristian Trout, Rune Kvist, Rajiv Dattani, 2026
  • A 37-author report argues that affirmative AI insurance with limits in the billions is achievable by 2030 only with industry-wide coordination, and names a frontier-developer mutual as one tool for catastrophic tail risks. Cristian Trout, Sanmi Koyejo, Sasha Romanosky et al., 2026
  • In November 2025, Great American, Chubb and W. R. Berkley sought permission from US regulators to exclude widespread AI-related liabilities from corporate policies, because of fears of many correlated losses at once. TechCrunch, 2025

Open problems

  • Losses from an AI catastrophe could run far beyond any pool of member capital, so such tail risks may also need other tools, such as government backstops. source
  • Correlated losses, where one model failure harms many parties at once, are hard for any insurer to price or absorb. source
  • Insurance can create moral hazard (less care because someone else pays); the net safety effect depends on the insurer, the risk and the market structure. source

Criticisms

  • Claims that insurers can regulate safety beyond managing moral hazard are overstated, and proposals to use insurance for AI harms share that weakness. Kenneth S. Abraham and Daniel Schwarcz, Indiana Law Journal (2022)
  • Major insurers want to exclude widespread AI liabilities from corporate policies because they cannot absorb thousands of simultaneous claims from one AI failure. Insurers and an Aon executive, as reported by TechCrunch (November 2025)
  • The proponents themselves say clear liability and mandatory insurance cannot replace regulation, but can direct resources to risk modelling and safe design. Cristian Trout

Who works on it

In their own words

Build a frontier AI mutual insurance company to hold the risk. Owned by the frontier AI companies it covers, this mutual would develop and enforce shared safety commitments, given members have a direct stake in each other's safety practices: Each member puts capital into a fund that pays out when any member causes harm.

Cristian Trout, Rune Kvist, Rajiv Dattani (Lawfare), Bootstrapping Frontier AI Governance by Mutualizing Risk, 2026

A frontier AI mutual would pull forward catastrophic third-party liability and convert it into reliable incident reporting, standards, third-party evaluations, safety research and development (R&D), and an antitrust-compliant mechanism for pacing the frontier. This requires no new laws or regulations, and the mutual could be issuing its first policies in three months.

Cristian Trout, Rune Kvist, Rajiv Dattani (Lawfare), Bootstrapping Frontier AI Governance by Mutualizing Risk, 2026
