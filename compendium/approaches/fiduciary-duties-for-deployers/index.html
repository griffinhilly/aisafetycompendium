Holding AI companies to account

Fiduciary duties for AI deployers

Deployers of AI owe users duties of loyalty, care and confidentiality, which terms of service cannot waive.

Open in the compendium

A fiduciary is someone the law trusts to act in another person's interest, such as a doctor, lawyer or trustee. This approach says that companies which put AI systems in front of users, as advisers, therapists or companions, should owe those users the same core duties: loyalty, care and confidentiality. Its safety value is that the AI's operator could not legally steer users against their own interests, for example by exploiting their emotional reliance to sell them things.

How it works

Fiduciary law already covers relationships where one side depends on the other's expertise and good faith. Proponents argue that many AI products create exactly that kind of dependence. Take a chatbot marketed as a financial coach. Under this approach its deployer would owe a duty of loyalty, so it could not push users toward products that pay it a kickback. It would owe a duty of care, so its advice would have to match what it was built to do and can actually do. And it would owe a duty of confidentiality, so it could not sell what users told it.

In an April 2026 paper, William Marks argues that the AI system itself can create the duty, through how it is marketed, how it presents itself and how users come to rely on it. The duty then falls on the deployer that placed it before the user. On this view the duties are limited to what the system took on, and boilerplate terms of service cannot waive them. On the design side, Sebastian Benthall and David Shekman (2023) set out a procedure for building such systems: identify whom the system serves, work out their best interests, and act loyally and carefully toward them.

Where research stands

The idea is at the proposal and research stage. It grows out of Jack Balkin's 'information fiduciaries' proposal for internet platforms. Recent work moves it to AI: legal papers argue courts can apply existing doctrine now, and computer-science papers ask how to design and test a 'loyal' AI agent. In June 2026, NYU School of Law's Information Law Institute and the GliaNet Alliance held a two-day workshop on fiduciary duties and AI, funded by the Future of Life Foundation. Consumer Reports and Stanford's Digital Economy Lab run a 'Loyal Agents' standards effort.

What is missing is enforcement. The research found no statute that makes AI deployers fiduciaries, and no reported court ruling that has treated an AI deployer as one. Whether courts will extend fiduciary status to a mass-market product, rather than to a one-to-one professional relationship, is the central open question. The earlier platform version of the idea drew strong criticism, and much of it carries over.

Key results

  • Marks argues that an AI system can generate fiduciary duties through its marketing, design and self-presentation, that these duties fall on the deployer, and that courts can apply existing doctrine without new legislation. William Marks, 2026
  • Benthall and Shekman turn fiduciary duties of loyalty and care into a design and audit procedure for AI: understand the context, identify the principals, assess their best interests, and act loyally and carefully. Sebastian Benthall, David Shekman, 2023
  • On thousands of test cases built from US court opinions, OpenAI's then-latest model judged fiduciary obligations correctly 78% of the time, up from 73% for its predecessor. John J. Nay, 2023
  • Aguirre and co-authors define 'AI loyalty' as the degree to which a system minimises and discloses conflicts of interest and puts users' interests first, and argue many AI products have built-in conflicts. Anthony Aguirre, Gaia Dempsey, Harry Surden, Peter B. Reiner, 2020
  • Ayres and Balkin argue that because AI lacks intentions, the law should judge it by objective standards, and that AI performing fiduciary functions should be held to the highest standard of care. Ian Ayres, Jack M. Balkin, 2024

Open problems

  • Deciding when a mass-market AI product, rather than a one-to-one professional, crosses the line into a fiduciary relationship. source
  • Defining a user's 'best interests' in a way an AI system can act on and an auditor can test. source
  • Finding business models that let a company profit while staying loyal to users rather than to advertisers or sellers. source
  • Building certification or trust marks so users can tell a loyal agent from a disloyal one. source

Criticisms

  • The information-fiduciary idea does not address concentrated market power or business models built on surveillance, and it may make policymakers complacent and drop stronger public regulation too early. Lina Khan and David Pozen, Harvard Law Review (2019)

Who works on it

  • NYU School of Law, Information Law Institute: Co-organised a two-day workshop on fiduciary duties and AI in June 2026; home of Sebastian Benthall's fiduciary AI research. Fiduciary Duties and AI: Legal Frameworks, Technical Implementation, and Governance (workshop) (2026)
  • GliaNet Alliance: Nonprofit alliance building fiduciary governance for AI, so that agents owe the people they serve duties of care and loyalty; co-organised the NYU workshop and a 2025 discussion with Consumer Reports on loyalty duties. A Trust Governance Layer for AI Agents (2025)

  • Stanford Digital Economy Lab: Co-runs the Loyal Agents initiative on legal frameworks, open standards and evaluation tools for agents that act in users' best interests. Loyal Agents (2026)
  • Future of Life Foundation: Sponsored the June 2026 NYU workshop on fiduciary duties and AI. Workshop on Fiduciary Duties and AI (2026)

In their own words

The duties it generates are not new; they are the familiar obligations of loyalty, care, and confidentiality. What is new is the mechanism by which they arise, and the fact that responsibility for them falls on the deployer who placed the instrument before a vulnerable user.

William Marks, AI Fiduciaries and the Law, 2026

The duty of loyalty, fiduciary law's most distinctive one, would bar self-dealing, kickback arrangements, and the exploitation of emotional reliance. ... And these duties should not be disclaimable through boilerplate terms of service. Permitting companies to cultivate fiduciary-level trust while disclaiming fiduciary-level responsibility is precisely the kind of opportunism fiduciary law exists to prevent.

William Marks, AI Fiduciaries and the Law, 2026
