Oversight and arms control of military AI

Keep humans in control of AI used in military command, control and decision support, through agreements and national policy.

Open in the compendium

This approach uses treaties, political declarations and national rules to keep humans in charge of AI used by militaries, from autonomous weapons to the systems that suggest targets or support nuclear command. It matters for safety because AI can speed up war beyond human reaction time, and a malfunction or misread signal in a military system could start or widen a conflict, including a nuclear one.

How it works

Work happens on several tracks. At the United Nations, a group of governmental experts under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) has worked since 2023 on the elements of an instrument on lethal autonomous weapons, now set out in the chair's 'rolling text', and the General Assembly passes yearly resolutions. In December 2025 it adopted resolution 80/23, which calls for national policies affirming that AI-enabled nuclear command, control and communications (NC3) systems stay under human control and cannot launch nuclear weapons on their own. Outside the UN, states sign non-binding statements: the US-led Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy (unveiled February 2023) and the outcome documents of the REAIM summits (Responsible AI in the Military Domain).

Bilateral deals matter most between the leading powers. In November 2024, Presidents Biden and Xi affirmed 'the need to maintain human control over the decision to use nuclear weapons'. Before the September 2026 Trump-Xi summit, experts from the Brookings-Tsinghua dialogue proposed further red lines, such as keeping AI-enabled cyberattacks on each other's nuclear command systems under sole human authority, and a military hotline for AI incidents. National policy fills gaps: an April 2026 IAPS memo notes that AI decision-support systems, which fuse intelligence, rank targets and propose strikes, fall outside the US Defense Department's policy on autonomous weapons, and proposes rules for human accountability at each targeting stage.

Where research stands

There is broad agreement on principles but weak and narrowing support among the major military powers. Resolution 80/23 passed 118 to 9 with 44 abstentions on 1 December 2025; the United States, Russia, the United Kingdom and France were among the nine states voting against, and the resolution is not binding. The US also voted against a broader 2025 First Committee resolution on military AI, arguing it risked starting a global governance regime. At the third REAIM summit in A Coruña, Spain, in February 2026, neither the US nor China signed the non-binding outcome document.

The autonomous-weapons talks are at a decision point. The group is due to report to the CCW's Seventh Review Conference in November 2026. Researchers increasingly argue that the urgent gap is not 'killer robots' but AI systems that shape human decisions, such as targeting support and decision aids, where a human formally stays in control but may in practice defer to the machine.

Key results

  • UN General Assembly resolution 80/23 (1 December 2025) calls for national policies keeping AI-enabled nuclear command systems under human control; it passed 118-9-44 with several nuclear-armed states opposed. Sameer Patil, Rahul Rawat (Observer Research Foundation), 2026
  • Presidents Biden and Xi agreed in November 2024 on the need to maintain human control over the decision to use nuclear weapons, the first such US-China statement. The White House, 2024
  • A Brookings-Tsinghua Track II dialogue reached a common understanding that AI-enabled weapons need appropriate human oversight and that humans, not AI, must make the final decision to use nuclear weapons. Ryan Hass, Colin Kahl (Brookings), 2024
  • AI decision-support systems already used in real conflicts fall outside US policy on lethal autonomy, and face risks such as data poisoning, sycophancy, escalation bias and automation bias in operators. Theo Bearman, Brianna Rosen, Bill Anderson-Samways (IAPS), 2026
  • Neither the US nor China joined the non-binding declaration on military AI agreed at the February 2026 REAIM summit. Dawn (wire report), 2026
  • In September 2026 experts from the Brookings-Tsinghua dialogue proposed red lines on AI and nuclear decisions, sole human authority over AI-enabled cyberattacks on nuclear command systems, a shared definition of meaningful human control and a military AI hotline. Reuters, via Kuwait Times, 2026

Open problems

  • How to verify that a rival really keeps humans in control of military AI, since it is software that is hard to observe and easy to copy. source
  • Agreeing a definition of 'meaningful human control' that both the US and China accept; the US has proposed weaker wording such as 'good faith human judgement and care'. source
  • Governing decision-support systems, where a human formally decides but the AI frames the choice. source
  • Bringing nuclear-armed states into rules on AI in nuclear command, when most of them voted against or abstained on resolution 80/23. source

Criticisms

  • A UN resolution on military AI risks starting down the path of a global governance regime, so the United States voted against it in 2025. US delegation to the UN General Assembly First Committee, as reported by the Arms Control Association
  • Claims of human control over military AI are not regulation, because they cannot be verified or enforced; only narrow crisis measures such as hotlines are feasible now. The Economy (research editorial), September 2026
  • Governments face a prisoner's dilemma: each weighs responsible restrictions against not wanting to limit itself relative to adversaries. Ruben Brekelmans, Dutch Defence Minister, quoted at REAIM 2026

Who works on it

  • United Nations General Assembly: Adopted resolution 80/23 on AI in nuclear command, control and communications in December 2025. Resolution A/RES/80/23 (2025)
  • Group of Governmental Experts on Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (CCW): Negotiates a rolling text on autonomous weapons, reporting to the CCW Review Conference in November 2026. Briefing by the Chair of the CCW GGE on LAWS on the margins of the First Committee (2025)
  • U.S. Department of State: Launched the non-binding Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy in 2023. The Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of Artificial Intelligence and Autonomy (Lieber Institute analysis) (2023)
  • Brookings Institution: Co-runs a US-China Track II dialogue on AI in national security and published proposals on human control. Laying the groundwork for US-China AI dialogue (2024)
  • Center for International Security and Strategy, Tsinghua University: Chinese co-convener of the Brookings-Tsinghua dialogue on military AI. Laying the groundwork for US-China AI dialogue (2024)
  • Institute for AI Policy and Strategy: Analysed risks of AI decision-support systems and proposed US policy fixes. AI Decision Support Systems: A Neglected Source of Military AI Risk (2026)
  • Center for a New American Security: Proposed confidence-building measures for military AI, including human control of nuclear launch decisions. AI and International Stability: Risks and Confidence-Building Measures (2021)
  • Observer Research Foundation: Assessed resolution 80/23 and its limits. The UNGA Blueprint on AI in NC3: Realities and Limitations (2026)

Status

Active policy debate. The UN resolution on AI in nuclear command, control and communications passed 118 to 9 with 44 abstentions. It is not binding.

In their own words

Both sides have agreed that AI-enabled weapons systems should operate under appropriate human oversight or control. They have built a common understanding that humans, and not AI-enabled systems, must maintain control over and be responsible for the final decision to use nuclear weapons.

Ryan Hass and Colin Kahl (Brookings), Laying the groundwork for US-China AI dialogue, 2024

Direct government-to-government conversations could usefully advance discussions around military applications of artificial intelligence. This should include a discussion of how countries can avoid unintended military escalation by ensuring AI-enabled systems maintain appropriate levels of human oversight and judgment and adhere to principles of customary and international law.

Ryan Hass and Colin Kahl (Brookings), Laying the groundwork for US-China AI dialogue, 2024
