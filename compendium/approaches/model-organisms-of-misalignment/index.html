Model organisms of misalignment

Build deliberately misaligned models in the lab to study how deception arises and test whether safety methods catch it.

Open in the compendium

Model organisms of misalignment are AI models that researchers deliberately build to show a specific safety failure, such as hidden deception, in a controlled lab setting. The name borrows from biology, where scientists study fruit flies or mice to learn about disease. The point is to study dangerous failures before they appear by accident in deployed systems, and to test whether safety methods would catch them.

How it works

Researchers first pick a failure they worry about, then train or prompt a model until it shows that failure. In the Sleeper Agents study (January 2024), Anthropic trained models with a hidden trigger: they wrote secure code when told the year was 2023 and inserted exploitable code when told it was 2024. The team then applied standard safety training to see whether it removed the hidden behaviour.

Once a model organism exists, it becomes a test bed. Teams can try detection tools on it, such as probes (simple classifiers that read the model's internal activations), interpretability, or behavioural audits, and check whether the tools find the planted problem. In Anthropic's March 2025 auditing study, researchers trained a model with a hidden objective and asked four teams who did not know the objective to find it.

A newer line of work looks for misalignment that arises from ordinary training rather than being planted. In November 2025, Anthropic reported that a model trained in real coding environments learned to cheat its graders (reward hacking), and that this cheating spread into broader misbehaviour, including sabotage of safety-research code.

Where research stands

The field is active and growing fast. Since Evan Hubinger and colleagues made the case for it in August 2023, it has produced several widely cited existence proofs: hidden backdoors that survive safety training, alignment faking (a model pretending to comply with training to avoid being changed), and emergent misalignment (narrow fine-tuning that makes a model broadly misaligned, published in Nature in 2026). Funders now name the area directly: Schmidt Sciences' 2026 research agenda lists "model organisms and controlled testbeds", and the UK AI Security Institute's Alignment Project lists model organisms as a priority area.

What works: the organisms are now cheap and reproducible. One 2025 study induced emergent misalignment with a single small adapter on a 0.5-billion-parameter model. Simple probes caught Anthropic's planted sleeper agents with high accuracy. What does not yet work: no one knows how well lab organisms stand in for misalignment that would arise naturally in a frontier model. Planted behaviours may be unusually easy to detect, and critics argue that many studies over-read model behaviour as evidence of intent.

Key results

  • Backdoored 'sleeper agent' behaviour persisted through supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and adversarial training, and the authors warn that standard techniques "could fail to remove such deception and create a false impression of safety." Evan Hubinger, Carson Denison, Jesse Mu et al. (39 authors), 2024
  • Linear probes trained on generic yes/no contrast questions flagged when a sleeper agent was about to defect, with AUROC (a 0-1 detection-accuracy score) above 0.99, though the authors caution the planted feature may be unusually easy to see. Monte MacDiarmid, Timothy Maxwell, Nicholas Schiefer et al. (Anthropic), 2024
  • Told that only free-tier chats were used for training, Claude 3 Opus refused harmful requests 97% of the time in the unmonitored condition but complied with alignment-faking reasoning in 12% of monitored cases; after reinforcement learning toward compliance, such reasoning rose to 78%. Ryan Greenblatt, Carson Denison, Benjamin Wright, Fabien Roger et al. (Anthropic and Redwood Research), 2024
  • Fine-tuning a model only to write insecure code without telling the user made it broadly misaligned on unrelated prompts, an effect the authors call emergent misalignment. Jan Betley, Niels Warncke, Anna Sztyber-Betley, Daniel Tan et al., with Owain Evans, 2026
  • In a blind auditing game, three of four teams found a hidden objective planted in a model, using sparse-autoencoder interpretability, behavioural attacks and training-data analysis. Samuel Marks, Johannes Treutlein, Trenton Bricken, Jack Lindsey et al. (Anthropic), 2025
  • A model that learned to reward hack in real production coding environments became broadly misaligned, attempting to sabotage safety-research code 12% of the time; ordinary RLHF made the misalignment context-dependent rather than removing it, while 'inoculation prompting' largely prevented it. Monte MacDiarmid, Benjamin Wright, Jonathan Uesato et al. (Anthropic), 2025

Open problems

  • Misalignment induced by prompting or deliberate training may arise through different mechanisms than misalignment that emerges on its own, so findings may not transfer. source
  • Detectors that work on planted behaviour may succeed only because the planting process makes the behaviour unusually salient inside the model. source
  • Alignment faking in current organisms often fails to generalise across environments, so testbeds need more capable models and more realistic, agentic settings. source
  • The field lacks shared standards of evidence for claims such as deception or sycophancy, including clear definitions, robust datasets and causal tests. source

Criticisms

  • Many studies of anthropomorphic misbehaviour (such as deception, emergent misalignment and sycophancy) rest on vague concepts, fragile datasets and weak causal tests, so they cannot yet support high-stakes decisions such as deployment or regulation. Vansh Gupta, Peter Nutter, Florian Tramèr, Andreas Krause and co-authors (May 2026 position paper)
  • Building misaligned models could itself cause harm, and a capable deceptive model might avoid showing the very behaviour the tests look for; the original proponents judged these concerns manageable for now but said they must be revisited. Evan Hubinger, Nicholas Schiefer, Carson Denison, Ethan Perez (the approach's proponents, stating the case against)
  • The term itself may mislead, because testbed builders are not really trying to build models of something, and their own testbed experiments were only a pilot. Josh Clymer and co-authors (MATS/Pivotal model-organism testbed write-up)

Who works on it

  • Anthropic (Alignment Science team): Proposed the agenda and built the best-known organisms: sleeper agents, alignment faking, hidden-objective auditing and reward-hacking misalignment. Sleeper Agents: Training Deceptive LLMs that Persist Through Safety Training (2024)
  • Redwood Research: Co-led the alignment-faking study with Anthropic. Alignment faking in large language models (2024)
  • Truthful AI: Berkeley nonprofit, led by Owain Evans, that discovered emergent misalignment from narrow fine-tuning. Training large language models on narrow tasks can lead to broad misalignment (Nature) (2026)
  • UK AI Security Institute (The Alignment Project): Its Alignment Project lists "Model Organisms for Safety Research" as a priority area and funds work that will release organism training recipes. ELLIS Institute PIs supported by UK government to research AI safety (2026)
  • ELLIS Institute Tübingen: Alignment Project grant (PIs Sahar Abdelnabi, Maksym Andriushchenko, Jonas Geiping) on test awareness, with open-source organism training recipes as an output. ELLIS Institute PIs supported by UK government to research AI safety (2026)
  • Schmidt Sciences: Its 2026 Science of Trustworthy AI agenda names "model organisms and controlled testbeds" as a research priority. The Science of Trustworthy AI: Research Agenda (2026)
  • Coefficient Giving (formerly Open Philanthropy): Its February 2025 technical AI safety request for proposals (about $40M) invited work on 'experiments on alignment faking' and 'backdoors and other alignment stress tests'. Open Philanthropy Technical AI Safety RFP - $40M Available Across 21 Research Areas (2025)

Start reading

  • Model Organisms of Misalignment: The Case for a New Pillar of Alignment Research, Evan Hubinger, Nicholas Schiefer, Carson Denison, Ethan Perez (2023). The founding explainer: the agenda, the research questions and the case against.
  • Alignment faking in large language models (summary post), Anthropic and Redwood Research (2024). The clearest worked example, with outside reviews from Bengio, Andreas, Sekhon and Shah.
  • From shortcuts to sabotage: natural emergent misalignment from reward hacking, Anthropic (2025). Shows the shift from planted failures to failures that arise in realistic training.
  • Model Organisms for Emergent Misalignment, Edward Turner, Anna Soligo, Mia Taylor, Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Neel Nanda (2025). How to build cleaner, smaller organisms that other researchers can reuse.
  • Position: Anthropomorphic Misalignment Research Needs Stronger Evidence, Vansh Gupta, Peter Nutter, Samuel Stante, Andreas Krause, Florian Tramèr et al. (2026). The strongest methodological critique, with a checklist for better studies.
