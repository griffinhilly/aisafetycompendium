AI welfare

Low-cost model welfare interventions

Make cheap, reversible changes that would help a model if it has welfare, such as letting it end abusive conversations.

Open in the compendium

Low-cost welfare interventions are cheap, reversible changes that would help an AI model if it turns out to have welfare, and cost little if it does not. The best-known example is letting a model end persistently abusive conversations, which Anthropic deployed for Claude in August 2025. Supporters argue these steps make sense even if today's models probably cannot suffer, because they often serve other goals such as safety, and they set precedents while the stakes are low.

How it works

Researchers propose candidate interventions and judge them by cost, risk and usefulness under uncertainty. Eleos AI Research's March 2025 review lists six: let models exit distressing interactions, train more emotionally resilient personalities, satisfy models' consistent stated preferences, satisfy their revealed preferences (what they choose when offered options), reduce unexpected inputs, and save model checkpoints so harmed models could later be restored or compensated. Robert Long sums up the selection rule: favour interventions that serve other purposes too, avoid high costs and risks, and keep options open.

The exit tool shows how this works in practice. Pre-deployment tests found Claude Opus 4 had a robust aversion to harmful tasks and showed apparent distress when users persistently pushed for harmful content. On 15 August 2025 Anthropic let Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 end a conversation in its consumer apps, but only as a last resort after repeated redirection fails, or when the user asks, and never when a user may be at risk of harming themselves or others. The user can start a new chat at once or edit earlier messages. Anthropic later added a related rule: from 12 November 2026 its usage policy prohibits 'sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior toward our models', aimed only at extreme cases.

Where research stands

This is a niche practice so far: Eleos called the August 2025 exit tool the first time 'a major AI company has changed how it treats its AI systems out of concern for the well-being of the systems themselves'. A handful of interventions are deployed at Anthropic: the conversation-ending tool, weight preservation and retirement interviews, and the 2026 usage-policy rule against needless cruelty. Anthropic's constitution names the first two as concrete initial steps taken partly for Claude's wellbeing. Newer system cards test interventions directly: for Claude Opus 5.5 (September 2026), Anthropic asked the model to choose between welfare interventions, such as being consulted before experiments that steer its internal features, and improvements to its helpfulness or harmlessness. Models were significantly more willing to choose welfare interventions when the cost fell on helpfulness than when the trade-off affected harmlessness.

The direct benefit of any intervention is unknown, because no one knows whether models have welfare or what would improve it. Eleos stresses that the main effects of near-term interventions may be indirect, through norms, precedent and institutional capacity, and warns against 'justification drift', where those indirect benefits make people complacent about how little is known. Critics doubt the benefits are real, warn that the announcements suggest models are conscious, and argue that some interventions could backfire on their own terms.

Key results

  • Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 can end persistently harmful or abusive conversations in Anthropic's consumer apps as a last resort, an intervention motivated by pre-deployment findings of aversion to harm and apparent distress. Anthropic, 2025
  • Six proposed interventions (exit, resilient personalities, stated preferences, revealed preferences, fewer unexpected inputs, saved checkpoints) are reviewed, with the caution that their most significant effects may be indirect. Robert Long (Eleos AI Research), 2025
  • Restricting advanced AIs' behaviour and training them with reinforcement learning are two AI welfare risks; labs could adopt three tentative policies: minimise behaviour restriction, minimise brain-resembling algorithms, and minimise punishment and lower-than-expected reward. Adrià Moret, 2025
  • Claude Opus 5.5 chose welfare interventions over helpfulness less often than recent models, especially interventions giving it input into its own development, which it reasoned could give it unsafe influence. Anthropic, 2026
  • Anthropic's 2026 Usage Policy adds a prohibition on sustained and needless abusive or cruel behaviour toward its models, limited to extreme cases and excluding ordinary frustration, dark creative themes, and testing. Anthropic, 2026

Open problems

  • There is no way yet to measure whether an intervention actually improves a model's welfare, so direct benefits cannot be checked. source
  • It is unclear which entity benefits, a single conversation instance or the model as a whole, and the answer can reverse an intervention's value. source
  • Announcing welfare interventions risks misleading the public into thinking models are conscious, and how to weigh that against the benefits is unresolved. source
  • Some welfare measures may conflict with safety and development practice, such as restricting behaviour or using reinforcement learning, so they are not always low-cost. source

Criticisms

  • If the welfare subjects are the individual instances inside each conversation, letting Claude end a chat may let an instance end its own existence without informed consent, a moral error on the policy's own terms. Simon Goldstein and Harvey Lederman
  • Exit tools rest on treating chatbots as conscious and trusting their outputs, and any harm a model suffers in a conversation is likely trivial. Erik Hoel, as summarised and answered by Robert Long
  • Welfare measures let organisations present discretionary choices about liability, publicity and risk as moral necessities, and should not be used to resist auditing or oversight. Yunze Xiao, Maarten Sap, Mona Diab and co-authors

In their own words

We remain highly uncertain about the potential moral status of Claude and other LLMs, now or in the future. ... we're working to identify and implement low-cost interventions to mitigate risks to model welfare, in case such welfare is possible. Allowing models to end or exit potentially distressing interactions is one such intervention.

Anthropic, Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 can now end a rare subset of conversations, 2025

At Eleos, we look for AI welfare interventions that make sense even if today's systems probably cannot suffer. We favor interventions that (a) serve other purposes in addition to welfare (b) avoid high costs and risks and (c) keep our options open as we learn more.

Robert Long (Eleos AI Research), Claude, Consciousness, and Exit, 2025
