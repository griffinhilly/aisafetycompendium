Securing model weights and lab secrets

Protect frontier model weights and key algorithmic ideas from theft by insiders and nation-states.

Open in the compendium

Securing model weights means protecting the trained numbers that make up a frontier AI model, along with the key research ideas behind it, from theft by criminals, insiders and nation-states. Whoever holds the weights can run the model and strip out its safety training. Every other safeguard in this group, such as classifiers, monitoring and control, fails if an attacker can copy the model and run it elsewhere.

How it works

A model's weights are a huge set of numbers, often terabytes of data. Securing them combines ordinary information security with measures specific to AI. A lab can limit how many people can touch the weights and require two people to approve any access ('two-party authorisation'). It can cap the data flowing out of the secure environment, so a full copy cannot be smuggled out quickly. Staff with access get background checks, and data centres need physical security.

In 2024 RAND set out a widely used yardstick: five security levels, SL1 to SL5. Each level is defined by the strongest attacker it should stop, from amateur attackers at SL1 to the top-priority operations of the world's most capable nation-states at SL5. Google DeepMind and the EU Code of Practice both refer to this framework. In May 2025, for example, Anthropic activated its AI Safety Level 3 security standard, which includes two-party authorisation for weight access and early controls on outbound bandwidth. A related problem is protecting algorithmic secrets, the research insights that let a rival build an equal model without stealing it. These are much smaller than weights and live in employees' heads, so they are harder to protect.

Where research stands

RAND's 2024 report identified about 38 distinct attack vectors and defined the five levels. Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework ties its security levels to RAND's, and in April 2026 added a 'Security Level 2+' tier with extra measures against insider threats and well-resourced non-state attackers. The EU's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice (final version July 2025) requires signatories to set a security goal that covers insider threats, protect unreleased weights, and report weight exfiltration within five days; it points providers to the RAND report. In March 2026 the SL5 Task Force released a first public draft of an SL5 standard aimed at nation-state attackers.

Practice lags well behind the top level. In August 2025 Sella Nevo, lead author of the RAND report, wrote that no existing facility or security plan can both fully use and defend frontier AI systems against highly capable nation-state adversaries, and that the United States would still be years away from being able to do so. Real thefts of AI trade secrets have happened: in January 2026 a US jury convicted a former Google engineer of economic espionage for taking Google's AI trade secrets.

Key results

  • RAND identified about 38 distinct attack vectors on model weights, estimated which attackers could execute each, and defined five security levels with preliminary benchmark systems for each. Sella Nevo, Dan Lahav, Ajay Karpur, Yogev Bar-On, Henry Alexander Bradley, Jeff Alstott, 2024
  • Nevo argued that no existing facility or security plan can both use and defend frontier AI against highly capable nation-states, and proposed a two-to-three-year national sprint toward SL5 coordinated by the White House. Sella Nevo (Institute for Progress), 2025
  • Algorithmic secrets need roughly a million times less bandwidth to exfiltrate than weights, and around 1,000 employees have broad access to them and can probably reproduce them from memory, so they are far harder to protect than the weights themselves. Romeo Dean, 2025
  • Anthropic's AI Safety Level 3 standard, activated with Claude Opus 4 in May 2025, added two-party authorisation for weight access and preliminary egress bandwidth controls. Anthropic, 2025
  • The EU General-Purpose AI Code of Practice requires a security goal covering insider threats, protection of unreleased model parameters, and reporting of weight (self-)exfiltration within five days. European Commission AI Office process, 2025
  • A US jury convicted a former Google engineer on seven counts of economic espionage and seven of trade-secret theft for taking more than 2,000 pages of confidential Google AI trade secrets. CBS News, 2026

Open problems

  • Reaching SL5, protection against top nation-state operations, which no current facility achieves. source
  • Protecting algorithmic secrets that live in employees' heads and need almost no bandwidth to leak. source
  • Finding out whether private-sector staff can feasibly be vetted at SL5 level, and whether long-distance network connections can be secured against nation-state attackers. source
  • Overcoming weak market incentives, since heavy unilateral spending on security puts a company at a competitive disadvantage. source

Criticisms

  • AI companies are not on track to secure weights against motivated top state actors; most are around SL2 to SL3, and some of the research needed for SL5 could take as long as five years. Jeffrey Ladish (Palisade Research)
  • A weight-focused approach misses algorithmic secrets, whose security at leading labs is described as very low. Romeo Dean (AI 2027)

  • Anthropic: Applies its AI Safety Level 3 security standard, including two-party authorisation and egress controls. Activating AI Safety Level 3 Protections (2025)

  • European Commission (AI Office): Oversees the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, whose security chapter covers weight protection and insider threats. General-Purpose AI Code of Practice: Safety and Security Chapter (2025)

Status

Framework with five security levels (SL1 to SL5). The AI 2027 forecast extends it to algorithmic secrets and calls current lab security for those secrets relatively relaxed.

In their own words

The authors of this report explore what it would take to protect model weights — the learnable parameters that encode the core intelligence of an AI — from theft by a variety of potential attackers.

Sella Nevo, Dan Lahav, Ajay Karpur, Yogev Bar-On, Henry Alexander Bradley, Jeff Alstott (RAND), Securing AI Model Weights: Preventing Theft and Misuse of Frontier Models, 2024

Specifically, the authors (1) identify 38 meaningfully distinct attack vectors, (2) explore a variety of potential attacker operational capacities, from opportunistic (often financially driven) criminals to highly resourced nation-state operations, (3) estimate the feasibility of each attack vector being executed by different categories of attackers, and (4) define five security levels and recommend preliminary benchmark security systems that roughly achieve the security levels.

Sella Nevo, Dan Lahav, Ajay Karpur, Yogev Bar-On, Henry Alexander Bradley, Jeff Alstott (RAND), Securing AI Model Weights: Preventing Theft and Misuse of Frontier Models, 2024
