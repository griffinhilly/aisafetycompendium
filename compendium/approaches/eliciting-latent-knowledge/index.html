Eliciting latent knowledge (ELK)

Design objectives that make a model honestly report what it internally believes.

Open in the compendium

Eliciting latent knowledge (ELK) is the problem of getting an AI to tell us what it knows, even when its training only rewards answers that look right to us. If a powerful model knows that a camera has been tampered with but its training rewards 'looks fine on camera', we need a way to pull that hidden knowledge out. The Alignment Research Center (ARC) set out the problem in December 2021 and called it central to alignment.

How it works

ARC's standard example is a vault holding a diamond, watched by cameras. An AI predicts what the cameras will show next. A robber could tamper with the cameras so the diamond only appears to be there. The AI's internal model of the world may 'know' about the tampering even though the camera images look normal. The goal is to train a second part, a 'reporter', that answers honest questions about the AI's internal picture, such as 'is the diamond really still there?'

But we can only train the reporter on cases humans can check. On those cases two reporters give the same answers: one that translates the AI's real knowledge (a 'direct translator') and one that predicts what a human would conclude from the images (a 'human simulator'). Only the first one helps in the cases we cannot check. ELK research looks for training methods or tests that reliably pick the honest one.

Later work tested these ideas in experiments. One line looks for a 'truth direction' in a model's activations. Another, mechanistic anomaly detection, learns the normal internal reason the model gives a 'diamond is safe' prediction and flags new cases where the same output arises for a different internal reason.

Where research stands

ARC ran an ELK prize in early 2022. It received 197 proposals and paid $274,000 across 32 prizes and 24 honourable mentions, but did not claim any proposal solved the problem. ELK is still unsolved, and ARC's own work has moved on to a related, more theoretical agenda. As of October 2026 its homepage describes its focus as a theoretical foundation for mechanistic explanations of neural network behaviour, and does not mention ELK. That agenda, which includes mechanistic anomaly detection, grew directly out of ELK.

On language models, a 2022 method found yes/no 'truth' directions in activations without labels, and later work found that truth is often represented along a roughly linear direction. But Google DeepMind showed in December 2023 that such unsupervised methods may latch onto whatever feature is most prominent, not the model's knowledge. Recent work uses planted test cases: models fine-tuned to answer wrongly in some contexts, or to keep a secret. In October 2025, a simple black-box trick (prefilling the start of the model's answer) beat interpretability tools at extracting such secrets.

Key results

  • An unsupervised method found a direction in activations that answers yes/no questions consistently, beating zero-shot accuracy by about 4% on average and holding up when models were prompted to answer wrongly. Collin Burns, Haotian Ye, Dan Klein, Jacob Steinhardt, 2022
  • Such unsupervised methods provably can be satisfied by arbitrary features, and in experiments they often found a prominent feature rather than the model's knowledge. Sebastian Farquhar, Vikrant Varma, Zachary Kenton, Johannes Gasteiger, Vladimir Mikulik, Rohin Shah, 2023
  • On 'quirky' models trained to answer wrongly whenever a prompt mentions 'Bob', middle-layer probes recovered 89% of the gap between truthful and untruthful contexts, and an anomaly detector flagged untruthful behaviour with 0.95 AUROC. Alex Mallen, Madeline Brumley, Julia Kharchenko, Nora Belrose, 2023
  • At sufficient scale, models represent whether a statement is true along a roughly linear direction, and pushing along it makes the model treat false statements as true. Samuel Marks, Max Tegmark, 2023
  • Four text benchmarks for detecting measurement tampering, framed as a specific case of ELK, showed current techniques beat simple baselines on most datasets but do not reach maximum performance. Fabien Roger, Ryan Greenblatt, Max Nadeau, Buck Shlegeris, Nate Thomas, 2023
  • On models trained to use a secret while denying it, prefill attacks extracted the secret best; logit-lens and sparse-autoencoder methods helped but less. Bartosz Cywiński, Emil Ryd, Rowan Wang, Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Neel Nanda, Arthur Conmy, Samuel Marks, 2025

Criticisms

  • Current unsupervised methods for discovering latent knowledge are insufficient: they find whatever feature of the activations is most prominent, and the identification problem will likely persist for future methods. Sebastian Farquhar, Rohin Shah et al. (Google DeepMind)
  • ARC's own route to scalable anomaly detection is mainly theoretical or tested only in very toy settings, so a broad empirical approach should run in parallel. Erik Jenner, Viktor Rehnberg, Oliver Daniels

Who works on it

  • Alignment Research Center (ARC): Defined ELK, ran the 2022 ELK prize, and now works on a theoretical foundation for mechanistic explanations of neural network behaviour; its mechanistic anomaly detection work grew out of ELK. ARC's first technical report: Eliciting Latent Knowledge (2021)
  • EleutherAI: Built 'quirky' language models as an empirical ELK testbed. Eliciting Latent Knowledge from Quirky Language Models (2023)
  • Redwood Research: Built benchmarks for measurement tampering detection, which it frames as a specific case of ELK. Benchmarks for Detecting Measurement Tampering (2023)
  • Google DeepMind: Tested unsupervised knowledge discovery and showed its main failure mode. Challenges with unsupervised LLM knowledge discovery (2023)

Status

In their own words

In these cases, the prediction model "knows" facts (like "the camera was tampered with") that are not visible on camera but would change our evaluation of the predicted future if we learned them. How can we train this model to report its latent knowledge of off-screen events? We'll call this problem eliciting latent knowledge (ELK).

Paul Christiano, Ajeya Cotra, Mark Xu (Alignment Research Center), Eliciting latent knowledge: How to tell if your eyes deceive you, 2021

We can fine-tune this model to also answer questions about the predictor's latent knowledge by adding a second "head" (which we'll call the reporter) that shares all the "figure out what's going on" computation with the predictor ... However, we can train the reporter to answer questions in simpler situations where humans can be confident about what happened.

Paul Christiano, Ajeya Cotra, Mark Xu (Alignment Research Center), Eliciting latent knowledge: How to tell if your eyes deceive you, 2021
