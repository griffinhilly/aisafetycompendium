Safety cases

A structured, auditable argument that a system is acceptably safe, borrowed from nuclear and aviation.

Open in the compendium

A safety case is a written, structured argument, backed by evidence, that a particular AI system is safe enough to use in a particular setting. The idea comes from industries such as aviation and nuclear power, where operators must convince a regulator before they run a plant or fly a plane. For AI, safety cases would turn scattered test results into one claim that a reviewer can check and challenge.

How it works

A safety case starts from a top-level claim, such as "deploying this model does not pose unacceptable risk." It breaks that claim into smaller sub-claims, and ties each sub-claim to evidence such as test results. Many authors use a notation called Claims-Arguments-Evidence for this tree. Joshua Clymer and co-authors (March 2024) sorted the possible arguments into four kinds: the system is unable to cause a catastrophe; control measures are strong enough to stop it; the system is trustworthy even though it is capable; and, for much more powerful systems, deference to AI advisors whose credibility has been established.

The simplest kind is an inability argument. In November 2024, GovAI and the UK AI Safety Institute published a template showing how a developer could argue that a model lacks dangerous cyber-offence ability. The template moves from risk models, to proxy tasks, to test settings, to test results. A control argument looks different. In January 2025, the UK AI Security Institute and Redwood Research sketched a case that an internal AI agent will not leak sensitive data. Its evidence is a test in which a red team builds deliberately misbehaving models and tries to get past the monitoring. That case rests on three claims: the red team drew out the model's full ability, the safeguards work as well in real use as in the test, and the developer extrapolates cautiously from test to deployment.

Safety cases now appear in company policies. Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework requires a safety case review before external launch when a model reaches a Critical Capability Level, and since September 2025 also before large internal deployments of models with advanced AI research ability. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy requires an "affirmative case" on misalignment risk once a model reaches its AI R&D-4 threshold. The EU's General-Purpose AI Code of Practice (July 2025) asks signatories to give regulators "a detailed justification for why the systemic risks stemming from the model are acceptable."

Where research stands

Most published work is still sketches and templates, not cases that actually decided a release. There are a few real-world tests. In 2025 Google DeepMind published a scheming inability case for Gemini 2.5, arguing that the model could not cause harm even if it were misaligned, because it lacks the needed stealth and situational awareness. In October 2025 Anthropic published a pilot sabotage risk report for Claude Opus 4. Anthropic called it a risk report rather than a safety case, because it does not claim a pre-set risk level was met, and METR reviewed it independently. In April 2026 an outside team reviewed the Google DeepMind case and found it unclear whether the case informed the deployment decision at all.

What works: inability arguments are clear and testable for today's models, and publishing cases lets outsiders find gaps. What does not work yet: arguments for models that could cause harm if they tried. These need control or trustworthiness arguments, which depend on unsolved problems such as detecting sandbagging (deliberate underperformance on tests) and proving that safeguards hold up against a scheming model. GovAI's authors say inability arguments are unlikely to stay the main focus, because systems will eventually cross dangerous capability thresholds. They also say reviewing cases needs a body with expertise in frontier AI and secure handling of sensitive information, and suggest starting with provisional bodies that give advice.

Key results

  • Proposed a framework for organising AI safety cases around four argument types: inability, control, trustworthiness and deference to credible AI advisors. Joshua Clymer, Nick Gabrieli, David Krueger, Thomas Larsen, 2024
  • Showed, as a proof of concept, how to make the implicit argument behind cyber capability testing explicit, using a Claims-Arguments-Evidence tree from risk models down to test results. Arthur Goemans, Marie Davidsen Buhl, Jonas Schuett, Tomek Korbak, Jessica Wang, Benjamin Hilton, Geoffrey Irving (GovAI and UK AI Safety Institute), 2024
  • Sketched a control safety case for an internal AI agent and showed that it depends on three claims: adequate red-team elicitation, safeguards that work as well in deployment, and cautious extrapolation. Tomek Korbak, Joshua Clymer, Benjamin Hilton, Buck Shlegeris, Geoffrey Irving (UK AI Security Institute and Redwood Research), 2025
  • Anthropic's pilot report on Claude Opus 4 found a "very low, but not completely negligible" risk of sabotage; METR's independent review agreed risk was low but judged parts of the argument possibly overconfident. Anthropic, 2025
  • Google DeepMind published a scheming inability safety case whose top claim is that "an AI could not cause harm even if it was misaligned", based on stealth and situational-awareness tests of five models. Mary Phuong, Roland S. Zimmermann, Ziyue Wang, et al. (Google DeepMind), 2025
  • An external review of that case found unclear scope, unpublished hazard and system models, unresolved counter-arguments, and no evidence that the model was not sandbagging. Stephen Barrett, Francisco Javier Campos Zabala, Sean P. Fillingham, Umair Siddique, James Walpole, Robin Bloomfield, Henry Papadatos, 2026

Open problems

  • Inability arguments are unlikely to stay the main basis for safety cases once systems cross dangerous capability thresholds, so other kinds of argument will be needed. source
  • It is unclear what level of confidence should attach to a safety case's top claim, and putting a number on that confidence is hard. source
  • Many assumptions behind safety cases against scheming have not yet been confidently met and need progress on several open research problems. source
  • Safety cases need review bodies with expertise, secure information handling and checks and balances; GovAI suggests starting with provisional bodies that give advice. source
  • Alignment safety cases built on methods such as debate rest on unproven links, for example that winning debates implies a model is honest. source

Criticisms

  • If transformative AI arrives within about four years, companies are unlikely (under 20%) to make high-assurance safety cases, because the needed security and scheming mitigations are too hard. Ryan Greenblatt
  • A developer writing its own safety case faces confirmation bias and conflicted incentives, so self-authored cases need independent external review. Stephen Barrett and co-authors (Arcadia Impact AI Governance Taskforce; SaferAI)
  • The alignment community's safety-case approaches have significant limitations and vary significantly from long-established assurance practice in safety-critical industries. Shaun Feakins, Ibrahim Habli, Phillip Morgan (University of York)
  • Even in mature industries, safety-case practice suffers from confirmation bias, after-the-fact assurance and cases treated as paperwork. Richard Hawkins

Who works on it

  • Centre for the Governance of AI (GovAI): Wrote the main policy case for frontier AI safety cases and co-wrote the cyber inability template. Safety cases for frontier AI (2024)
  • UK AI Security Institute: Runs a safety-case research programme: templates, control and debate-based sketches, and links to the Frontier AI Safety Commitments. Safety Cases: A Scalable Approach to Frontier AI Safety (2025)
  • Redwood Research: Co-wrote a control safety case sketch with the UK AI Security Institute. A sketch of an AI control safety case (2025)
  • Anthropic (Alignment Science team): Published hypothetical ASL-4 safety case sketches and a pilot sabotage risk report; its policy requires an affirmative misalignment case at AI R&D-4. Three Sketches of ASL-4 Safety Case Components (2024)
  • Google DeepMind: Requires safety case reviews when Critical Capability Levels are reached and published a scheming inability safety case. Strengthening our Frontier Safety Framework (2025)
  • Meta: Its October 2026 framework uses safety cases, with extra threat modelling, to decide whether a flagged model is at the high or critical tier. Meta Superintelligence Scaling Framework, Version 2.1 (2026)
  • Apollo Research: Led a paper, co-written with the UK AI Safety Institute, METR, Redwood Research and UC Berkeley, on how evaluations could support safety cases against scheming. Towards evaluations-based safety cases for AI scheming (2024)
  • METR: Independently reviewed Anthropic's pilot sabotage risk report. Review of the Anthropic Summer 2025 Pilot Sabotage Risk Report (2025)
  • Arcadia Impact (AI Governance Taskforce): Reviewed Google DeepMind's public scheming inability safety case. Lessons from External Review of DeepMind's Scheming Inability Safety Case (2026)
  • European Commission (AI Office): Published the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, whose model reports must justify why systemic risks are acceptable. The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice (2025)

In their own words

One way to do so is via safety cases: reports that make a structured argument, supported by evidence, that a system is safe enough in a given operational context. Safety cases are already common in other safety-critical industries such as aviation and nuclear power.

Marie Davidsen Buhl, Gaurav Sett, Leonie Koessler, Jonas Schuett, Markus Anderljung (GovAI), Safety cases for frontier AI, 2024

We propose a framework for organizing a safety case and discuss four categories of arguments to justify safety: total inability to cause a catastrophe, sufficiently strong control measures, trustworthiness despite capability to cause harm, and -- if AI systems become much more powerful -- deference to credible AI advisors.

Joshua Clymer, Nick Gabrieli, David Krueger, Thomas Larsen, Safety Cases: How to Justify the Safety of Advanced AI Systems, 2024
