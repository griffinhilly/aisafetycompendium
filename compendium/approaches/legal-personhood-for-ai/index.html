Legal personhood and standing for AI

Give some AI systems a legal status, such as the right to make contracts, hold property or sue.

Open in the compendium

This approach asks whether some AI systems should get a legal status of their own, such as the power to sign contracts, own property or sue. Legal personhood is a legal category, not a claim about minds: corporations are legal persons without being conscious. The safety case, made most fully by Peter Salib and Simon Goldstein, is that giving capable AI systems a stake in lawful trade could make peaceful cooperation a better strategy for them than conflict with humans.

How it works

Salib and Goldstein start from a game-theoretic model (a mathematical model of strategic choices). Under today's law, an AI system is property: it can own nothing, and its owner can modify or delete it. They argue that a capable AI with goals that differ from ours would then face a prisoner's dilemma with humans, in which each side's best move is to disempower the other first. If the AI could instead make enforceable contracts, hold property and bring tort claims (lawsuits for harm), humans and AIs could gain from many small, repeated trades, and cooperation would become the better strategy. They propose these private-law rights, as corporations have, not political rights such as voting.

Other scholars take different routes. Joel Leibo and colleagues at Google DeepMind treat personhood as a 'bundle of obligations' that societies can split up and assign for practical reasons, for example to create an accountable party that can be sanctioned when an AI agent signs a contract. Heather Alexander, Jonathan Simon and Frédéric Pinard compare keeping AI as objects of law, creating corporation-like 'fictional' persons, and recognising some future systems as full legal persons with their own identity.

Where research stands

This is a live debate in legal scholarship with no legal adoption so far, and lawmakers are moving the other way. Idaho's statute says artificial intelligence 'shall not be granted personhood', and a Utah law in force since 1 May 2024 bars any government entity from granting or recognising legal personhood in AI. In 2023 North Dakota amended its legal definition of 'person' to exclude artificial intelligence, and bills to bar AI personhood were introduced in Ohio in September 2025 and in Oklahoma in January 2026. An earlier European proposal stalled: a 2017 European Parliament resolution floated an 'electronic personality' for advanced robots, and in 2018 more than 150 robotics, AI and legal experts signed an open letter to the European Commission opposing it.

The scholarship is divided. Nadia Banteka's analysis of US court decisions from 1809 onward finds that the conditions courts use for legal personhood do not fit AI. Joanna Bryson and co-authors argue that AI personhood would be morally unnecessary and legally troublesome, mainly because it would be hard to hold an AI accountable. Supporters reply that the question is not whether AI is like a human, but which narrow bundle of rights and duties would make society safer.

Key results

  • Formal game-theoretic models suggest that under current law humans and misaligned AGIs are trapped in a prisoner's dilemma, and that giving AIs basic private-law rights to contract, hold property and sue could shift both sides toward a peaceful equilibrium. Peter N. Salib and Simon Goldstein, 2026
  • A statistical analysis of US judicial decisions conferring legal personhood on artificial entities from 1809 onward finds that courts' conditions for personhood are incompatible with AI entities. Nadia Banteka, 2021
  • Conferring legal personhood on purely synthetic entities would be morally unnecessary and legally troublesome, because holding such 'electronic persons' to account would be difficult. Joanna J. Bryson, Mihailis E. Diamantis, Thomas D. Grant, 2017
  • Treating AI as objects of law is adequate for 2025 systems, but for some advanced future systems full ('non-fictional') legal personhood may be the most coherent option, while corporation-like fictional personhood would not be durable. Heather J. Alexander, Jonathan A. Simon, Frédéric Pinard, 2025
  • Personhood can be treated as a bundle of obligations that can be unbundled and tailored, so tools such as AI contracting can be built without first settling whether AI is conscious. Joel Z. Leibo, Alexander Sasha Vezhnevets, William A. Cunningham, Stanley M. Bileschi, 2025

Open problems

  • Which AI systems would qualify, and how to define an individual AI when one model runs as many copies and changes through training, is unresolved. source
  • Granting AI legal standing could let developers shift blame onto their systems, so any such scheme must settle how liability is split between an AI and the company that built it. source
  • Whether private-law rights would be secure without political rights is debated: a government could in principle tax AI property into irrelevance, though Salib and Goldstein argue political rights are probably superfluous. source
  • State nonpersonhood statutes conflict with leading jurisprudential theories of personhood, and more such legislation is likely as advocacy for nonhuman entities grows. source

Criticisms

  • Electronic personhood rests on the incorrect claim that damage liability would otherwise be impossible to prove, and on an overvaluation of the actual capabilities of even the most advanced robots. Signatories of the 2018 open letter to the European Commission on artificial intelligence and robotics
  • Courts' existing tests for legal personhood do not fit AI, and conferring it would undermine legal certainty. Nadia Banteka
  • Granting personhood to AI could let developers argue they are not responsible for what their programs do. Michael Froomkin (University of Miami), quoted by Straight Arrow News
  • AI is a man-made tool that should have no more rights than a hammer. Oklahoma state representative Cody Maynard, sponsor of a 2026 anti-personhood bill

Status

Debated in legal scholarship. Banteka argues US case law does not support AI personhood. Salib and Goldstein argue for basic private law rights. Alexander and colleagues suggest some future systems may be best treated as legal persons. Idaho, Utah and North Dakota have passed laws that bar AI personhood, and by February 2026 lawmakers in Oklahoma and Ohio had filed similar bills.

In their own words

The Article contends that one surprising legal change could help to reduce catastrophic risk: AI rights. Not just any rights will do. To promote human safety, AIs should be given the basic private law rights already enjoyed by other non-human agents, like corporations.

Peter N. Salib and Simon Goldstein, AI Rights for Human Safety, 2026

Granting these rights would enable humans and AIs to engage in iterated, small-scale, mutually beneficial transactions. This, we show, changes humans' and AIs' optimal game-theoretic strategies, encouraging a peaceful strategic equilibrium. The reasons are familiar from human affairs. In the long run, cooperative trade generates immense value, while violence destroys it.

Peter N. Salib and Simon Goldstein, AI Rights for Human Safety, 2026
