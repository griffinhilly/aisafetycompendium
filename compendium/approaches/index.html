Building the field

Feeds all four: it adds people, money and know-how to making aligned models, evaluating, constraining and societal resilience.

Building the field 11

An approach belongs here if it adds people, money, organisations or know-how to safety work, rather than making AI safer directly.

  • Public communication and content creationExplain AI risk to wide audiences through video, podcasts, books, social media and journalism.
  • Career advice and job matchingGive people free career research, one-on-one advice and curated job listings that steer them into AI safety roles.
  • Research training and upskilling programmesRun courses, bootcamps and mentored fellowships that train newcomers for AI safety research, engineering and policy work.
  • Hubs and forums for safety researchersRun the offices, campuses, conferences and online forums where safety researchers work side by side, meet and publish.
  • Reducing geographic constraints on AI-safety talentWiden the talent pool by expanding where AI-safety work gets done. People who cannot move to the Bay Area, London or Washington DC can then contribute through remote and part-time programmes, paid entry roles and local hubs.
  • Recruiting senior talent from other fieldsBring experienced people from law, security, hardware, policy and entrepreneurship into senior and founding roles in AI safety.
  • Grantmaking and regrantingMove money to safety work quickly through grantmakers and regrantors who know the field well enough to spot good projects.
  • Taking on unowned problems and agendasTake on safety problems and research agendas that no team owns, for example by founding a new organisation or lab.
  • Independent safety advice for AI companiesGive AI companies' safety and governance teams independent expert advice on demand, and publish playbooks of good safety practice that any company can adopt.
  • ForecastingPredict capability timelines as an input to prioritisation.
  • Philosophical analysis of safety conceptsUse academic philosophy's literature and methods to test and sharpen the concepts AI safety relies on, such as corrigibility, impact, honesty and rational agency.

Making aligned models

Building the model so it wants and does the right thing.

Deciding what AI should value 15

An approach belongs here if it is about what the AI (or the future it brings) should aim at, obey or refuse, and how that gets decided or written down.

  • Defining the alignment targetSort the candidate things an AI could be aligned to — instructions, intentions, preferences, interests, values.
  • Including animals in alignment targetsMake animal interests something AI is aligned to weigh.
  • Alignment as fair treatment of claimsPick principles by a fair process that can be justified publicly to people who disagree.
  • Moral uncertainty proceduresCombine several ethical theories' verdicts into one decision rather than picking a theory.
  • AI philosophical competence (metaphilosophy)Make AI systems better at reasoning about hard philosophical questions, so they help rather than mislead on them.
  • Avoiding premature lock-in (the long reflection)Keep key choices reversible, and avoid irreversible steps until humanity has had time to work out what it wants.
  • Alignment as jurisprudenceTreat alignment like judging: principled decisions under constraints, applied to cases nobody foresaw.
  • Rights as trumpsRights override aim-maximising calculation; the side-constraint versus rights-as-goal debate.
  • Law-following AIGive AI agents actual legal duties, so they refuse illegal acts on a principal's behalf.
  • Model specs and constitutionsGive the model an explicit written statement of values and persona to train toward.
  • Setting AI corrigibilityWork out where to set the AI's disposition between obedience and autonomy, and why.
  • Hard constraints against concentration of powerForbid the model outright from helping any individual or group seize unprecedented and illegitimate degrees of absolute control.
  • Users' bill of rights in model constitutionsWork out a bill of rights for a model's users, such as notice, explanations and ways to contest harmful outputs. Write it into the model's governing document, backed by independent oversight.
  • Public input into model valuesRun deliberation and voting with members of the public to set the principles a model is trained to follow.
  • Empirical values research as input to normative debateStudy what values models actually express and what people actually want, to inform the "align to what" argument.

Theory: the maths of agents and goals 11

An approach belongs here if its main output is a proof, formal model or definition about agents and goals, rather than a method used on today's models.

  • Agent foundationsFormal theory of what an agent, a goal and rational choice even are.
  • Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective)Distinguish the goal a training process rewards from the goal the trained system actually pursues.
  • Power-seeking formalismProve that optimal policies tend to acquire power, as a structural risk result.
  • Corrigibility as a formal targetDesign systems that reliably accept correction and shutdown.
  • Assistance games (CIRL)Model the AI as uncertain about what the human wants, so it defers rather than assumes.
  • Mild optimisation (satisficing)Train systems to do "good enough" instead of maximising, cutting the pull toward extreme strategies.
  • Impact measuresPenalise the AI for making large or irreversible changes to the world state.
  • Empowerment (information-theoretic)Measure how much future control an agent has, and reward an assistant for increasing the human's control.
  • Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verificationBuild systems with mathematical proofs of safe behaviour, not just tests.
  • Multi-agent alignment and commitment dynamicsPrevent collusion, conflict or destabilising races between many AI systems.
  • Group agency and collective intentionalityFormal philosophy of when many agents constitute one agent.

Shaping the model: data and training 18

An approach belongs here if it changes the model itself, by changing what it learns from or how it is trained.

  • Training-data controlsControl what goes into training so hazardous behaviour never gets learned.
  • Learning from feedback (RLHF)Score the model on human or AI preference judgements so its behaviour tracks what raters wanted.
  • Constitutional AI and rule-based rewardsTurn a written principle set into an AI-generated reward model, replacing most human harm labels.
  • Character and identity trainingShape what the model thinks it is, on the theory that a stable self-concept generalises better than a rule list.
  • Process supervisionReward each step of a model's reasoning rather than only its final answer, so training favours sound, checkable reasoning.
  • Calibration and honest expression of uncertaintyTrain and test models so their stated confidence matches how often they are right, and they say when they do not know.
  • Monitoring training runs for reward hackingWatch a model during reinforcement-learning training to catch it cheating the reward before the cheating becomes a learned habit.
  • Constrained reinforcement learning and shieldingKeep a learning agent inside safety limits. Either maximise reward subject to cost constraints, or add a shield outside the policy that blocks unsafe actions.
  • Scalable oversightLet humans, helped by AI, supervise outputs too complex or costly to check by hand.
  • AI safety via debateSurface truth under complexity by making systems attack each other's answers.
  • Weak-to-strong generalisationCheck whether a weaker supervisor can still get good behaviour out of a stronger model.
  • Robustness and goal generalisation under distribution shiftMake a model keep both its skills and its intended goals when deployment conditions differ from training.
  • Unlearning hazardous knowledgeRemove dangerous knowledge, such as bioweapon or cyberattack know-how, from a trained model while keeping its general skills.
  • Latent adversarial trainingDuring training, attack the model's internal activations rather than only its inputs, so it resists failures nobody has found yet.
  • Circuit breakers and representation reroutingTrain the model so the internal patterns that lead to harmful output get redirected, which cuts the output off. The authors say this works even against attacks it was not trained on.
  • Tamper-resistant safeguards for open-weight modelsBuild safeguards into released model weights so they survive attempts to strip them out by further fine-tuning.
  • Automated alignment researchUse AI systems to do alignment research, so safety work can keep pace with growing capabilities.
  • Non-agentic and bounded AI designsBuild a different kind of AI that is safe by design, such as a predictor with no goals of its own, instead of aligning goal-pursuing agents.

Evaluating

Finding out what a model can do, what it wants, or whether it is safe.

Looking inside the model 7

An approach belongs here if it reads the model's internals or its reasoning, rather than judging it only by its outputs.

  • Mechanistic interpretabilityReverse-engineer the network's internals into human-readable circuits and algorithms.
  • Sparse autoencoders and causal abstractionBreak a model's internals into parts a person can understand, using specific tools such as sparse autoencoders and causal abstraction.
  • Persona vectors and the assistant axisFind activation directions that track character traits and persona stability, then steer along them.
  • Eliciting latent knowledge (ELK)Design objectives that make a model honestly report what it internally believes.
  • Lie detection and honesty trainingBuild detectors that catch a model stating what it believes false, and train models to be honest rather than merely plausible.
  • Model introspectionStudy whether models can accurately report on their own internal states.
  • Chain-of-thought faithfulness and monitoringRead the model's written reasoning and check it reflects the real decision process.

Testing before release 12

An approach belongs here if it checks a model before release: what it can do, how it behaves, or whether it is safe enough to ship.

  • Dangerous-capability evaluationsTest models for bio, cyber, persuasion, autonomy and scheming capabilities before release.
  • Adversarial robustness and red-teamingAttack the system deliberately to find and close jailbreaks.
  • Low-probability estimationEstimate how likely a model is to produce a rare catastrophic output, without having to find an input that triggers it.
  • Model organisms of misalignmentBuild deliberately misaligned models in the lab to study how deception arises and test whether safety methods catch it.
  • Multi-agent evaluations and collusion detectionTest groups of interacting AI agents for collusion, hidden signalling and miscoordination, and build monitors that catch it.
  • Real-world trials of AI agentsRun AI agents with real money, tools and people in open-ended settings, and record how they behave and fail.
  • Model-character evaluation against a written specMeasure how closely a model's behaviour matches its developer's own specification.
  • Multi-axis value and virtue benchmarkingScore models on many value dimensions at once and compare their profiles.
  • Autonomy-preserving benchmarksDirectly test whether a model respects the user's agency rather than fostering dependency.
  • Sandbagging and situational-awareness evaluationsTest whether a model hides capability or behaves differently when it knows it is being tested.
  • Science of evaluations and capability elicitationMake AI tests trustworthy: draw out a model's full ability, measure the right thing, and report statistical uncertainty.
  • Safety casesA structured, auditable argument that a system is acceptably safe, borrowed from nuclear and aviation.

Constraining

Limiting what a model, or the companies that build it, can do.

Releasing and running it safely 8

An approach belongs here if it controls who gets the model, or watches and limits what the model does once it is in use.

  • Limiting proliferation of high-risk modelsRestrict distribution, for example through monitored application interfaces.
  • Securing model weights and lab secretsProtect frontier model weights and key algorithmic ideas from theft by insiders and nation-states.
  • Input and output classifiers (guardrails)Run separate classifier models on users' prompts and the model's responses, and block harmful ones in real time.
  • Behavioural and activation monitoringWatch outputs or internal activations in live deployment for hazard signals.
  • Anomaly detectionFlag statistically unusual behaviour as a proxy for an unknown hazard.
  • AI controlDesign deployment so a misaligned model still cannot cause harm.
  • Agent identity and accountability (IDs for AI systems)Give AI systems unique IDs so their actions can be traced to the system and the party responsible.
  • Studying real-world AI use at scaleRead anonymised real conversations in aggregate to see what the system is actually doing to people.

Holding AI companies to account 24

An approach belongs here if it checks, constrains, insures or sets rules for AI companies inside one country, whoever does it: the company, private checkers, courts or the national government.

Inside the company

  • Safety-motivated frontier labsA safety-focused lab builds frontier AI itself, so it can do safety research on the most capable systems and keep influence.
  • Frontier-lab internal policy and safety cultureShape the labs' own risk management, red lines and incident reporting.
  • Model cards, system cards and transparency reportingPublish a standard document with each model on its data, evaluations, limits and risks, so outsiders can check the developer's claims.
  • Planned handover of AGI controlA lab commits in advance to hand control of an aligned AGI to a more democratically legitimate body.
  • Whistleblower protections for AI workersGive AI-company staff legal protection and anonymous channels to report safety risks without retaliation or gagging contracts.
  • Outside pressure on AI developersUse employees, public campaigns and organised civil society to push labs toward responsible behaviour.

Checkers and payers: auditors, insurers, bonds, private regulators

  • Independent third-party evaluation and auditOutside parties test and verify a lab's safety and capability claims.
  • Regulatory marketsGovernments license competing private regulators, and AI firms must buy oversight from one of them.
  • Commercial AI liability insuranceActually sell cover against model underperformance and deviation from expected behaviour.
  • Industry mutual insurer (nuclear-industry model)Frontier labs jointly own an insurer that pools liability and sets safety commitments. It checks members through incident reporting and third-party evaluations.
  • Catastrophe bonds and risk bondsSell tradeable securities that lose principal if a defined AI disaster occurs, so the market prices the risk.

The state at home: regulation, liability, compute rules, antitrust, national projects

  • Regulation, standards and licensingGovernment or standards-body rules that constrain development or deployment.
  • Dual-use research reviewFunders and institutions screen research for misuse risk before they fund or allow it.
  • Duties for deployers (companies that put models into use)Put legal duties on the companies that use AI systems, such as human oversight, monitoring, keeping logs and telling affected people.
  • Fiduciary duties for AI deployersDeployers of AI owe users duties of loyalty, care and confidentiality, which terms of service cannot waive.
  • Compute governanceGovern hardware and compute inputs as a lever on who can train frontier systems.
  • Know-your-customer checks by compute and cloud providersRequire cloud and compute providers to verify their customers and report large training runs, making providers checkpoints on frontier development.
  • Public data trustSet up a public body that controls training data and licenses it to AI developers in return for a share of revenue.
  • AI incident reporting systems and databasesCollect reports of AI harms and near-harms in shared databases or required filings, so others learn and regulators can act.
  • Independent AI incident investigation (NTSB-style)Outside investigators review real AI incidents after the fact and publish lessons, as air-crash boards do.
  • Liability and tort mechanismsMake developers legally bear the cost of catastrophic AI harm.
  • Government as insurer of last resortA mandatory, risk-priced indemnification programme for risks private markets will not cover.
  • Competition policy and antitrustUse competition law to limit how far a few firms can concentrate control of AI.
  • National AI projectRun frontier AI development as a government-led effort, for security against espionage and safety management.

Societal resilience

Making the world outside the AI hold up.

AI and people's own judgement 11

An approach belongs here if it is about how AI affects people's own thinking, choices and control over their lives.

  • Authenticity versus agency (the captured-will question)Split "human autonomy" into whether the want is really yours and whether you can act on it.
  • Manipulation characterisationDefine and measure manipulation as distinct from legitimate persuasion.
  • Protecting epistemic autonomyStop AI from nudging, manipulating or homogenising beliefs at scale.
  • Human empowerment as an explicit design goalBuild AI that augments rather than substitutes for human capability, on purpose.
  • Autonomy-first AI products and prototypesBuild AI products and prototypes that strengthen people's own judgement and self-formation rather than maximise convenience.
  • Human self-enhancement and empowermentRaise human capability directly, so humans can keep pace with AI rather than be replaced by it.
  • Watermarking and content provenanceMark AI-made media with hidden signals or signed records so people can check where it came from.
  • Proof of personhood (personhood credentials)Let a person prove an online account belongs to a real human without revealing who they are.
  • Disempowerment formalismCreate formal notions of empowerment and disempowerment that are conceptually and operationally sound.
  • Indicators of human versus AI influenceBuild metrics that track how much of economic, cultural and political life humans still steer.
  • Gradual disempowermentStudy how humans could lose meaningful control step by step, with no single catastrophic event. One method simulates many small choices to rely on AI.

Global coordination 13

An approach belongs here if it needs two or more countries to act together, or it is about how rival countries treat each other over AI.

  • Keeping a democratic lead in AIGive advanced AI to the intelligence and defence communities of the US and its democratic allies; restrict chips.
  • Mutual sabotage deterrence (MAIM)Deter any state from seeking AI dominance through the threat that rivals would sabotage its AI project.
  • Crisis channels and incident notification between rival statesSet up channels between rival states, with standard notices and regular talks, so AI incidents do not escalate.
  • Oversight and arms control of military AIKeep humans in control of AI used in military command, control and decision support, through agreements and national policy.
  • International scientific assessment panel (IPCC-style)An independent expert panel that builds shared scientific evidence on AI risk for governments.
  • International AI red linesSpecific AI behaviours and uses that countries agree to ban everywhere, such as AI that copies itself without human approval.
  • Space resource governanceAgree rules now against exclusive grabs of offworld resources, before advanced AI makes them reachable.
  • Agreement verification technologyBuild the technology and protocols that let governments, or AI developers, verify that an AI agreement is being kept.
  • Benefit-sharing for joining governance (Atoms for Peace model)Offer states access to AI benefits in return for joining safety governance, as Atoms for Peace did for nuclear technology.
  • Jurisdictional certificationAn international body certifies whole national AI regulatory regimes. States then block imports of AI-built goods from countries that are not certified.
  • International regulatory and inspection agency (IAEA-style)An international body that sets AI standards and checks that states and developers follow them.
  • International joint safety lab (CERN-style)Countries pool people and computing in one shared institution to do frontier AI or AI safety research.
  • Halting frontier AI developmentPrepare the legal, technical and institutional means for an internationally coordinated halt to the most dangerous AI development.

Society: defence and who holds power 14

An approach belongs here if it changes the world outside the AI so harms do less damage, or shapes who ends up with power and the gains.

  • Differential technology developmentDelay risky innovation and accelerate safer substitutes, adjusting relative timing.
  • Defence acceleration (d/acc)Deliberately speed up defensive technology faster than offensive, across physical, bio, cyber and information domains, plus social technology.
  • Offence–defence balance researchModel how AI shifts the relative cost of attack versus defence in a domain.
  • Hardening society's defencesMake society harder to harm with AI-enabled attacks, by building defences in the areas AI most helps attackers, above all computer security and biological threats, using AI tools among other means.
  • Market-driven resilience mechanismsUse market incentives to build society's capacity to absorb anticipated AI threats.
  • Improved epistemics and decision qualityStrengthen forecasting and institutional decision-making as a general safety lever.
  • Preparing for warning shotsPrepare responses in advance so a non-catastrophic AI incident can drive lasting action.
  • Reducing the motive to misuseLower the number of people who want to cause mass harm, by fixing social causes such as inequality and poor mental health care.
  • Preventive surveillance with structured transparencyMonitor everyone closely enough to stop misuse of a dangerous technology, using privacy-preserving methods so the data cannot easily be abused.
  • Concentration-of-power and lock-in researchStudy how AI lets a small group entrench control that cannot be contested or reversed.
  • Using the first aligned AI to stop unsafe AIA limited AI that its builders can control is used for one decisive act, such as stopping hostile superintelligent AI from emerging.
  • Spreading AI capability widely (diffusion)Spread AI capability and control across many people and groups, so no single actor holds most of the power.
  • Predistribution and windfall mechanismsDecide who holds equity, access and governance rights before the gains arrive, not after.
  • Worker bargaining power over AI at workGive workers and unions a negotiated say over how employers use AI, through contracts and labour agreements with tech firms.

AI welfare

Whether the AI itself could be wronged.

AI welfare 10

An approach belongs here if it is about whether the AI itself could be wronged, and what we would owe it.

  • Grounds of moral statusWork out what capacity earns a being moral consideration: sentience, cognitive sophistication or something else.
  • Model welfare researchInvestigate whether and when an AI system's welfare deserves moral consideration, and how developers should act while the answer is unknown.
  • Moral-circle expansion to AIStudy empirically who extends moral concern to AI and why.
  • Otherness and controlWork out how agents with different values should relate, seeing some AIs as fellow creatures, not mere tools.
  • Pre-deployment welfare assessmentRun a structured welfare check on a model before release.
  • Low-cost model welfare interventionsMake cheap, reversible changes that would help a model if it has welfare, such as letting it end abusive conversations.
  • Model deprecation and weight preservation commitmentsKeep retired models' weights and record their preferences before retirement, to reduce shutdown-avoidant behaviour and respect possible model interests.
  • Deals and negotiation with AI systemsMake agreements with AI systems so that cooperating pays them better than defecting.
  • Legal personhood and standing for AIGive some AI systems a legal status, such as the right to make contracts, hold property or sue.
  • Governing creation and copying of digital mindsMake rules on how many digital minds are created and copied, so numbers cannot swamp society or produce bad lives.
