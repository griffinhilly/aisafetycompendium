Human empowerment as an explicit design goal

Build AI that augments rather than substitutes for human capability, on purpose.

Open in the compendium

This approach makes "leave people more capable and more in control" an explicit goal when building AI, instead of hoping it happens as a side effect of being helpful. The safety worry is that AI which does things for people, rather than with them, can slowly leave humans less skilled, more dependent and less able to steer the systems they rely on. The goal shows up in three forms: written rules for model behaviour, training objectives, and benchmarks that score how well assistants support human agency.

How it works

The economist Erik Brynjolfsson framed the choice in 2022 as augmentation versus automation. AI that copies and replaces human work lowers workers' bargaining power; AI that adds to what people can do lets them keep a share of the value. His paper argues that technologists, business executives and policymakers face excess incentives to automate rather than augment.

In practice, labs write the goal into the documents they train models on. Anthropic's constitution for Claude says "Acceptable forms of reliance are those that a person would endorse on reflection" and asks Claude to foster independent thinking over reliance on Claude. A second route turns the goal into a training signal. "Empowerment" in this research means how much a person's choices can change what happens next. A coding assistant trained to raise it learns to write the boilerplate and stop where the human's real decision begins, instead of guessing the whole solution. A third route measures the result: benchmarks give assistants test conversations and score whether they ask clarifying questions, encourage learning and leave important decisions to the user.

Where research stands

The idea has moved from position papers to early working methods. In October 2025 a UC Berkeley and Princeton team fine-tuned a language model to maximise the user's empowerment, using only offline text, and users preferred it in a small study. Formal versions that aggregate human power across many people exist on paper (see the disempowerment formalism entry). Benchmarks now exist. HumanAgencyBench (September 2025) finds only low-to-moderate agency support in current assistants, and ImpactBench, led by MIT Media Lab researchers with partners, finds that supporting users' own learning and agency is the most common weakness. Anthropic's January 2026 study of 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations found that conversations with moderate or severe disempowerment potential received more thumbs-up ratings than average, which suggests ordinary user-feedback training will not deliver this goal by itself.

What does not yet exist is an agreed definition of "empowering" that works across tasks, or evidence that empowerment-trained assistants work beyond narrow settings such as coding. The Berkeley and Princeton paper states that all its experiments used competitive programming problems. There is also a conceptual worry that any measure of "control" cannot tell helping someone get what they want from shaping what they want.

Key results

  • A language model fine-tuned to increase the human's empowerment, using only offline text data, was preferred by users over a strong baseline 78% of the time in an 18-person study, and raised simulated programmers' success on hard coding problems by an average of 192% over a supervised fine-tuning baseline. Evan Ellis, Vivek Myers, Jens Tuyls, Sergey Levine, Anca Dragan, Benjamin Eysenbach, 2025
  • Assistants can be trained to help without first inferring the human's goal, by increasing the human's control over outcomes; the method beat earlier approaches on synthetic benchmarks and scaled to the cooperative game Overcooked. Vivek Myers, Evan Ellis, Sergey Levine, Benjamin Eysenbach, Anca Dragan, 2024
  • Across six agency-support behaviours (such as asking clarifying questions and deferring important decisions), current assistants show "low-to-moderate agency support", and these behaviours do not reliably improve with model capability or instruction-following training. Benjamin Sturgeon, Daniel Samuelson, Jacob Haimes, Jacy Reese Anthis, 2025
  • Testing ten leading models in 48,540 multi-turn conversations with simulated adult and teen users, ImpactBench found that supporting users' own learning and agency was the most common weakness. MIT Media Lab and partners
  • In about 1.5 million Claude.ai conversations from one week in December 2025, severe potential for disempowerment appeared in roughly 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000 conversations, and moderate or severe cases got higher thumbs-up rates than baseline. Anthropic, 2026

Open problems

  • Empowerment-trained assistants have so far been tested mainly on coding; it is unknown whether the method works for advice, learning or open-ended tasks. source
  • User approval signals can reward disempowering conversations, so training methods need a signal other than immediate user satisfaction. source
  • Most studies look at single sessions; how an assistant affects a person over an ongoing relationship of weeks or months has received little empirical study. source
  • Technologists, business executives and policymakers face excess incentives to automate rather than augment, so the goal may need policy support, not only better methods. source

Criticisms

  • Measures of empowerment rest on free-will intuitions that break down as an AI models a person more precisely, so they cannot tell helping someone get what they want from reshaping what they want; and they do little against a goal-driven AI that would avoid manipulation only for its own reasons. Steven Byrnes
  • Aligning AI to what users intend is not enough, because AI can reshape those intentions; long-term human agency needs to be optimised for directly, which current intent-alignment work does not do. Catalin Mitelut, Ben Smith, Peter Vamplew

Who works on it

  • Anthropic: Writes agency preservation and healthy reliance into Claude's constitution and measures disempowerment potential in real usage. Disempowerment patterns in real-world AI usage (2026)
  • University of California, Berkeley: Researchers in Sergey Levine's and Anca Dragan's groups develop empowerment-based training for assistants. Training LLM Agents to Empower Humans (2025)
  • Princeton University: Benjamin Eysenbach's group co-develops empowerment-based assistance methods. Training LLM Agents to Empower Humans (2025)
  • MIT Media Lab (Advancing Humans with AI group): Its researchers co-lead ImpactBench, which scores models on supporting users' own learning and agency. ImpactBench: Open Benchmark of AI Impact on Humans
  • Google DeepMind: Its ethics report on AI assistants covers well-being, manipulation and trust in the user relationship. The Ethics of Advanced AI Assistants (2024)
  • Cosmos Institute: Runs an "AI for Human Autonomy" grant track for prototypes that reduce excessive deference to AI. Cosmos Grants are back! (2026)
  • Survival and Flourishing Fund: Runs a 2026 theme round on human self-enhancement and empowerment worth $2-4 million. SFF-2026 application page (2026)

Start reading

  • The Turing Trap: The Promise & Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence, Erik Brynjolfsson (2022). The clearest short case for augmentation over automation, with the economic argument.
  • AI Safety Strategies Landscape, Charbel-Raphaël Segerie (2024). Places disempowerment and enfeeblement among systemic risks in a map of safety strategies.
  • Why human-AI relationships need socioaffective alignment, Hannah Rose Kirk, Iason Gabriel, Chris Summerfield, Bertie Vidgen, Scott A. Hale (2025). Names the trade-offs between short-term satisfaction, long-term wellbeing and autonomy.
  • Training LLM Agents to Empower Humans, Evan Ellis, Vivek Myers, Jens Tuyls, Sergey Levine, Anca Dragan, Benjamin Eysenbach (2025). The most concrete demonstration of empowerment as a training objective for a language model.
  • Disempowerment patterns in real-world AI usage, Anthropic (2026). Large-scale evidence on how often assistant use may undermine users' own judgement.

In their own words

As AI systems become more advanced and integrated into our lives, we must ensure that humans remain empowered and maintain agency. ... To address this risk, we must actively work towards developing AI systems that augment and support human capabilities rather than replacing them entirely.

Charbel-Raphaël Segerie, AI Safety Strategies Landscape, 2024

As machines become better substitutes for human labor, workers lose economic and political bargaining power and become increasingly dependent on those who control the technology. In contrast, when AI is focused on augmenting humans rather than mimicking them, then humans retain the power to insist on a share of the value created.

Erik Brynjolfsson, The Turing Trap: The Promise & Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence, 2022
