Shaping the model: data and training

Aligned models

Calibration and honest expression of uncertainty

Train and test models so their stated confidence matches how often they are right, and they say when they do not know.

Open in the compendium

Calibration means a model's stated confidence matches how often it is right: when it says "90% sure", it should be right about nine times in ten. This approach trains and tests models to be calibrated and to say "I don't know" when they do not know. It matters for safety because people and oversight systems can only rely on a model if they can tell when to doubt it.

How it works

There are two main ways to get a confidence number from a language model. One reads the model's internal probabilities for its answer. The other simply asks the model to state its confidence in words, such as "90% confidence" or "low confidence". In 2022 researchers at the University of Oxford and OpenAI fine-tuned GPT-3 on arithmetic questions to state such verbal confidences. It stayed moderately calibrated even on harder questions of a different kind.

A second method asks the model to grade itself. Anthropic's 2022 study had models propose an answer and then estimate the probability that it was correct, or estimate whether they knew the answer before trying. Larger models were well calibrated on many multiple-choice and true/false questions in the right format.

Training and testing choices then decide whether calibration survives. In September 2025 OpenAI researchers argued that most benchmarks give zero credit for "I don't know" and the same zero for a wrong answer, so models learn to guess. They proposed telling the model the scoring rule in the prompt, for example: "Answer only if you are >t confident, since mistakes are penalized t/(1-t) points".

Where research stands

The basic picture is well studied. Pretrained models are often well calibrated, but post-training can damage this: OpenAI's GPT-4 technical report (March 2023) showed the pretrained model was highly calibrated, and that "after the post-training process, the calibration is reduced". For such fine-tuned models, a 2023 study by Stanford and Harvard researchers found that asking for confidence in words often gave better calibration than reading the model's probabilities, cutting calibration error by about half in relative terms. But a 2023 study of many prompting methods found verbal confidence tends to be overconfident and that no single method won consistently.

The current focus is on incentives. Kalai and co-authors (2025) frame hallucination, confident false statements, as a result of training and evaluation that "reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty". Their proposed fix is to change how widely used benchmarks score abstentions, rather than to add new hallucination tests. Factuality benchmarks such as OpenAI's SimpleQA (October 2024, 4,326 short questions) grade answers as correct, incorrect or not attempted, so declining to answer can be measured. Whether calibration in short factual questions carries over to long agent tasks is an open question.

Key results

  • Large language models are well calibrated on diverse multiple-choice and true/false questions in the right format, and can be trained to predict whether they know an answer, though this generalises imperfectly to new tasks. Saurav Kadavath et al. (Anthropic, 36 authors), 2022
  • GPT-3 learned to state its confidence in words, and stayed moderately calibrated on harder, differently structured arithmetic questions than it was trained on. Stephanie Lin, Jacob Hilton, Owain Evans, 2022
  • GPT-4's pretrained model was highly calibrated, but calibration was reduced after post-training. OpenAI, 2023
  • For RLHF-tuned models, verbal confidence was typically better calibrated than token probabilities, often cutting expected calibration error by about 50% in relative terms. Katherine Tian, Eric Mitchell, Allan Zhou, Archit Sharma, Rafael Rafailov, Huaxiu Yao, Chelsea Finn, Christopher D. Manning, 2023
  • Models are overconfident when they state confidence in words; sampling consistency and better prompts help, but no method consistently wins and all struggle on expert-knowledge tasks. Miao Xiong, Zhiyuan Hu, Xinyang Lu, Yifei Li, Jie Fu, Junxian He, Bryan Hooi, 2023
  • Hallucinations arise from ordinary statistical pressures plus scoring that rewards guessing; the authors propose stating explicit confidence thresholds and penalties in evaluations. Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh S. Vempala, Edwin Zhang, 2025

Criticisms

  • Adding more hallucination-specific benchmarks will not help while the main leaderboards still reward guessing; the scoring of existing benchmarks must change. Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh S. Vempala, Edwin Zhang
  • Verbal confidence may mirror how humans talk about confidence rather than reflect what the model knows, which makes it systematically overconfident. Miao Xiong, Bryan Hooi and co-authors

Who works on it

  • Anthropic: Showed language models can estimate whether their own answers are correct and whether they know an answer. Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know (2022)
  • OpenAI: Researchers argued benchmark scoring drives hallucination and proposed confidence targets; also built SimpleQA, which credits abstaining. Why Language Models Hallucinate (2025)
  • University of Oxford: Researchers, with an OpenAI co-author, taught GPT-3 to express calibrated uncertainty in words. Teaching Models to Express Their Uncertainty in Words (2022)
  • Stanford University: Researchers showed verbal confidence is better calibrated than token probabilities for RLHF-tuned models. Just Ask for Calibration: Strategies for Eliciting Calibrated Confidence Scores from Language Models Fine-Tuned with Human Feedback (2023)
  • National University of Singapore: Researchers benchmarked many confidence-elicitation methods and found widespread overconfidence. Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty? An Empirical Evaluation of Confidence Elicitation in LLMs (2023)

In their own words

We show that a GPT-3 model can learn to express uncertainty about its own answers in natural language -- without use of model logits. When given a question, the model generates both an answer and a level of confidence (e.g. "90% confidence" or "high confidence"). These levels map to probabilities that are well calibrated.

Stephanie Lin, Jacob Hilton, Owain Evans, Teaching Models to Express Their Uncertainty in Words, 2022

Like students facing hard exam questions, large language models sometimes guess when uncertain, producing plausible yet incorrect statements instead of admitting uncertainty. ... We argue that language models hallucinate because the training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty, and we analyze the statistical causes of hallucinations in the modern training pipeline.

Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh S. Vempala, Edwin Zhang, Why Language Models Hallucinate, 2025
