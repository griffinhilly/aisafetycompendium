Global coordination

Jurisdictional certification

An international body certifies whole national AI regulatory regimes. States then block imports of AI-built goods from countries that are not certified.

Open in the compendium

Jurisdictional certification would have an international body check whole countries' AI rules, not individual companies, against agreed safety standards. Countries that pass get full access to markets and chips; countries that do not face trade barriers. It matters for safety because it could spread minimum safety standards worldwide using trade incentives, without needing a powerful international inspector.

How it works

The idea comes from a 2023 report by Robert Trager and eleven co-authors, published by the Oxford Martin School. States would set up an International AI Organization (IAIO). The IAIO would agree minimum standards, for example licensing for training runs above a compute threshold, and would assess whether each country's national regulator enforces them. Domestic regulators would still oversee their own companies. This keeps company secrets inside each country, acting as a 'firewall' against leaking sensitive technical knowledge.

Enforcement comes from trade. Member states would ban imports of goods whose supply chains include AI from uncertified countries, and would block exports of key inputs such as advanced chips to them. The models are aviation and shipping: the International Civil Aviation Organization and International Maritime Organization set standards, and states refuse planes or ships from places that do not meet them. Another model is the Financial Action Task Force, which places countries with weak anti-money-laundering rules on public 'grey' or 'black' lists. Haydn Belfield (July 2025) proposes a similar link: under a 'Secure Chips Agreement', states without a certified regime could not import advanced chips.

Where research stands

This approach is still a research proposal as of October 2026; no IAIO exists and no country has adopted certification-linked trade rules for AI. It is a niche idea with a small literature, mostly from the original authors and close colleagues. The report argues the trade measures would be compatible on their face with World Trade Organization rules, under exceptions for safety and essential security, and that resting on international standards helps them qualify.

The closest real-world step was national, not international. In January 2025 the US Commerce Department issued the AI Diffusion Rule, which sorted countries into three tiers with different access to advanced AI chips. The rule was rescinded in May 2025, before it took effect. The design questions the report itself leaves open include who governs the IAIO, how to set standards without revealing frontier secrets, and how to stop the regime unravelling if a major market leaves.

Key results

  • An International AI Organization could certify national jurisdictions for compliance with oversight standards, enforced by import bans on AI-embodying goods and export controls on chips for uncertified jurisdictions. Robert Trager, Ben Harack, Anka Reuel, Allison Carnegie, Lennart Heim, Lewis Ho, Sarah Kreps, Ranjit Lall, Owen Larter, Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh, Simon Staffell, José Jaime Villalobos, 2023
  • Participating states' trade policies would give other states an incentive to join and seek certification, so even partial consensus among major players could spread compliance. Robert Trager, Sarah Kreps, Ben Harack (Centre for the Governance of AI), 2023
  • An international agency could harmonise, monitor and certify national frontier AI regulators, with chip imports conditioned on certification. Haydn Belfield, 2025
  • The US AI Diffusion Rule of January 2025 sorted countries into three tiers of access to advanced AI chips, a national form of jurisdiction-based control; it was rescinded in May 2025. Kirkland & Ellis, 2025

Open problems

  • How to set and check standards, such as compute thresholds, that require frontier knowledge without leaking that knowledge to rivals. source
  • How to keep the regime from collapsing if a key market leaves and others follow. source
  • How to give powerful states enough say to join while keeping the body legitimate to everyone else. source
  • How to control trade in software and AI services, which cross borders more easily than physical goods. source

Criticisms

  • A club of aligned countries setting standards together would be faster but would undermine legitimacy and compliance among states left outside. Robert Trager and co-authors (2023), on the club version of their own proposal
  • AI governance led by a few wealthy powers, backed by chip export controls, excludes developing countries and limits their equal access to innovation. Onyango K'Onyango (The Star, Kenya), June 2026

Who works on it

  • Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative: Published the jurisdictional certification report. International Governance of Civilian AI: A Jurisdictional Certification Approach (2023)
  • Centre for the Governance of AI: Published the authors' summary of the proposal. Proposing an International Governance Regime for Civilian AI (2023)
  • U.S. Department of Commerce, Bureau of Industry and Security: Issued and then rescinded a tiered, country-by-country rule on access to advanced AI chips in 2025. BIS Rescission of the Biden Administration's AI Diffusion Framework (2025)

Status

Research proposal (2023). It draws on the ICAO, IMO and FATF models.

In their own words

We propose that states establish an International AI Organization (IAIO) to certify state jurisdictions (not firms or AI projects) for compliance with international oversight standards. States can give force to these international standards by adopting regulations prohibiting the import of goods whose supply chains embody AI from non-IAIO-certified jurisdictions.

Robert Trager, Ben Harack, Anka Reuel, Allison Carnegie, Lennart Heim, Lewis Ho, Sarah Kreps, Ranjit Lall, Owen Larter, Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh, Simon Staffell, José Jaime Villalobos, International Governance of Civilian AI: A Jurisdictional Certification Approach, 2023

States can also adopt multilateral controls on the export of AI product inputs, such as specialized hardware, to non-certified jurisdictions. ... As international actors reach consensus on risks of and minimum standards for advanced AI, a jurisdictional certification regime could mitigate a broad range of potential harms, including threats to public safety.

Robert Trager, Ben Harack, Anka Reuel, Allison Carnegie, Lennart Heim, Lewis Ho, Sarah Kreps, Ranjit Lall, Owen Larter, Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh, Simon Staffell, José Jaime Villalobos, International Governance of Civilian AI: A Jurisdictional Certification Approach, 2023
