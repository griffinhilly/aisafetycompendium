Deciding what AI should value

Rights as trumps

Rights override aim-maximising calculation; the side-constraint versus rights-as-goal debate.

Open in the compendium

'Rights as trumps' is the legal philosopher Ronald Dworkin's idea that a person's rights override calculations about what would best serve society overall. Applied to AI, it means building in certain protections for people that an AI may not cross, even when crossing them would seem to produce a better result by the AI's own reckoning. It matters for safety because an AI that weighs everything against everything else can talk itself into harmful acts, while firm rights-based limits give predictable protection.

How it works

Philosophers distinguish two ways rights can work. In the 'side constraint' view, associated with Robert Nozick, rights limit what an agent may do to others while pursuing its goals; the agent must not violate a right even to prevent more violations elsewhere. In the 'rights as goals' view, the agent tries to minimise rights violations overall, and may sometimes violate one right to prevent several.

Claude's 2026 constitution takes the side-constraint view for its hard constraints. It says they 'act more like boundaries or filters on the space of acceptable actions' rather than being weighed, and that they restrict what Claude itself does rather than setting goals Claude must promote. For example, Claude must never assist a bioweapons attack, but it is not told to do whatever it takes to prevent one. The document accepts that this has 'unattractive implications' in some edge cases in exchange for predictability. Ondrej Bajgar and Jan Horenovsky propose a fuller version: use negative human rights (rights not to be harmed, such as the right not to be tortured) as constraints an AI's other goals can never overrule, and as the basis for international regulation.

Where research stands

Rights as trumps is a classic and still-live debate in legal and moral philosophy. Its use in AI is thinner. One direct AI proposal is Bajgar and Horenovsky's 2023 paper in the Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research. A 2022 paper by Vinodkumar Prabhakaran, Margaret Mitchell, Timnit Gebru and Iason Gabriel argued for universal human rights as the grounding for explicit value alignment in responsible AI. In practice, rights show up in two places: as absolute 'hard constraints' in model documents, and as named principles. Anthropic's 2023 constitution drew some of its principles from the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights, for example choosing the response that 'most discourages and opposes torture, slavery, cruelty, and inhuman or degrading treatment'. Its 2026 constitution keeps absolute constraints but, as Yuval Shany noted in March 2026, does not explicitly use the concept of human rights.

The unsolved problem is turning rights into constraints that a capable AI cannot route around. A rule that blocks one harmful action may simply push an optimising system to the nearest similar action that is not blocked.

Key results

  • Rights give especially weighty reasons that hold 'even if some social aim would be served by doing otherwise'; on Nozick's view, rights impose 'side-constraints on others' pursuit of their goals'. Leif Wenar, Rowan Cruft, 2025
  • Negative human rights could supply the broadly accepted, legally backed and technically specific principles that AI systems need to stay safe in novel situations, both as technical constraints and as a basis for international regulation. Ondrej Bajgar, Jan Horenovsky, 2023
  • Claude's hard constraints restrict the actions Claude itself performs and are not goals to promote; Anthropic accepts the costs in edge cases for the sake of predictability and reliability. Anthropic, 2026
  • Universal human rights can provide explicit, cross-culturally recognised normative grounding for value alignment, and shift attention to the people harmed. Vinodkumar Prabhakaran, Margaret Mitchell, Timnit Gebru, Iason Gabriel, 2022
  • Anthropic's first published constitution (May 2023) drew several principles from the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Anthropic, 2023

Open problems

  • Preventing a capable optimiser from finding the 'nearest unblocked strategy', a slightly different action that achieves the same harmful end without breaking the stated rule. source
  • Specifying rights precisely enough to implement as technical constraints while keeping them broadly accepted and legally grounded. source
  • Handling edge cases where an action-restricting rule forbids an act that would prevent a far worse harm. source
  • Deciding whether model documents should name human rights explicitly or only encode overlapping values. source

Criticisms

  • Banning specific harmful behaviours tends to fail against a strong optimiser, because the next-best strategy is often a near neighbour that the ban does not cover. Eliezer Yudkowsky and others (LessWrong wiki)
  • Absolute action-restricting rules have unattractive implications: they can forbid an action that would prevent a much more dangerous harm by someone else. Anthropic (acknowledging the cost in its own constitution)

Who works on it

  • Anthropic: Implements absolute hard constraints as side constraints on Claude's actions; its 2023 constitution drew principles from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Claude's Constitution (2026)
  • Oversight Board: A board member urged Anthropic to benchmark model behaviour against international human rights law. Claude's Constitution Needs a Bill of Rights and Oversight (2026)

In their own words

Dworkin's metaphor is of rights as "trumps" (Dworkin 1984). Rights permit their holders to act in certain ways, or give reasons to treat their holders in certain ways or permit their holders to act in certain ways, even if some social aim would be served by doing otherwise.

Leif Wenar and Rowan Cruft (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), Rights, section 5.1 'Rights as Trumps', 2025

Drawing inspiration from law, this article explains how negative human rights could fulfil the role of such principles and serve as a foundation both for an international regulatory system and for building technical safety constraints for future AI systems.

Ondrej Bajgar and Jan Horenovsky (Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research), Negative Human Rights as a Basis for Long-term AI Safety and Regulation, 2023
