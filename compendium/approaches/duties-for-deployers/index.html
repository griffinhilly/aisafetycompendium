Holding AI companies to account

Constraining

Duties for deployers (companies that put models into use)

Put legal duties on the companies that use AI systems, such as human oversight, monitoring, keeping logs and telling affected people.

Open in the compendium

Most AI rules target the companies that build models. Deployer duties target the companies that put AI to use, such as a bank using AI to screen loans or an employer using it to rank job applicants. Typical duties are human oversight, monitoring, keeping logs, reporting problems and telling affected people. They matter because many harms arise from how and where a system is used, which the builder does not control.

How it works

The EU AI Act is the main example. Article 26 applies to deployers of 'high-risk' systems, a list that includes AI used in hiring, credit scoring and education. A deployer must use the system as the provider's instructions say. It must assign human oversight to staff with the competence and authority to intervene. Where it controls the input data, the data must be relevant and representative. It must monitor the system, stop using it and alert the provider and authorities if it sees a risk, keep the system's logs for at least six months, tell workers before using it at work, and tell people when a high-risk system makes or helps make decisions about them.

Smaller versions exist in US law. New York City's Local Law 144, in force since July 2023, requires employers using automated hiring tools to publish bias audits and notify candidates. Colorado's replacement AI law (SB 26-189), effective 1 January 2027, makes deployers give consumers notice before or when an automated decision tool is used, explain adverse decisions within 30 days, offer human review, and keep records for three years. California's privacy regulator adopted rules in 2025 that, from 1 January 2027, let consumers opt out of automated decision-making that replaces human judgement in significant decisions.

Where research stands

Deployer duties are law but mostly not yet in force, and they have been scaled back. The EU's Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), in force since 27 July 2026, moved the high-risk obligations from August 2026 to 2 December 2027 for standalone systems and 2 August 2028 for AI built into regulated products. Colorado's 2024 AI Act (SB 24-205) would have required deployers to run risk-management programmes and annual impact assessments. Governor Jared Polis signed it 'with reservations' in May 2024, and in May 2026 the state repealed and replaced it before it took effect, with a narrower law built mainly on notice, review and record-keeping duties.

The best evidence on effectiveness comes from New York City, and it is discouraging. A 2024 Cornell study of 391 employers found only 18 had posted bias audits and 13 had posted notices, partly because employers decide for themselves whether the law covers them. A December 2025 audit by the New York State Comptroller found that the city regulator had received only two complaints, found one violation among 32 companies it reviewed where the auditors found at least 17 potential ones, and lacked the technical skill to evaluate the tools. For frontier AI safety, a related question is whether to regulate 'downstream developers' who fine-tune or modify models; a 2025 GovAI paper concludes that duties on the original developers will likely still be needed.

Key results

  • Of 391 New York City employers checked, 18 had posted bias audits and 13 had posted transparency notices under Local Law 144, because employers have wide discretion over whether the law applies to them. Lucas Wright, Roxana Mika Muenster, Briana Vecchione, Jacob Metcalf, J. Nathan Matias et al., 2024
  • The New York State Comptroller found the city regulator received only two complaints in two years, found one violation where auditors found at least 17 potential ones, and lacked technical expertise to evaluate tools. Office of the New York State Comptroller, 2025
  • Colorado's SB 26-189, signed on 14 May 2026, repealed the duty of reasonable care, risk-management programmes and annual impact assessments, while keeping notice and human review duties and adding a three-year record-keeping duty, from 1 January 2027. Eckert Seamans, 2026
  • The EU Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, entered into force on 27 July 2026 and set the high-risk rules, including deployer duties, to apply from 2 December 2027 (Annex III systems) and 2 August 2028 (AI in products covered by Annex I). European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2026
  • Because downstream developers who modify models are numerous and diverse, regulating upstream developers to limit risky modifications will likely be necessary, with direct downstream duties only where they can raise risk to unacceptable levels. Sophie Williams, Jonas Schuett, Markus Anderljung, 2025

Open problems

  • Stopping deployers from deciding for themselves that a rule does not cover their system. source
  • Giving enforcement agencies the technical skill to check deployers' AI systems. source
  • Splitting duties between the developer who builds a model, those who modify it, and the company that uses it. source
  • Making human oversight meaningful, so staff have the competence and authority to override the system rather than rubber-stamp it. source

Criticisms

  • Broad deployer duties could hamper the development and spread of new technology, so they need fine-tuning before taking effect. Colorado Governor Jared Polis, signing letter (May 2024)
  • Duties that rely on self-assessed scope, transparency notices and individual complaints give little real accountability; regulators should limit deployer discretion. Lucas Wright, J. Nathan Matias and co-authors (Cornell)
  • Direct duties on the many downstream developers who modify models are hard to apply, so rules on the original developers to limit risky modifications will likely still be needed. Sophie Williams, Jonas Schuett, Markus Anderljung (GovAI)

Status

In EU law for high-risk systems. A 2026 amendment delayed these duties to December 2027 for standalone high-risk systems and August 2028 for systems built into products. Colorado repealed and replaced its broader deployer duties in May 2026, before they took effect. The replacement law keeps narrower notice and record-keeping duties from January 2027.

In their own words

Deployers of high-risk AI systems must ensure they use the systems according to instructions, with competent human oversight, and monitor their operation. They must manage input data, keep logs for at least six months, and inform providers and authorities of any risks or incidents. ... Deployers must inform individuals subject to AI-assisted decisions and cooperate with authorities on regulatory actions.

European Commission, AI Act Service Desk: Article 26, Obligations of deployers of high-risk AI systems (summary), 2024
