Agreement verification technology

Build the technology and protocols that let governments, or AI developers, verify that an AI agreement is being kept.

Open in the compendium

Verification technology lets one party check that another is keeping an AI agreement, without having to take its word for it. Examples are chips that log how they are used, monitoring devices in data centres, and inspection routines. It matters for safety because rival governments, or rival companies, are unlikely to accept limits on AI development unless they can detect cheating.

How it works

Most proposals focus on the specialised chips that frontier AI needs, because they are scarce, made by few firms and housed in large data centres. One early design (Shavit, March 2023) has chip firmware save occasional snapshots of a model's weights, keeps training records so inspectors can check how those weights were produced, and tracks the chip supply chain so no one can gather a large stock of untracked chips. A 2024 survey by Wasil and colleagues sorts methods into three kinds: national technical means such as satellite imagery that need no access to the other country; methods that need the other country's permission, such as inspections; and methods built into the hardware.

A concrete prototype shows the idea. Amodo Design, a small engineering consultancy in Sheffield, England, built a monitor that runs on eight Nvidia chips. It is meant to check that a data centre is only running an agreed model, not training a new one. It does this by re-running a sample of the data centre's outputs on its own copy of the model and comparing the results. It is designed to send out only a 'passed' or 'failed' signal, so no secrets leave the facility. In a demo reported by TIME in August 2026, it correctly identified the open model GPT-OSS-120B.

Where research stands

The field is young but growing fast. A RAND-led report (July 2025) describes six largely independent layers of verification, three based on hardware and three on people, such as whistleblower programmes, and says many of these technologies have not yet been built or stress-tested. A 2025 report from the Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative, with the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy, judges that verifying rules on data-centre AI 'appears to be possible within a few years', while rules on mobile AI devices such as weapons face severe political challenges. MIRI researchers (November 2024) note that many ideal technical tools are not yet feasible, but that greater access, such as physical inspection of data centres, can often substitute for them.

Funding and political interest rose in 2026. Coefficient Giving's Project Tailwind lists agreement verification technology as a featured initiative it wants founded, calling for hardware and protocols that are tamper-resistant, commercially viable and open-source enough to win wide trust. In July 2026 an open letter signed by over 1,300 employees of frontier AI companies asked the US government to support an international effort to build tools to 'deliberately pace the frontier'. The US government's stated position in 2026 rejects global governance of AI, and Nvidia, the leading chipmaker, rejects built-in kill switches and backdoors, so no verification system is deployed for any international AI agreement as of October 2026.

Key results

  • Compliance with limits on large training runs could in principle be verified through on-chip weight snapshots, training records that inspectors can check, and supply-chain tracking, while leaving consumer devices unrestricted. Yonadav Shavit, 2023
  • Ten verification methods for detecting unauthorised training and unauthorised data centres fall into three groups: national technical means, access-dependent methods and hardware-dependent methods, each with known ways to evade it. Akash R. Wasil, Tom Reed, Jack William Miller, Peter Barnett, 2024
  • States could eventually verify compliance using six overlapping layers (on-chip security features, two kinds of attached monitoring devices, and three personnel-based methods), but significant research and development is still needed. Mauricio Baker, Gabriel Kulp, Oliver Marks, Miles Brundage, Lennart Heim, 2025
  • Verification of many international AI agreements appears possible without speculative advances in verification technology, and verifying rules on data-centre AI may be possible within a few years if serious efforts are made. Ben Harack, Robert F. Trager, Anka Reuel and others (Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative, with contributors from the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy), 2025
  • Flexible hardware-enabled guarantees (flexHEGs) pair an auditable processor that monitors chip use with a tamper-resistant enclosure, and could support compute limits and privacy-preserving model evaluations. James Petrie, Onni Aarne, Nora Ammann, David Dalrymple, 2025
  • A prototype data-centre monitor correctly identified which model a server was running, but it needs unencrypted data and network tapping, and uses one-fifth to one-third as much compute as the model it watches. Billy Perrigo (TIME), 2026

Open problems

  • Building tamper-resistant monitoring hardware that holds up even when powerful states such as the US and China must prove they are complying. source
  • Proving what a chip is doing without revealing the model weights, data or methods that companies and governments want to keep secret. source
  • Cutting the cost of monitoring, since current prototypes use a large share of the compute they monitor. source
  • Guarding verification systems against abuse and against helping one party concentrate power. source

Criticisms

  • Built-in kill switches or backdoors in chips would be permanent weaknesses that hackers and hostile states could exploit, and would damage trust in the hardware. David Reber Jr., Chief Security Officer, NVIDIA (August 2025)
  • Verification tools are only useful if the US and China agree to a treaty, and the US government has said it rejects global governance of AI. TIME reporting, quoting Amodo Design CEO Tom Milton and White House science and technology policy director Michael Kratsios (August 2026)
  • Many of the ideal technical solutions are not yet feasible, so near-term agreements would lean on more intrusive access, such as physical inspections of data centres. Aaron Scher and Lisa Thiergart (MIRI)

  • Verifying International Agreements on AI: Six Layers of Verification, Mauricio Baker, Gabriel Kulp, Oliver Marks, Miles Brundage, Lennart Heim (2025). The most complete technical and policy treatment, written for both audiences.

  • The People Building a Way to Slow Down the AI Race, Billy Perrigo (TIME) (2026). A readable account of a working prototype and its limits.

In their own words

International agreements about AI development may be required to reduce catastrophic risks from advanced AI systems. However, agreements about such a high-stakes technology must be backed by verification mechanisms--processes or tools that give one party greater confidence that another is following the agreed-upon rules, typically by detecting violations.

Aaron Scher and Lisa Thiergart (MIRI), Mechanisms to Verify International Agreements About AI Development, 2025

We divide the verification methods into three categories: (a) national technical means (methods requiring minimal or no access from suspected non-compliant nations), (b) access-dependent methods (methods that require approval from the nation suspected of unauthorized activities), and (c) hardware-dependent methods (methods that require rules around advanced hardware).

Akash R. Wasil, Tom Reed, Jack William Miller, Peter Barnett, Verification methods for international AI agreements, 2024
