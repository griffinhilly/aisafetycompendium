Society: defence and who holds power

Societal resilience

Differential technology development

Delay risky innovation and accelerate safer substitutes, adjusting relative timing.

Open in the compendium

Differential technology development is the principle of changing the order in which technologies arrive: slow down the ones that raise risk and speed up the ones that reduce it. Nick Bostrom coined the original term, "differential technological development", in 2002. For AI safety it means trying to make safety, defensive and substitute technologies arrive before the dangerous capabilities they guard against.

How it works

The principle assumes we usually cannot stop a technology but can affect its timing. A 2022 paper by Jonas Sandbrink, Hamish Hobbs, Jacob Swett, Allan Dafoe and Anders Sandberg sorts technologies into four kinds. Risk-increasing technologies can cause harm. Safety technologies make a risky technology itself safer. Defensive technologies reduce the harm without changing the risky technology. Substitute technologies give similar benefits with less risk. Policy then funds or speeds up the last three and delays the first.

The paper points to the preferential advancement of low-emission technologies as a model, and argues the same lever can reduce risks beyond climate change. In AI, the same logic appears in proposals to speed up specific helpful tools. In March 2025 Forethought researchers Lizka Vaintrob and Owen Cotton-Barratt argued for accelerating AI forecasting, negotiation and alignment-research tools so they arrive before risk-generating capabilities. They note that one actor can often speed up a good application alone, while slowing a dangerous one may need agreement among many.

Within AI research, the principle asks whether a safety project advances general capability as a side effect. Dan Hendrycks and Mantas Mazeika call this the balance between safety and general capabilities.

Where research stands

The principle is a governance heuristic with a small academic literature and no applied programme or budget of its own. To its authors' knowledge, the 2022 paper is the first academic article to set it out in full. It finds that the principle's two requirements, anticipating a technology's effects and changing its timing, are each "sometimes viable". Much current work applies the idea in narrow ways: research funders choosing safety work, and the d/acc movement (see the d/acc entry) picking defensive start-ups.

Safety and capability are hard to keep apart in practice. In 2024 a meta-analysis by Richard Ren and colleagues at the Center for AI Safety found that many AI safety benchmarks track general capability and training compute closely, so work labelled as safety progress can turn out to be capability progress. Paul Christiano, who helped develop reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), guessed in 2023 that his work on it sped up AI progress by only a few tenths of a percent.

Key results

  • Bostrom first named the principle: retard dangerous technologies and accelerate beneficial ones, especially those that reduce the hazards of other technologies. Nick Bostrom, 2002
  • The first full academic statement of the principle sorts technologies into risk-increasing, safety, defensive and substitute types, and finds the two preconditions for using it each "sometimes viable". Jonas B. Sandbrink, Hamish Hobbs, Jacob L. Swett, Allan Dafoe, Anders Sandberg, 2022
  • Many AI safety benchmarks correlate highly with general capabilities and training compute, which can let capability gains be passed off as safety progress ("safetywashing"). Richard Ren, Steven Basart, Adam Khoja, et al., Dan Hendrycks (Center for AI Safety), 2024
  • Forethought argues that specific helpful AI applications (forecasting, coordination, automated alignment research) can be sped up through data, scaffolding and compute allocation, often by one actor alone. Lizka Vaintrob, Owen Cotton-Barratt (Forethought), 2025
  • Christiano guesses that his RLHF and early language-model work at OpenAI (2017-2020) sped up AI progress by "a few tenths of a percent", and argues that incidental capability gains from safety research are usually a small part of total progress. Paul Christiano, 2023

Criticisms

  • It is often hard to say what counts: "one person's offensive technology is another person's defensive technology", and the answer depends partly on factors outside the technology, such as who owns and controls it. Michael Nielsen (Astera Institute)
  • The principle assumes the cure for technology is more technology, which shifts power toward technocrats and away from non-technocrats. Michael Nielsen (Astera Institute)
  • The authors themselves find that anticipating impacts and changing timing are each only "sometimes viable", which limits where the principle can be applied. Sandbrink, Hobbs, Swett, Dafoe and Sandberg

Who works on it

  • Future of Humanity Institute, University of Oxford: Home of the 2022 paper that set out differential technology development as a responsible-innovation principle. Differential technology development: preprint on the concept (2022)
  • Open Philanthropy: Funded Jonas Sandbrink's doctoral research behind the 2022 paper. Differential technology development: preprint on the concept (acknowledgements) (2022)
  • Forethought: Argues for "differential AI development": speeding up AI tools that help navigate existential risk. AI Tools for Existential Security (2025)
  • Center for AI Safety: Studies the balance between safety and general capabilities and tests whether safety benchmarks measure something separate. Safetywashing: Do AI Safety Benchmarks Actually Measure Safety Progress? (2024)
  • Astera Institute: Michael Nielsen's notes examine the definition and usefulness of the concept. Notes on Differential Technological Development (2024)

Status

Governance heuristic; its two preconditions, anticipating impacts and intervening in timing, are only "sometimes viable" by the authors' own finding.

In their own words

We adapt Nick Bostrom's original concept to propose a responsible innovation principle of "differential technology development", which calls relevant actors to leverage risk-reducing interactions between technologies by affecting their relative timing. Thus, it may be beneficial to delay risk-increasing technologies and preferentially advance risk-reducing defensive, safety, or substitute technologies.

Jonas B. Sandbrink, Hamish Hobbs, Jacob L. Swett, Allan Dafoe, Anders Sandberg, Differential technology development: A responsible innovation principle for navigating technology risks, 2022

Our focus should be on what I want to call differential technological development: trying to retard the implementation of dangerous technologies and accelerate implementation of beneficial technologies, especially those that ameliorate the hazards posed by other technologies.

Nick Bostrom, Existential Risks: Analyzing Human Extinction Scenarios and Related Hazards, 2002
