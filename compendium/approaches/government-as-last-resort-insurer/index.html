Holding AI companies to account

Constraining

Government as insurer of last resort

A mandatory, risk-priced indemnification programme for risks private markets will not cover.

Open in the compendium

Some AI risks may be too large for any private insurer or company to pay for, which means no one could be held to account after a disaster. The proposal is for government to act as 'insurer of last resort': developers of frontier models must join a public programme and pay fees priced to how risky their work is. The safety point is the fee, not the payout: a price on risk gives developers a reason to reduce it, and the money can fund safety research.

How it works

In Cristian Trout's proposal (first posted September 2024, accepted to an ICML 2024 workshop), any developer training a model above a set compute threshold must join a government indemnification programme. Indemnification means the government promises to cover certain losses. The developer pays a fee for each training run, scaled to the compute used. To set the price, the government surveys experts, including the developers, and uses a scoring method called the Bayesian Truth Serum that rewards honest, careful answers. Fee revenue funds safety research, with a matching rule (quadratic financing) that adds public money to projects developers themselves choose to fund.

The idea copies existing public backstops. Under the US Price-Anderson Act, nuclear operators carry private insurance; above that, each reactor can be charged up to about $158 million per incident, a pooled layer of about $15 billion as of October 2023. Under the Terrorism Risk Insurance Program, created in 2002, the federal government pays the majority of losses above a threshold after a large terrorist attack. In August 2026 Harvard's Daniel Carpenter and co-authors proposed a smaller first step modelled on the terrorism programme, in which the government would ask insurers each year to estimate losses from set AI disaster scenarios, as it already does for terrorism.

Where research stands

This remains a proposal, with one main academic paper and a few related essays. Because so few organisations work on it, this entry lists only those with direct outputs. The paper's author says the mechanisms need more empirical tests, and admits a core limit: for truly existential risks, the government is also unable to pay afterwards, so the scheme works by pricing risk in advance, not by compensating victims.

Public backstops for AI firms are politically sensitive. In November 2025, after OpenAI's chief financial officer suggested a federal 'backstop' for its data-centre financing, the White House AI adviser David Sacks posted 'There will be no federal bailout for AI.' That episode concerned data-centre financing, but it shows the resistance any public risk-sharing scheme for AI companies is likely to meet.

Key results

  • Proposes a mandatory, government-run indemnification programme for frontier developers, with per-training-run fees priced by expert surveys using the Bayesian Truth Serum and revenue directed to safety research. Cristian Trout, 2024
  • The nuclear-industry precedent: each US reactor can be assessed up to $158.026 million per incident, a secondary pool of about $15 billion, after an October 2023 inflation adjustment. Morgan Lewis (Up & Atom blog), 2023
  • Proposes an annual government exercise, modelled on the terrorism programme's data calls, in which insurers estimate losses from defined AI disaster scenarios, building the loss data that pricing would need; the terrorism programme's outlays run $4 million to $7 million a year. Daniel Carpenter, Feodora Douplitzky-Lunati, Arjun Purohit, 2026
  • Argues that the AI insurance market will reduce harm only if policymakers steer it, with measures that include clearer liability, mutual insurers and government backstops for catastrophic risks. Cristian Trout, 2025

Open problems

  • How to stop developers from understating risk in the expert surveys that set their own fees, given that the Bayesian Truth Serum can be vulnerable to collusion. source
  • What the programme should promise for existential risks, where no government could pay afterwards either. source
  • How to gather loss estimates for AI disasters that have never happened. source
  • How to set the compute threshold for mandatory participation as algorithms become more efficient. source

Criticisms

  • Insurance may not be able to price complex, unforeseen frontier AI risks at all; as one respondent put it, 'One cannot insure what one cannot quantify.'. David F. Brochu, reader comment on Carpenter et al. (AI Frontiers)
  • Public financial backstops for AI firms face strong political opposition; 'There will be no federal bailout for AI.'. David Sacks, White House AI adviser, reported by Gizmodo
  • Flat per-plant fees, as in the nuclear model, price risk crudely and blunt the incentive to take care; a backstop helps safety only if each developer's fee tracks its own risk. Cristian Trout (on the Price-Anderson model)

In their own words

Many experts believe that AI systems will sooner or later pose uninsurable risks, including existential risks. This creates an extreme judgment-proof problem: few if any parties can be held accountable ex post in the event of such a catastrophe. This paper proposes a novel solution: a government-provided, mandatory indemnification program for AI developers.

Cristian Trout, Insuring Uninsurable Risks from AI: Government as Insurer of Last Resort, 2024

The program uses risk-priced indemnity fees to induce socially optimal levels of care. Risk-estimates are determined by surveying experts, including indemnified developers. The Bayesian Truth Serum mechanism is employed to incent honest and effortful responses.

Cristian Trout, Insuring Uninsurable Risks from AI: Government as Insurer of Last Resort, 2024
