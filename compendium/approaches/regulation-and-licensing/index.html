Holding AI companies to account

Regulation, standards and licensing

Government or standards-body rules that constrain development or deployment.

Open in the compendium

Regulation means binding rules from governments, backed by fines or bans. Standards are detailed technical rules, usually written by standards bodies, that show how to comply. Licensing means a developer needs government permission before training or releasing a model. Together they are the main way societies make safety practices compulsory instead of voluntary, so a company cannot skip them to win a race.

How it works

Most current frontier AI laws work through transparency and reporting rather than licences. California's Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 53), signed on 29 September 2025 and in force since 1 January 2026, requires large frontier developers to publish a safety framework, publish transparency reports when they release new models, report critical safety incidents to the state, and protect whistleblowers, with civil penalties enforced by the Attorney General. New York's RAISE Act, amended by a chapter amendment the governor signed on 27 March 2026 (Chapter 96 of 2026) and taking effect on 1 January 2027, places its main duties on developers with more than $500 million in annual revenue whose models were trained with more than 10^26 operations, and requires frontier developers to report critical safety incidents within 72 hours.

The European Union's AI Act is broader. It entered into force in August 2024. Bans on some practices applied from 2 February 2025 and duties for general-purpose AI models from 2 August 2025. The most capable models, those with 'systemic risk', must assess and reduce those risks; a voluntary Code of Practice published on 10 July 2025 shows how, and was signed by companies including Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Mistral AI and OpenAI (xAI signed only the safety chapter). Harmonised standards, being written by the CEN-CENELEC committee JTC 21, will give companies that follow them a legal presumption of conformity.

Licensing remains mostly a proposal. A 2023 paper led by the Centre for the Governance of AI set out three building blocks for frontier regulation: standard-setting, registration and reporting, and compliance mechanisms that could include licensing.

Where research stands

This is one of the most crowded areas in AI safety, and binding rules now exist in several places. Besides the EU, California and New York, South Korea's AI Basic Act took effect on 22 January 2026, including safety duties for systems trained with 10^26 or more operations. But many rules are being slowed or narrowed. In the EU, the 'Digital Omnibus on AI', published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026, delayed high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 (stand-alone systems) and 2 August 2028 (AI in regulated products). California's earlier, stricter bill SB 1047 was vetoed on 29 September 2024.

In the United States there is still no federal law regulating frontier AI. Executive Order 14365 of 11 December 2025 set up a Justice Department task force to challenge state AI laws and asked for a federal framework that would preempt them. Twenty-six state attorneys general wrote to Congress on 23 September 2026 asking it to regulate frontier AI and explicitly bar preemption. Open questions include how to set thresholds that track risk, how to keep rules current as capabilities change, and whether transparency duties change behaviour.

Key results

  • Frontier models are hard to regulate because dangerous capabilities appear unexpectedly, misuse is hard to prevent, and capabilities spread; regulation needs standard-setting, registration and reporting, and compliance mechanisms. Markus Anderljung, Joslyn Barnhart, Anton Korinek, Jade Leung, Cullen O'Keefe, Jess Whittlestone and 18 others, 2023
  • California's SB 53, signed 29 September 2025, became the first US state law aimed at frontier AI safety, requiring published safety frameworks, incident reporting to the Office of Emergency Services and whistleblower protection. Office of the Governor of California, 2025
  • New York's amended RAISE Act, signed 27 March 2026 and effective 1 January 2027, aligns with California's law but requires incident reports within 72 hours and carries penalties of up to $1 million for a first violation and $3 million for later ones. Cooley LLP, 2026
  • The EU's Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted by Parliament on 16 June 2026 and the Council on 29 June 2026, postponed high-risk AI obligations to December 2027 and August 2028 while leaving general-purpose model duties in place since August 2025. Future of Privacy Forum, 2026
  • South Korea's AI Basic Act took effect on 22 January 2026, adding safety duties for systems trained with 10^26 or more operations and a one-year grace period before fines. Cooley LLP, 2026
  • A US executive order directed a federal task force to challenge state AI laws and called for a national framework that preempts conflicting state rules. The White House, 2025

Open problems

  • Whether rules should be triggered by training compute or by a system's actual use and risk, since smaller specialised models may also be dangerous. source
  • Keeping rules current with a fast-moving technology; SB 53 asks the state to recommend updates every year. source
  • Whether US federal law will preempt state frontier AI laws, and what would replace them. source
  • Finishing harmonised technical standards that turn broad legal duties into checkable requirements. source

Criticisms

  • Licensing protects established firms from competitors; 'a license is inherently an anti-competitive, anti-innovative vehicle for incumbent enrichment.'. Tom Wheeler (Brookings, former FCC chairman)
  • Regulating only the largest, most expensive models 'could give the public a false sense of security' while smaller, specialised models may prove as dangerous. Governor Gavin Newsom, SB 1047 veto message
  • Transparency laws such as SB 53 punish missed paperwork rather than actual harm, creating 'a compliance minefield without clear standards.'. Aden Hizkias (Chamber of Progress), quoted in The Register

Who works on it

  • Centre for the Governance of AI: Led the influential 2023 proposal for frontier AI regulation, including standards, registration and licensing options. Frontier AI Regulation: Managing Emerging Risks to Public Safety (2023)
  • European Commission AI Office: Implements the EU AI Act's rules for general-purpose AI models and oversaw the Code of Practice. The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice (2025)
  • CEN-CENELEC Joint Technical Committee 21 (Artificial Intelligence): Writes the harmonised European standards that give a presumption of conformity with the AI Act. Artificial Intelligence (CEN-CENELEC topic page) (2026)
  • Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI), NIST: US government centre that helps industry develop voluntary standards and runs evaluations under voluntary agreements with developers. CAISSI (2026)
  • Anthropic: Endorsed California's SB 53 and proposed changes such as more detailed testing disclosures. Anthropic is endorsing SB 53 (2025)
  • Secure AI Project: Advised on and promoted state frontier AI bills, including SB 53 and the RAISE Act. Bills we support (2026)
  • Center for AI Policy: Published model federal legislation to regulate frontier AI systems. Model Legislation: Responsible AI Act (RAIA) (2025)
  • AI Standards Lab: Nonprofit that helps accelerate the writing of AI safety standards and submits input to EU AI Act consultations. Input to the European Commission's targeted consultation on draft high-risk AI classification guidelines (2026)
  • Ada Lovelace Institute: Maps UK options for AI regulation, from business as usual to a comprehensive AI bill. Making sense of the current options for AI regulation in the UK (2026)

Status

Crowded. A landscape review commissioned by Rethink Priorities (Michael Dickens) finds "Implementation-focused policy advocacy and communication are neglected," and estimates implementation funding at less than 10% of research funding.

In their own words

Frontier AI models pose a distinct regulatory challenge: dangerous capabilities can arise unexpectedly; it is difficult to robustly prevent a deployed model from being misused; and, it is difficult to stop a model's capabilities from proliferating broadly.

Markus Anderljung, Joslyn Barnhart, Anton Korinek, Jade Leung, Cullen O'Keefe, Jess Whittlestone et al. (24 authors; GovAI-led), Frontier AI Regulation: Managing Emerging Risks to Public Safety, 2023

... at least three building blocks for the regulation of frontier models are needed: (1) standard-setting processes to identify appropriate requirements for frontier AI developers, (2) registration and reporting requirements to provide regulators with visibility into frontier AI development processes, and (3) mechanisms to ensure compliance with safety standards for the development and deployment of frontier AI models.

Markus Anderljung, Joslyn Barnhart, Anton Korinek, Jade Leung, Cullen O'Keefe, Jess Whittlestone et al. (24 authors; GovAI-led), Frontier AI Regulation: Managing Emerging Risks to Public Safety, 2023
