Mutual sabotage deterrence (MAIM)

Deter any state from seeking AI dominance through the threat that rivals would sabotage its AI project.

Open in the compendium

Mutual Assured AI Malfunction (MAIM) is the idea that no major power can safely race for decisive AI dominance, because its rivals would sabotage the project before it succeeded. Its authors argue this already describes the US-China situation and could become a stable deterrence regime, loosely like nuclear deterrence. Critics argue the red lines are too vague and AI progress too hard to observe for deterrence to work, and that sabotage threats could start a war.

How it works

Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt and Alexandr Wang set out MAIM in Superintelligence Strategy (March 2025). If one state looked close to building superintelligence, meaning AI that far exceeds humans at nearly all mental tasks, its rivals would fear being permanently dominated or wiped out by a loss of control. They would act first to disable the project. The paper lists an escalation ladder, from lowest to highest: spying on AI developers; covert sabotage such as degrading training runs; overt cyberattacks on data centres or their power plants; physical strikes on data centres; and broader hostilities.

Because sabotage is relatively easy, the authors argue that each side is already deterred from an open bid for dominance. To keep this stable, they propose making the escalation ladder common knowledge, building data centres far from cities, separating destabilising projects from ordinary consumer AI through mutual transparency, and using AI-assisted inspections that confirm compliance without revealing secret code. MAIM is one of three pillars, alongside nonproliferation (keeping dangerous AI from rogue actors through chip export controls and security) and competitiveness (strengthening economies and militaries with AI).

Where research stands

MAIM is a strategy proposal, not adopted policy. No government has declared a MAIM doctrine as of October 2026. It has drawn a sizeable debate in 2025. Researchers at MIRI, policy analysts and a political scientist have challenged whether its conditions hold, and Barnett and Scher's May 2025 research agenda treats a 'Threat of Sabotage' world as one of the scenarios with unacceptable risk.

The main points of contention are clear. Critics say there is no clear, monitorable red line; that compute and data centres are poor signs of a rival's real progress; that breakthroughs could come too fast to respond to; and that sabotage would only delay a project. The authors replied in September 2025 that most of these objections apply to any AI race, not to MAIM in particular, that MAIM relies on preemption rather than retaliation, and that deterrence can push reluctant states toward verified agreements. One shared conclusion is that MAIM would need far better transparency and verification than exists today.

Key results

  • Superintelligence Strategy introduces MAIM, argues that preventive sabotage is relatively easy, and pairs deterrence with nonproliferation and competitiveness as a national strategy. Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt, Alexandr Wang, 2025
  • MAIM's main weakness is the lack of effective, monitorable and clear red lines; the author suggests earlier red lines such as chip fabrication or large compute concentrations. David Abecassis (MIRI), 2025
  • MAIM depends on each side accurately observing the other's progress, which is unreliable because compute is a poor proxy, progress may be sudden and decentralised, and spying itself can escalate. Jason Ross Arnold (AI Frontiers), 2025
  • MAIM lacks the features that made nuclear deterrence work, such as clear red lines, easy attribution and a clear ability to retaliate, and sabotage threats may not be credible. Peter Wildeford, Oscar Delaney, 2025
  • Of four strategic scenarios, the authors judge a world held together by threats of sabotage to carry unacceptable risk of catastrophe, compared with a coordinated halt. Peter Barnett, Aaron Scher (MIRI), 2025

Open problems

  • Defining a red line that a rival can observe early enough to act on; a line such as fully automated AI research may leave too little warning. source
  • Seeing a rival's true progress when algorithmic breakthroughs can matter as much as chips and data centres. source
  • Keeping sabotage threats from escalating into wider war, including between nuclear powers. source
  • Building the transparency and inspection tools that would let states tell destabilising projects from ordinary AI services. source

Criticisms

  • Without clear, monitorable red lines, and with sabotage likely to delay rather than stop a project, the deterrence regime would be highly unstable. David Abecassis (MIRI)
  • Errors in either direction are dangerous: overestimating a rival could trigger needless sabotage, and underestimating it could let it gain decisive dominance. Jason Ross Arnold (Virginia Commonwealth University)
  • MAIM lacks the characteristics that made nuclear deterrence effective, and governments may not accept that an AI training run justifies military action. Peter Wildeford and Oscar Delaney
  • Reply to critics: most objections apply to any race to superintelligence; MAIM relies on preemption, not retaliation, and need not stop a rival outright to shape its behaviour. Dan Hendrycks and Adam Khoja

Who works on it

  • Center for AI Safety: Its director Dan Hendrycks co-wrote Superintelligence Strategy, and its newsletter summarised the MAIM framework. AI Safety Newsletter #49: Superintelligence Strategy (2025)
  • Machine Intelligence Research Institute (technical governance team): Analysed what MAIM would need to work and treated a sabotage-based world as a scenario in its research agenda. Refining MAIM: Identifying Changes Required to Meet Conditions for Deterrence (2025)
  • AI Frontiers: Hosted the published exchange between MAIM's critics and its authors. AI Deterrence Is Our Best Option (2025)

Status

Proposal stage: set out in a 2025 strategy paper and analysed as a scenario in a research agenda.

In their own words

We introduce the concept of Mutual Assured AI Malfunction (MAIM): a deterrence regime resembling nuclear mutual assured destruction (MAD) where any state's aggressive bid for unilateral AI dominance is met with preventive sabotage by rivals. Given the relative ease of sabotaging a destabilizing AI project ... MAIM already describes the strategic picture AI superpowers find themselves in.

Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt, Alexandr Wang, Superintelligence Strategy: Expert Version, 2025

This strategic condition, which we refer to as Mutual Assured AI Malfunction (MAIM), represents a potentially stable deterrence regime, but maintaining it could require care. We outline measures to maintain the conditions for MAIM, including clearly communicated escalation ladders, placement of AI infrastructure far from population centers, transparency into datacenters, and more.

Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt, Alexandr Wang, Superintelligence Strategy: Expert Version, 2025
