Releasing and running it safely

Constraining

Limiting proliferation of high-risk models

Restrict distribution, for example through monitored application interfaces.

Open in the compendium

Limiting proliferation means keeping the most dangerous AI models from spreading uncontrollably, so that their use can be watched, limited and, if needed, cut off. A developer might offer such a model only through a monitored online interface instead of releasing the model files for anyone to download. Once a model's weights are public, no one can recall them, add new safeguards, or stop someone from removing the safety training.

How it works

Toby Shevlane set out the idea of 'structured access' in January 2022. The developer keeps the model on its own servers and lets people use it at arm's length, usually through an API (a programming interface for sending requests over the internet). Because the model never leaves the developer's control, the developer can filter requests, monitor for misuse, ban abusive accounts, and update safeguards. Releases sit on a spectrum, from fully closed, through staged and API access, to downloadable weights and fully open release.

Proliferation can also happen without a download. In 'distillation', a rival trains its own model on large numbers of answers from a stronger model. In February 2026 Anthropic reported that three AI labs had run more than 16 million exchanges with Claude through about 24,000 fraudulent accounts for this purpose. It detected them with classifiers and behavioural fingerprinting. On the government side, a Biden-era US rule added an export control on the weights of certain advanced closed models, but the Commerce Department rescinded it in May 2025, days before its compliance date.

Where research stands

Hosted, API-only access is a common way to offer frontier models today, and other developers release capable open-weight models. The policy debate turns on 'marginal risk': how much extra harm open weights add compared with what is already possible. In July 2024 the US National Telecommunications and Information Administration found the evidence insufficient to justify restricting open weights at that time, and recommended monitoring and readiness to act. A September 2023 report published by the Centre for the Governance of AI argued that some future highly capable models should not be open-sourced, at least initially.

Open models reach once-frontier capabilities about a year later, according to Epoch AI's November 2024 estimate, so restricting the leader only buys time. Closed models can also leak, through theft of weights or through distillation via the API. Research on how to measure marginal risk, and on hard-to-evade access controls, is still thin.

Key results

  • Structured access lets developers offer controlled, arm's-length use of AI systems while preventing users from modifying or reverse-engineering them, and works best through cloud-based services. Toby Shevlane, 2022
  • Release options form a gradient of six levels, from fully closed through staged, hosted, API and downloadable access to fully open, each trading concentration of power against risk mitigation. Irene Solaiman, 2023
  • A 22-author report concluded that highly capable foundation models should not be open-sourced, at least not initially, and explored alternative ways to keep open-source benefits. Elizabeth Seger et al., 2023
  • The US government's review concluded that current evidence could not definitively show whether restricting open weights was warranted, and recommended monitoring, evidence-gathering and readiness to restrict access if risks grow. National Telecommunications and Information Administration, 2024
  • Open models reach once-frontier capabilities with a lag of about one year (a weighted estimate of about 14 months). Ben Cottier, Josh You, Natalia Martemianova, David Owen (Epoch AI), 2024
  • Anthropic reported that DeepSeek, Moonshot AI and MiniMax generated over 16 million exchanges with Claude through about 24,000 fraudulent accounts to distil its capabilities, and described classifier and fingerprinting defences. Anthropic, 2026

  • Measuring how long capabilities in leading closed models take to appear in open models, and setting thresholds that would trigger restrictions. source
  • Stopping distillation through an API without blocking legitimate users. source

Criticisms

  • Open models bring innovation, competition, more distributed decision-making and transparency; a marginal-risk framework shows why their extra misuse risk is low in some cases, and that disagreements about risk often come from different assumptions. Sayash Kapoor, Rishi Bommasani, Percy Liang, Arvind Narayanan and co-authors
  • Restricting open weights without governed alternatives may displace risk rather than reduce it, deepen inequality for countries that rely on open models, and push use into unsupervised settings. Vinicius Santana Gomes
  • Release choices trade risk mitigation against concentration of power, since keeping powerful systems closed leaves control with a few large companies. Irene Solaiman

In their own words

Structured access is an emerging paradigm for the safe deployment of artificial intelligence (AI). Instead of openly disseminating AI systems, developers facilitate controlled, arm's length interactions with their AI systems. The aim is to prevent dangerous AI capabilities from being widely accessible, whilst preserving access to AI capabilities that can be used safely.

Toby Shevlane, Structured access: an emerging paradigm for safe AI deployment, 2022

The developer must both restrict how the AI system can be used, and prevent the user from circumventing these restrictions through modification or reverse engineering of the AI system. Structured access is most effective when implemented through cloud-based AI services, rather than disseminating AI software that runs locally on users' hardware.

Toby Shevlane, Structured access: an emerging paradigm for safe AI deployment, 2022
