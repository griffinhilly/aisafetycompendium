Building the field

Research-lab building on unclaimed agendas

Start a lab around a promising safety agenda nobody is staffing.

Open in the compendium

This approach builds a new research lab around a promising safety research agenda that no existing team is pursuing at scale. Its premise is that the number of promising research directions has grown faster than the number of researchers, so good ideas sit unstaffed. It matters because a focused, full-time team can push one agenda much faster than scattered individuals, and independent labs give policymakers outside experts who can run their own experiments.

How it works

A founder picks an agenda, writes a research plan, hires a team and raises money for staff and compute. Coefficient Giving's Project Tailwind (September 2026) lists 'Unclaimed research labs' as a featured initiative and names agendas it wants staffed, including high-effort AI control (much stronger safeguards even at a cost to usefulness), incrimination (turning ambiguous signs of misalignment into compelling evidence), the science of model personas and model specs, generalisation science, new interpretability methods, scalable oversight, and training that does not reward long-horizon goals. It suggests the 'focused research organization' model: a time-bound nonprofit with full-time staff, clear milestones and a public-goods mission.

A concrete example is Resolution, a new lab for 'semiautomated alignment theory and rigorous empirics' launched in June 2026 by researchers from the UK AI Security Institute's alignment team and Timaeus, aiming for 40 to 80 staff within two years. It chose to be one large organisation with a federated structure, so several research directors keep their own agendas while sharing funding, security and automation tools. In July 2026 it announced a $160 million grant from Coefficient Giving. A smaller example: in 2026 Arcadia Impact built an eight-person alignment research team in London in about four months, funded first by the UK AI Security Institute's Alignment Project and working closely with its alignment team.

Where research stands

Lab-building on specific agendas has a track record. Apollo Research was built around scheming (AI systems covertly pursuing misaligned goals) from 2023. Timaeus built an agenda on singular learning theory (a mathematical theory of how training shapes neural networks) and is now part of Resolution. Goodfire, a for-profit interpretability lab, raised a $150 million Series B at a $1.25 billion valuation in February 2026. Public funders have joined private ones: the UK AI Security Institute's Alignment Project awarded over £27 million to more than 60 projects in its first round, with grants of up to £1 million.

The main limits are people and judgment, not money. Interview studies find a shortage of senior researchers who can lead and mentor, and MATS reports that deployed research talent grows much more slowly than applications. Picking which agenda deserves a lab is hard: Coefficient describes several 'moonshot' agendas as high-value but low-probability bets, each with serious obstacles.

Key results

  • Resolution received a $160 million grant ($108 million base plus $52 million conditional) to put rigorous alignment research on a closer-to-even footing with frontier labs. Geoffrey Irving, Jesse Hoogland, Alex HT, Jacob Pfau, Daniel Murfet, Marco Cozzi, Stan van Wingerden, 2026
  • The UK AI Security Institute's Alignment Project awarded over £27 million to more than 60 alignment projects in its first round, backed by a coalition of governments, labs and philanthropies. UK AI Security Institute, 2025
  • A new eight-person alignment team at Arcadia Impact was built in about four months; paid work tests and references were the highest-signal parts of hiring, and most hires came from safety fellowships. Andrew Draganov, Erin Robertson, 2026
  • Goodfire, an interpretability lab founded on the agenda of understanding and editing model internals, raised a $150 million Series B at a $1.25 billion valuation. Goodfire, 2026
  • At MATS, mentor applications grew 2.2 times a year and fellow applications 1.8 times a year, but deployed research talent grew only 1.25 times a year, which the author reads as a shortage of founders and training programs. Ryan Kidd, 2025

Open problems

  • Choosing which agendas deserve a whole lab, when many 'moonshot' agendas have serious obstacles and low odds of success. source
  • Finding research leads with enough taste and seniority to run a lab, given a shortage of senior researchers who can mentor juniors. source
  • Securing enough compute for independent labs, especially if researchers come to manage many automated research agents that each need their own compute. source

Criticisms

  • The case that founding is needed to scale the field is weak: the best organisations were founded by people who also wrote a lot and developed a good model of the problems while running them, and someone who just wants to scale safety work could join a large existing organisation that can absorb lots of talent. The central examples of 'safety founders' are the people who founded Anthropic, OpenAI and arguably DeepMind. Oliver Habryka (habryka), comment on 'AI safety undervalues founders'
  • Each senior researcher can mentor only a few juniors before quality drops, and growing an organisation too quickly risks overwhelming that capacity. MATS Research (2026 talent-needs study)
  • A large new lab may deepen the shortage it responds to: by recruiting the same experienced researchers that other founding teams need, and by not hiring early-career researchers who need mentorship, it may reduce opportunities for the next generation. Resolution's founding team (self-critique in its launch memo)
