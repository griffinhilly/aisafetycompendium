Society: defence and who holds power

Societal resilience

Preventive surveillance with structured transparency

Monitor everyone closely enough to stop misuse of a dangerous technology, using privacy-preserving methods so the data cannot easily be abused.

Open in the compendium

This approach asks whether society may one day need monitoring strong enough to stop a single person from causing mass destruction with a cheap, dangerous technology, and how to build it without creating a tool for tyranny. Nick Bostrom's 2019 "vulnerable world hypothesis" argues that, for civilisation to be able to handle some kinds of technology in general, it would need ubiquitous real-time worldwide surveillance. "Structured transparency" is a separate privacy framework that tries to let monitoring detect specific threats while revealing little else.

How it works

Bostrom pictures technological progress as drawing balls from an urn. Most are beneficial, but a "black ball" would be a technology that devastates civilisation by default. One type would let individuals cause mass harm with easily available means. Because some small fraction of any large population would use such a capability, he argues that only extremely effective preventive policing, plus strong global governance, could stabilise such a world. He also argues the capacity would have to exist before it is needed, since a dangerous discovery might give only hours or days of warning. He sketches a "High-tech Panopticon" as an illustration, and openly notes the risk of "turnkey totalitarianism".

Structured transparency, set out in 2020 by Andrew Trask and co-authors including Eric Drexler, Allan Dafoe and Iason Gabriel, offers a different design. Instead of collecting everything, a system controls each information flow. It can strip identities, cap the number of questions anyone can ask, filter queries, seal old records, let AI search for a narrow pattern and report only the match, and require several keys to unlock deeper access. Banks already use pattern detection on anonymised transaction data to flag fraud. Drexler argues that such tools can serve "legitimate security needs without granting abusable powers".

Where research stands

This is a debated idea with no real-world programme of AI-era preventive surveillance. Most research effort goes into the privacy-preserving building blocks rather than the surveillance case itself. OpenMined is piloting privacy-preserving audits of AI systems, and a 2026 funding call by the Effective Institutions Project and the Collective Intelligence Project lists structured transparency as a priority area. Related techniques are proposed for checking compliance with international AI agreements without exposing secrets.

The opposite worry is now prominent: that AI makes mass surveillance cheap. Longview Philanthropy's 2026 call on power concentration includes "protecting against AI-enabled mass surveillance" as a priority, warning that near-ubiquitous surveillance may already be achievable in advanced economies. Supporters reply that structured transparency is a way to get security without that dystopia. Critics doubt that technical safeguards can hold once the capacity exists.

Key results

  • If a technology ever lets individuals cause civilisational devastation with readily available means, civilisation would need ubiquitous real-time surveillance and effective global governance to remain stable. Nick Bostrom (Global Policy), 2019
  • Many concerns about sharing information reduce to the "copy problem"; a five-part framework of privacy and governance tools ("structured transparency") can make information flows governable. Andrew Trask, Emma Bluemke, Teddy Collins, Ben Garfinkel, Eric Drexler, et al., 2020
  • Composable controls such as rate limits, query filtering, time windows, AI pattern search and multi-key permissions could detect threats while blocking mass surveillance. Eric Drexler (AI Prospects), 2024
  • Structured transparency is being piloted for privacy-preserving audits of AI systems and is proposed as a tool for democratic oversight of sensitive state actions. Effective Institutions Project and Collective Intelligence Project, 2026

Open problems

  • Whether any vulnerable-world technology will actually arrive, which Bostrom himself treats as an open question. source
  • How to keep a standby surveillance capacity from being captured and used for oppression. source
  • How to make structured-transparency guarantees verifiable to the people being monitored, not only to the operators. source
  • How to stop cheap AI-enabled surveillance of political opponents in the meantime. source

Criticisms

  • Universal surveillance would not deliver uniform law enforcement; it would not fix prosecutorial discretion or bad laws, and most governments lack strong protections for political or religious freedom. Kelsey Piper (Vox)
  • Stronger global governance adds a single point of failure and reduces competition between political systems; "bad cases make bad law". Robin Hanson, as quoted in Vox
  • The centre is often itself the source of risk; defence should not assume "the good guys" are in charge, and centralised control for safety has well-known downsides. Vitalik Buterin
  • Once built, a surveillance architecture would be hard to dismantle and too potent a tool if it fell into the wrong hands; Bostrom quotes this concern himself. Julian Sanchez, as quoted by Nick Bostrom

Status

Debated idea. Wikipedia calls Bostrom's case for mass surveillance controversial. Structured transparency is a separate, general privacy framework.

In their own words

Some areas, such as synthetic biology, could produce a discovery that suddenly democratizes mass destruction, e.g. by empowering individuals to kill hundreds of millions of people using readily available materials. In order for civilization to have a general capacity to deal with "black ball" inventions of this type, it would need a system of ubiquitous real-time worldwide surveillance.

Nick Bostrom (Global Policy), The Vulnerable World Hypothesis, 2019

A common view suggests that we have only bad options: accept invasive surveillance for security, or protect privacy and accept vulnerability. This view, however, makes strong but tacit assumptions about the structure of transparency - about how information from monitoring systems is collected, processed, filtered, and applied, and about how these systems are governed.

Eric Drexler (AI Prospects), Security without Dystopia: Structured Transparency, 2024
