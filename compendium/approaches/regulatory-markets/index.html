Holding AI companies to account

Regulatory markets

Governments license competing private regulators, and AI firms must buy oversight from one of them.

Open in the compendium

In a regulatory market, the government sets the safety outcomes AI must achieve, licenses private companies to act as regulators, and requires AI firms to buy oversight from one of them. The private regulators compete to find better and cheaper ways to check AI systems. The idea aims to combine public control over goals with the technical skill and speed of the private sector, which government agencies often lack.

How it works

There are three parties. The government decides the outcomes, for example that AI systems in commercial drones must resist adversarial attacks (inputs crafted to fool a model). Licensed private regulators design their own methods to achieve that outcome, such as testing, monitoring or required safeguards. AI companies must hire one licensed regulator and follow its rules. The government then licenses and oversees the regulators themselves. Jack Clark and Gillian Hadfield used this drone case as their example in their 2019 paper.

The key difference from self-regulation is who sets the goal: the government, not the industry. The key difference from ordinary regulation is who writes the technical rules: competing private bodies, not the agency. A real-world version is now taking shape in a voluntary form. California's SB 813, signed on 9 September 2026, has the state designate 'independent verification organizations' (IVOs) that assess AI systems' risks and the methods behind those assessments. Using an IVO is optional, and an IVO audit is relevant to, but does not settle, liability in a later lawsuit.

Where research stands

The academic literature is small: two papers by Clark and Hadfield (2019, revised April 2025; and 2023, published in the journal Jurimetrics in 2026), a game-theory study of how to pay private regulators, and related 'private governance' proposals such as Dean Ball's (April 2025). The policy track has moved faster. The nonprofit Fathom has promoted the IVO model in several states. California enacted SB 813 in September 2026; an earlier draft would have given certified developers a defence against some lawsuits, but the enacted law is voluntary and makes an IVO audit relevant to, but not conclusive of, liability. Virginia passed a law in April 2026 asking a state commission to study an IVO framework. Connecticut's omnibus AI law (SB 5, Public Act 26-15), signed on 27 May 2026, sets up a voluntary independent verification pilot that starts on 1 July 2027.

What these laws do not yet do is the core of the original idea: require AI firms to buy oversight. All enacted versions are opt-in. So the main claims, that competition among regulators raises safety and that government can supervise the regulators well, are untested for AI. Finance offers a partial precedent in FINRA, the private body that polices US broker-dealers, and also a warning: its powers have faced constitutional challenge in court.

Key results

  • Clark and Hadfield propose 'global regulatory markets' for AI safety and illustrate them with adversarial attacks on AI in commercial drones. Jack Clark, Gillian K. Hadfield, 2019
  • Hadfield and Clark argue that regulatory markets address both a 'technical deficit' (regulators struggle to turn legal rules into technical ones) and a 'democratic deficit' (industry standards let private firms make value-laden choices). Gillian K. Hadfield, Jack Clark, 2023
  • A game-theory model found that paying private regulators only for catching unsafe behaviour can backfire, and recommends rewarding them unless they miss unsafe behaviour they should have caught. Paolo Bova, Alessandro Di Stefano, The Anh Han, 2023
  • Ball proposes government-authorised private bodies that certify frontier developers on an opt-in basis, with certified firms protected from tort liability for customer misuse. Dean W. Ball, 2025
  • California enacted SB 813 on 9 September 2026, creating state-designated independent verification organizations; an executive order of 18 September 2026 moved some deadlines up by about a year. Crowell & Moring, 2026

Open problems

  • Designing payments for private regulators so they compete on safety rather than on being lenient with clients. source
  • Giving government enough technical skill to judge whether private regulators are doing their job. source
  • Getting from voluntary, opt-in verification to the mandatory purchase of oversight the original proposal requires. source
  • Deciding what independent verification should look like in practice, including the safety standards verifiers check against. source

Criticisms

  • Private bodies given enforcement power without clear legislative guardrails could face separation-of-powers challenges, as FINRA did in a 2024 federal appeals court ruling. Gregory Ferenstein, Reason Foundation (June 2025)
  • Firms will tailor their behaviour to how hard regulators look, so badly designed incentives discourage regulators from innovating and can undermine the market's goal. Paolo Bova, Alessandro Di Stefano, The Anh Han

Status

Proposal in two papers by the same authors. The 2019 paper was revised in 2025; the 2023 paper was revised in February 2026 and published in Jurimetrics (2026). They illustrate it with commercial drones. A voluntary version is now law: California SB 813, signed on 9 September 2026, has a state agency designate independent verification organizations, but does not require firms to use them.

In their own words

We propose a solution: regulatory markets, in which governments require the targets of regulation to purchase regulatory services from a government-licensed private regulator. This approach to AI regulation could overcome the limitations of both command-and-control regulation and excessive delegation to industry.

Gillian K. Hadfield, Jack Clark, Regulatory Markets: The Future of AI Governance, 2023

In this approach, governments create a market for regulation in which private sector organizations compete to achieve regulatory outcomes set by a government regulator. ... A key distinction between regulatory markets and self-regulation is that in regulatory markets the overall outcomes that must be achieved by regulation are determined by the government and not the regulated entity itself.

Jack Clark, Gillian K. Hadfield, Regulatory Markets for AI Safety, 2019
