Building the field

Field-building

Taking on unowned problems and agendas

Take on safety problems and research agendas that no team owns, for example by founding a new organisation or lab.

Open in the compendium

This approach takes on safety problems and research agendas that no team owns. One route funds and supports people to start the new organisations that AI safety lacks, such as independent auditors, incident investigators and security firms; another builds a new research lab around a promising safety agenda that no existing team is pursuing at scale. Its premise is that founders, not money or ideas, are now the main limit on how much safety work gets done, and that the number of promising research directions has grown faster than the number of researchers. It matters because institutions take years to build, so gaps that will matter in two years need founders now, and a focused, full-time team can push one agenda much faster than scattered individuals.

How it works

A funder or incubator publishes a list of problems it wants solved, finds would-be founders, and backs them in stages. The clearest example is Coefficient Giving's Project Tailwind, announced in September 2026 'in the spirit of Y Combinator's Requests for Startups'. It lists 36 initiatives in seven categories, from independent assessment to talent and convenings, each endorsed by a named grantmaker. Founders can pitch a listed idea or their own. Funding comes in three tiers: pre-seed ($200k to $2m to develop an idea and team over 9 to 18 months, with no legal entity needed), seed ($2m to $20m over 18 to 36 months), and scale ($20m to $200m+).

Incubators add structure around the money. They help a founder pick a problem, test it with experts, find co-founders, and reach funders. BlueDot Impact's Incubator Week, for example, runs five days in San Francisco: threat models on Monday, building and testing interventions mid-week, and a funding pitch on Friday, with up to $200k in grants for teams it backs. GovAI's Entrepreneur-in-Residence role gives a year of salaried runway and $150k in seed funding through partner funders. Fiscal sponsors (established nonprofits that host new projects under their own legal umbrella) handle payroll and compliance so founders can start before they incorporate.

To take on an unstaffed research agenda, a founder picks an agenda, writes a research plan, hires a team and raises money for staff and compute. Project Tailwind also lists 'Unclaimed research labs' as a featured initiative and names agendas it wants staffed, including high-effort AI control (much stronger safeguards even at a cost to usefulness), incrimination (turning ambiguous signs of misalignment into compelling evidence), the science of model personas and model specs, generalisation science, new interpretability methods, scalable oversight, and training that does not reward long-horizon goals. It suggests the 'focused research organization' model: a time-bound nonprofit with full-time staff, clear milestones and a public-goods mission.

A concrete example is Resolution, a new lab for 'semiautomated alignment theory and rigorous empirics' launched in June 2026 by researchers from the UK AI Security Institute's alignment team and Timaeus, aiming for 40 to 80 staff within two years. It chose to be one large organisation with a federated structure, so several research directors keep their own agendas while sharing funding, security and automation tools. A smaller example: in 2026 Arcadia Impact built an eight-person alignment research team in London in about four months, funded first by the UK AI Security Institute's Alignment Project and working closely with its alignment team.

Where research stands

This is a live and fast-growing practice, not a research field. Coefficient Giving says its technical AI safety, security and fieldbuilding programs directed roughly $350 million in 2025 and were on track to direct more than $1 billion in 2026, and that the binding constraint has moved from money to organisations and founders. It points to Epoch, Redwood Research and MATS as institutions that started within the last five years as a few people with an idea. Large single grants now happen: in July 2026 Resolution announced a $160 million grant from Coefficient Giving, and said the process from first conversation to confirmation took six weeks. A cluster of incubators has formed. By their own reports, Catalyze Impact has incubated or accelerated more than 20 organisations, and BlueDot's past Incubator Week cohorts had 50 participants who raised more than $10 million. Fifty Years, Seldon Lab, GovAI and Halcyon Futures also run founder programs. Coefficient closed its AI governance request for proposals on January 25, 2026, moving staff toward proactively filling gaps.

Lab-building on specific agendas has a track record. Apollo Research was built around scheming (AI systems covertly pursuing misaligned goals) from 2023. Timaeus built an agenda on singular learning theory (a mathematical theory of how training shapes neural networks) and is now part of Resolution. Goodfire, a for-profit interpretability lab, raised a $150 million Series B at a $1.25 billion valuation in February 2026. Public funders have joined private ones: the UK AI Security Institute's Alignment Project awarded over £27 million to more than 60 projects in its first round, with grants of up to £1 million.

What is not yet known is whether the new organisations will work. Safety nonprofits lack a revenue signal to show what is working. Experienced incubators warn that few people suit founding, that the best founders produce most of the impact, and that young organisations often stall for lack of funding in years two to five. Self-reported incubator figures (organisations launched, money raised) measure activity, not reduced risk.

The main limits are people and judgment, not money. Interview studies find a shortage of senior researchers who can lead and mentor, and MATS reports that deployed research talent grows much more slowly than applications. Picking which agenda deserves a lab is hard: Coefficient describes several 'moonshot' agendas as high-value but low-probability bets, each with serious obstacles.

  • Funding young organisations through years two to five, after seed money runs out but before they are large enough for big institutional funders. source
  • Deciding when a gap is better filled by a new team inside an existing organisation than by a new organisation. source
  • Building enough fiscal sponsorship and operations capacity to absorb dozens of new projects without single points of failure. source
  • Choosing which agendas deserve a whole lab, when many 'moonshot' agendas have serious obstacles and low odds of success. source
  • Finding research leads with enough taste and seniority to run a lab, given a shortage of senior researchers who can mentor juniors. source
  • Securing enough compute for independent labs, especially if researchers come to manage many automated research agents that each need their own compute. source

Criticisms

  • New incubators often do not address the real bottlenecks: probably fewer than 5% of people suit founding a nonprofit, the top 20% of founders generate most of the impact, and many charities stall for lack of mid-stage funding, so doubling the founder pool does not double impact. Joey Savoie (Charity Entrepreneurship)
  • Founding is not for everyone, and some initiatives fit better inside existing organisations because of returns to scale, expertise, relationships and access; the main funder itself urges would-be founders to talk to existing teams first. Coefficient Giving (Tailwind FAQs)
  • More people should found organisations, but the post's arguments for it are weak: the best safety organisations were founded by people who also wrote a lot and developed a good model of the problems while running them, someone who only wants to scale the field can join a large existing organisation, and the central examples of 'safety founders' are those who founded Anthropic, OpenAI and arguably DeepMind. Oliver Habryka (habryka), comment on Ryan Kidd's 'AI safety undervalues founders'
  • Each senior researcher can mentor only a few juniors before quality drops, and growing an organisation too quickly risks overwhelming that capacity. MATS Research (2026 talent-needs study)
  • A large new lab may deepen the shortage it responds to: by recruiting the same experienced researchers that other founding teams need, and by not hiring early-career researchers who need mentorship, it may reduce opportunities for the next generation. Resolution's founding team (self-critique in its launch memo)

Project Tailwind is Coefficient Giving's call for founders to launch ambitious AI safety initiatives. ... The world is not ready. There are critical, basic problems that no one owns. Any industry whose failures can cause serious harm needs a safety ecosystem.
