Holding AI companies to account

Constraining

Catastrophe bonds and risk bonds

Sell tradeable securities that lose principal if a defined AI disaster occurs, so the market prices the risk.

Open in the compendium

A catastrophe bond is a security that pays investors a high return but loses some or all of its principal if a defined disaster happens; the lost principal then pays for the damage. Applied to AI, a developer would pay investors to carry the risk of a defined AI disaster. The safety idea is that investors would demand a higher price from riskier labs, so markets would put a visible cost on unsafe practice and fund compensation that ordinary insurers cannot.

How it works

Catastrophe bonds already exist for hurricanes and earthquakes. Investors' money is held as collateral in a separate legal entity. Each year the sponsor pays investors a coupon, much like an insurance premium. If the defined event happens, the collateral goes to the sponsor or to victims; if not, investors get their money back plus the coupons.

In January 2026 Daniel Reti and Gabriel Weil proposed that frontier AI developers sponsor such bonds. The payout trigger could be a court ruling (accurate but slow), a measurable event (fast but hard to define), or an industry-wide claim threshold. The coupon would combine a base rate with a surcharge set by an independent 'Catastrophic Risk Index' that rates each lab's safety practices, much as credit ratings set borrowing costs, so a safer lab pays less. They estimate a coupon of 10 to 14 percent a year and an initial market of $350 million to $500 million, and suggest regulators could require minimum coverage as a licensing condition.

A June 2026 paper in Data & Policy proposes a related instrument, 'AI Risk Bonds', for present-day systems. The developer or deployer issues the bond and defines in advance which harmful behaviour triggers a payout to victims. Independent auditors rate its risk management, and weaker ratings mean higher yields.

Where research stands

This is an early idea: two published proposals, one in a peer-reviewed journal. The organisations involved are few, so this entry lists only those with direct outputs. The underlying market is large and growing: catastrophe bonds outstanding reached a record $61.3 billion at the end of 2025.

The main unsolved pieces are pricing a risk with no loss history, defining a trigger that pays fast without disputes, and building the independent risk ratings that the pricing depends on. Experience outside AI shows that trigger design can fail: the World Bank's pandemic bonds paid out only after rigid criteria were met, more than five weeks after COVID-19 was declared a pandemic, and the bank did not issue a second round.

Key results

  • Proposes AI catastrophe bonds sponsored by frontier developers, priced by an independent risk index so safer labs pay lower coupons, with an estimated 10 to 14 percent annual coupon and an initial market of $350 million to $500 million. Daniel Reti and Gabriel Weil, 2026
  • A simple equilibrium model shows bond yields fall as a system's expected impact improves, so the instrument puts a higher cost of capital on riskier AI systems. Gleb Papyshev, Keith Jin Deng Chan, Sara Migliorini, 2026
  • Catastrophe bond issuance hit a record $25.6 billion in 2025 and the outstanding market a record $61.3 billion, showing that investors will hold large tail risks for a yield. Steve Evans (Artemis), 2026
  • The World Bank's pandemic bonds, the nearest precedent for a novel-risk bond, paid about $196 million for COVID-19 only after rigid triggers were met more than five weeks after the pandemic declaration. Tracy Alloway and Tasos Vossos (Bloomberg, via Insurance Journal), 2020

Open problems

  • Pricing a risk with no historical loss data and a highly uncertain probability. source
  • Designing a payout trigger that is fast, hard to dispute and still tied to one developer's conduct. source
  • Building independent risk ratings and evaluation standards credible enough for investors, and keeping issuer-paid auditors free of conflicts. source
  • AI disasters would likely coincide with falls in AI share prices, which may reduce investor appetite exactly when cover is needed. source

Criticisms

  • Rigid triggers can make novel-risk bonds pay late, little or never; critics of the pandemic bonds said the triggers 'had to be late' so payment was less likely. Olga Jonas, quoted by Bloomberg (Insurance Journal)
  • Auditors cannot predict genuinely unpredictable behaviour, only judge the rigour of a developer's processes, so bond prices may still rest on weak information. Gleb Papyshev, Keith Jin Deng Chan, Sara Migliorini (on their own proposal)

Status

Recent proposals, one in a peer-reviewed journal; not yet implemented.

In their own words

As an alternative market-driven solution to liability insurance, we propose AI catastrophe bonds: a method of insurance tailored to cover a specific set of large-scale AI disasters, inspired by how the market insures against natural disasters. As well as offering coverage, our proposal would be structured to encourage higher safety standards, reducing the likelihood of all AI disasters.

Daniel Reti and Gabriel Weil (AI Frontiers, guest commentary), Making Extreme AI Risk Tradeable, 2026

While academic debates prioritize existential threats, this article shifts focus to governing present-day AI through AI Risk Bonds: market-driven instruments inspired by catastrophe bonds. These bonds securitize AI-related liabilities, using investor scrutiny to price risks based on a system's expected impact and behavioral predictability. By dynamically adjusting bond yields, higher risks escalate capital costs for developers, incentivizing proactive risk mitigation.

Gleb Papyshev, Keith Jin Deng Chan, Sara Migliorini (Data & Policy, Cambridge University Press), AI Risk Bonds: a market-based mechanism for governing liability, 2026
