Crisis channels and incident notification between rival states

Set up channels between rival states, with standard notices and regular talks, so AI incidents do not escalate.

Open in the compendium

This approach sets up direct lines between rival governments, chiefly the US and China, so that each can warn the other quickly when an AI system malfunctions or is misused in a way that could be mistaken for an attack. It adds standard notice formats and regular talks. It matters for safety because an AI incident that crosses borders, such as a rogue AI-driven cyberattack, could escalate into conflict if the other side misreads it.

How it works

The model is older crisis tools such as the US-Soviet hotline and the US-China Military Maritime Consultative Agreement, a standing forum where the two navies discuss unsafe encounters at sea. For AI, the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy (IAPS) proposes two pieces. First, a standing US-China AI Risk and Incident Dialogue would build shared definitions of AI incidents, share information routinely, agree notification procedures and review incidents afterwards (September 2026). Second, an incident channel would use a standard written template that can be sent within hours of detection (October 2026). The template would report facts and confidence levels, not blame.

A concrete example: suppose an AI agent running on servers in one country starts probing the other country's power grid without anyone ordering it. Under the proposal, the first side would send a short written notice saying what it has seen and how sure it is. IAPS recommends email-style, asynchronous messages over telephone hotlines, starting between two senior officials and later handing specific incident types to the relevant agencies. Notices would be confidential and would not imply fault.

Where research stands

This approach has a live diplomatic opening. On 20 September 2026, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent proposed to Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng a notification mechanism to warn each other of AI incidents serious enough to threaten national security. After the Trump-Xi summit in Washington on 24 September 2026, the White House said the two countries had agreed to set up a 'bilateral communication channel' for AI incidents. China's Foreign Ministry readout described an agreed dialogue on the risks and benefits of AI, with the next exchange in November, but did not describe the incident channel. No protocol, trigger threshold or responsible agencies had been made public as of October 2026.

Earlier work laid the groundwork. Unofficial 'Track II' talks between Brookings and Tsinghua University reached shared positions on human control of AI weapons and nuclear decisions (April 2024). CNAS researchers proposed AI confidence-building measures, including a military hotline for incidents involving autonomous systems, in January 2021. The main doubt is practical: China has often not answered the existing US-China military hotline, and an AI incident may be hard to attribute to a government, a non-state group or the AI system itself.

Key results

  • A standing US-China AI Risk and Incident Dialogue, modelled on the Military Maritime Consultative Agreement, could build shared incident definitions, notification procedures and post-incident reviews. Clarissa Koh, Sarah Godek, Karson Elmgren (IAPS), 2026
  • To implement the agreed mechanism, the two sides should use a written template sendable within hours, focused on facts and confidence levels, over an asynchronous text channel formalised by a memorandum of understanding. Sarah Godek, Clarissa Koh, Karson Elmgren, Cara Labrador, Craig Jolley (IAPS), 2026
  • The White House said on 25 September 2026 that the US and China agreed to set up a bilateral communication channel for AI incidents; Beijing had not commented at the time. Al Jazeera, 2026
  • Confidence-building measures for military AI could include an international autonomous-incidents agreement with a military-to-military hotline for real-time incidents. Michael C. Horowitz, Paul Scharre (CNAS), 2021
  • A five-year Brookings-Tsinghua Track II dialogue reached common ground on human control of AI-enabled weapons and nuclear decisions, and began a shared AI glossary. Ryan Hass, Colin Kahl (Brookings), 2024

Open problems

  • Agreeing what counts as an AI incident serious enough to notify, so that the channel is neither silent nor flooded. source
  • Telling whether an incident was ordered by a government, carried out by a non-state group, or caused by an AI system acting beyond its limits. source
  • Making sure the other side actually responds in a crisis, given the existing military hotline's record. source
  • Sharing enough technical detail to be useful without revealing secrets or admitting fault. source

Criticisms

  • China has repeatedly ignored the existing US military hotline, including a refused call after the 2023 balloon episode, so warm summit rapport may not carry over into a real crisis. The Economist (republished by Der Standard)
  • An earlier 2024 US-China AI dialogue failed because frontier AI risks got mixed in with other issues. Scott Singer (Carnegie Endowment), quoted by Al Jazeera

Status

Policy-memo stage with a live diplomatic hook. After the Trump-Xi summit in Washington on 24 September 2026, the White House said on 25 September that the two countries agreed to open a "bilateral communication channel" for AI incidents. China's Foreign Ministry described only an agreed bilateral AI dialogue on the risks and benefits of AI. In their own words

To manage shared risks and ensure stability, we recommend establishing a standing dialogue mechanism informed by the experience of mechanisms like the Military Maritime Consultative Agreement (MMCA). ... As the risk of AI-enabled incidents with destabilizing impacts grows, now is the time to develop communications channels—before they are needed in a crisis.

Clarissa Koh, with Sarah Godek and Karson Elmgren (Institute for AI Policy and Strategy), Establishing a U.S.-China AI Risk and Incident Dialogue, 2026

To implement the incident notification mechanism agreed between the United States and China, the two sides should establish a text-based standard template for sharing incident information, first between principals and then between relevant agencies. ... The incident notification channel should use text-based asynchronous notification, such as an email connection.

Sarah Godek, with Clarissa Koh, Karson Elmgren, Cara Labrador and Craig Jolley (Institute for AI Policy and Strategy), Operationalizing U.S.-China AI Incident Notification, 2026
