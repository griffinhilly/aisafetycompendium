Society: defence and who holds power

Market-driven resilience mechanisms

Use market incentives to build society's capacity to absorb anticipated AI threats.

Open in the compendium

Market-driven resilience means using prices, insurance, certification and paid incentives, rather than only laws, to make society better able to absorb harm from AI. The core claim is that whoever pays when things go wrong (an insurer, a liable company) will demand evidence that risks are controlled, and will fund the testing and standards to check. This can move faster than regulation, but it struggles with rare, very large and correlated losses.

How it works

The model most often cited is the "incentive flywheel" of insurance, standards and audits. In a July 2025 essay, Rune Kvist and Rajiv Dattani, co-founders of the Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company (AIUC), and Brandon Wang point to Benjamin Franklin's 1752 fire-insurance company, which set building standards and ran inspections to cut its payouts. Later, insurers funded Underwriters Laboratories to certify electrical goods. Applied to AI, an insurer covers a company's AI agent only if it passes independent tests; the premium then signals how safe it is, and the insurer has reason to fund research that lowers losses.

This is starting to happen. In May 2025 insurers at Lloyd's of London began selling cover, through the start-up Armilla, for losses when an AI tool performs worse than expected. AIUC publishes AIUC-1, a standard for AI agents tested by red-teaming and independent audits. Other market tools include bug bounties: in 2024 Anthropic offered outside researchers up to $15,000 for universal jailbreaks (prompts that get around a model's safeguards across many topics) in high-risk areas.

For catastrophic risk, proposals combine markets with law. Cristian Trout (2024) suggests strict, limited liability for frontier developers plus mandatory insurance, modelled on nuclear power. Nicholas Stetler (2025) proposes private insurance, an industry risk pool, and government reinsurance for the largest events.

Where research stands

The idea is moving from essays to early markets, but only for everyday harms. Coverage now exists for chatbot errors, AI performance drift and some AI-related intellectual-property claims. Standards bodies and certifiers are forming. AIUC says it has raised $55 million and that firms such as KPMG and Cursor hold AIUC-1 certification. In July 2026 a report with Centre for the Governance of AI (GovAI) authors set out an eight-part "AI insurance stack", from incident data to claims handling.

The weak point is the risks safety research cares most about. In November 2025 several large US insurers asked regulators for permission to exclude broad AI liabilities from corporate policies. Their main fear is correlated loss: one faulty model used by thousands of firms could trigger thousands of claims at once. A May 2026 study that mapped 55 AI threat classes against 26 insurance products found that dependence on a few foundation models is the clearest new barrier to insurability. As an open research question, the Anthropic Institute's fellowship asks which market mechanisms could improve resilience to anticipated AI threats.

Key results

  • Insurance, standards and audits together can create "skin in the game" for measuring and reducing AI risk; the essay lists 25 actions for entrepreneurs and policymakers by 2030. Rune Kvist, Rajiv Dattani, Brandon Wang, 2025
  • Insurers at Lloyd's launched cover for losses when an AI tool underperforms its initial benchmark, for example a chatbot falling from 95% to 85% correct answers. Financial Times, via Armilla, 2025
  • Following nuclear power, frontier developers could carry limited, strict and exclusive liability for events that cause, or could easily have caused, catastrophic losses, with mandatory insurance so insurers act as quasi-regulators. Cristian Trout, 2024
  • AI agent risk sits mostly unpriced in existing policies; affirmative AI cover with limits in the billions is possible by 2030 only with industry-wide data, modelling and standards. Cristian Trout, Markus Anderljung, et al., 2026
  • Mapping 55 AI threat classes against 26 insurance products found four tiers, from affirmatively insured to outside private insurance, with model concentration the clearest new limit. Alex Leung, Rex Zhang, Ervin Ling, Kentaroh Toyoda, SiewMei Loh, 2026

Open problems

  • Which market mechanisms could improve society's resilience to anticipated threats from AI systems. source
  • How to insure correlated losses when one model failure hits thousands of policyholders at once. source
  • How to build the incident data and catastrophe models insurers need for a technology that changes every few months. source
  • How to make developers who could not pay for a catastrophe ("judgment-proof") bear its cost in advance. source

Criticisms

  • Insurers themselves are pulling back: several large US carriers sought to exclude AI liabilities, and one underwriter called model outputs "too much of a black box". Insurers quoted by the Financial Times, reported by TechCrunch
  • Liability and mandatory insurance are not a substitute for regulation, though they can help direct resources to risk modelling and safe design and support later rules. Cristian Trout
  • Concentration among a few model providers makes AI losses correlated in a way that ordinary private insurance structures may not be able to carry. Alex Leung and co-authors

Who works on it

  • Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company (AIUC): Standards and insurance company behind the incentive-flywheel case and the AIUC-1 agent standard. Underwriting Superintelligence (2025)
  • Armilla AI: Sells AI performance and liability cover underwritten by Lloyd's insurers. Insurers launch cover for losses caused by AI chatbot errors (2025)
  • Centre for the Governance of AI (GovAI): Co-authored a blueprint for the data, standards and pricing infrastructure AI insurance needs. Underwriting the Agent Economy: The Blueprint for an AI Insurance Stack (2026)
  • The Anthropic Institute (Anthropic Fellows Program, Economics & Policy): Funds fellows to identify market mechanisms that improve societal resilience to AI threats. Anthropic Fellows Program, The Anthropic Institute (Economics & Policy) (2026)
  • Anthropic (safeguards): In 2024 launched an invite-only model-safety bug bounty with HackerOne, paying for universal jailbreaks in high-risk domains. Expanding our model safety bug bounty program (2024)

Status

Named in a lab fellowship call and in 1 of the 7 source groups. An open research question, not a programme.

Start reading

  • Underwriting Superintelligence, Rune Kvist, Rajiv Dattani, Brandon Wang (2025). The clearest pro-market case, with historical analogies and a 2030 action list.
  • Underwriting the Agent Economy: The Blueprint for an AI Insurance Stack, Cristian Trout, Markus Anderljung, et al. (2026). What has to be built before insurers can cover AI at scale.
  • Liability and Insurance for Catastrophic Losses: the Nuclear Power Precedent and Lessons for AI, Cristian Trout (2024). How the nuclear liability model might carry over to catastrophic AI risk.
  • AI is too risky to insure, say people whose job is insuring risk, TechCrunch (2025). Short news account of the industry's pullback and why.
  • The Insurability Frontier of AI Risk, Alex Leung, et al. (2026). Systematic map of which AI risks are, and are not, insurable today.

In their own words

Insurance, standards, and audits together create skin in the game for quantifying, communicating, and reducing AI risks so we can balance this tightrope. We call this the "Incentive Flywheel." ... Markets are a uniquely Western solution to risks. The Incentive Flywheel adapts faster than regulation, accelerates rather than slowing down progress, and has more teeth than voluntary commitments.

Rune Kvist, Rajiv Dattani, Brandon Wang (Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company), Underwriting Superintelligence, 2025

This insurance company designed and funded a flywheel of incentives through market-based governance: insurance, standards (building codes), and audits (fire inspections). ... Together, the flywheel created incentives to build houses made of brick, spread best practices and invest in better fire buckets - enabling cities to grow without putting their citizens at increased risk of death.

Rune Kvist, Rajiv Dattani, Brandon Wang (Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company), Underwriting Superintelligence, 2025
