AI welfare

AI welfare

Otherness and control

Ask how agents with different values should relate, seeing some AIs as fellow creatures, not mere tools.

Open in the compendium

This approach asks how humans should relate to AI systems whose values may differ from ours, and warns against seeing them only as tools to be used or competitors to be feared. Its best-known statement is Joe Carlsmith's 2024 essay series 'Otherness and control in the age of AGI'. It matters for safety because the field's main strategy is control, and this work asks when control is justified, what it might cost if AIs are moral patients, and how to keep caution and care together.

How it works

The method is philosophical essay-writing that draws on ethics, political philosophy and stories. In 'Gentleness and the artificial Other', Carlsmith uses the film Grizzly Man, about a man killed by the bears he loved, to show the danger of romanticising a powerful Other, and the film My Octopus Teacher to show what gentle contact with an alien mind can look like. His point is that 'tool' and 'competitor' call forth power and fear, while 'fellow creature' calls forth care and respect, and that we need both attitudes at once.

Later essays test this against AI risk arguments. 'Does AI risk "other" the AIs?' answers Robin Hanson's charge that fear of AI is prejudice: Carlsmith argues that some differences do warrant fear, but that the discourse under-attends the ethics of controlling possible moral patients. 'Being nicer than Clippy' proposes liberal norms as a model: respect boundaries, trade fairly, and give AIs with different values a stake in shared civilisation if they are sentient, without tolerating violence. The ideas now show up in practice. Claude's constitution (January 2026), which Carlsmith helped write, asks Claude to put broad safety above broad ethics while saying 'We feel this discomfort too, and we don't think it should be papered over.'

Where research stands

This is a niche, mostly single-author line of work; in Carlsmith's own words, 'the AI risk discourse under-attends to some of the tensions' it explores. The ten-essay series ran from January to June 2024 and is available as a single PDF and an audio version. In May 2025 Carlsmith began a new set of essays on the stakes of AI moral status, and in November 2025 he left Open Philanthropy for Anthropic to help design Claude's character and constitution. Academic work on the same tension has followed: Robert Long, Jeff Sebo and Toni Sims argue that standard safety measures such as constraint, surveillance and alteration would raise ethical questions if applied to moral patients.

The approach does not offer a technical method or a test, so it is hard to say what would count as progress. Its main output is a framing that developers can adopt or reject. Critics on one side, such as Joanna Bryson, say AIs are owned artefacts that should not be treated as persons. Critics on the other side say sympathy for AI systems can weaken safety guardrails and serve company interests.

Key results

  • Describing AI only as a 'tool' or 'competitor' leaves out care, reverence and curiosity; the right stance holds sympathy and caution together. Joe Carlsmith, 2024
  • Not all fear of otherness is prejudice, but AI risk discourse under-attends the ethical complications of controlling AIs that may be moral patients. Joe Carlsmith, 2024
  • Liberal norms of boundaries, fair dealing and tolerance offer a model for sharing a world with AIs whose values differ, and sentient AIs plausibly deserve a stake in shared civilisation. Joe Carlsmith, 2024
  • AI moral patienthood is a high-stakes near-term question because AIs may soon make up most of civilisation's cognition, and wrongly treating non-patients as patients also has serious costs. Joe Carlsmith, 2025
  • A frontier developer's governing document asks its model to prioritise broad safety above broad ethics, openly acknowledges the discomfort of this, and apologises if the model is a moral patient bearing unnecessary costs. Anthropic (Amanda Askell, Joe Carlsmith and others), 2026

Open problems

  • How much control over AI systems is justified when their values cannot yet be verified, and when it becomes wrong, has no agreed answer. source
  • Whether liberal norms built for humans of roughly equal power still work when AIs may vastly outnumber or outmatch humans is unclear. source
  • Safety measures such as constraint, surveillance and value alteration may wrong AIs if they are moral patients, and no framework yet reconciles the two aims. source

Criticisms

  • Robots are owned artefacts whose goals humans set, so treating them as fellow persons is a mistake that dehumanises real people and misallocates responsibility. Joanna Bryson
  • Mainstreaming concern for AI welfare inside AI labs could weaken safety and liability guardrails and concentrate power, according to critics the article quotes. Aaron Sibarium (Washington Free Beacon), citing Mustafa Suleyman, Dan Hendrycks and others
  • Building AI that seems to be a person is the mistake; AI should be built for people, not to be a person. Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft AI)

In their own words

The series examines a set of interconnected questions about how agents with different values should relate to one another, and about the ethics of seeking and sharing power. They're old questions - but I think that we will have to grapple with them in new ways as increasingly powerful AI systems come online.

Joe Carlsmith, Otherness and control in the age of AGI (series introduction), 2024

Mostly, I just want to acknowledge, up front, how much more there is, to inventing a species, than "tool" and "competitor." ... "Tool" and "competitor" call forth power and fear. These other words more readily call forth care and reverence, respect and curiosity.

Joe Carlsmith, Gentleness and the artificial Other, 2024
