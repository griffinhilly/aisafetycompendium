Proof of personhood (personhood credentials)

Let a person prove an online account belongs to a real human without revealing who they are.

Open in the compendium

Proof of personhood lets someone show an online service that they are a real, unique human without revealing who they are. As AI agents become able to pass as people cheaply and at scale, services need a way to limit fake accounts, bot swarms and AI-driven deception without demanding full identity checks. A 2024 proposal by 32 authors calls these tools "personhood credentials".

How it works

A trusted issuer, such as a government agency or another institution, checks once that an applicant is a real person and gives them a digital credential. The 2024 paper by Steven Adler, Zoë Hitzig, Shrey Jain and colleagues sets two core requirements. "Credential limits" means each eligible person gets at most one credential from a given issuer, so one person cannot run thousands of accounts. "Unlinkable pseudonymity" means the person uses a different pseudonym with each service, and cryptography stops the issuer or services from tracking them across sites. Zero-knowledge proofs, for example, prove a fact without revealing the data behind it.

A social network could require a credential before an account can post, so a bot operator could run only as many accounts as it has real people. The service learns only "this is a real person who has not already registered here", not a name. The largest deployed system, World ID, checks uniqueness with an "Orb" device that images a person's irises and face, then uses zero-knowledge proofs so that third parties cannot link different actions by the same ID. Older proposals, such as "pseudonym parties", skip biometrics and issue renewable tokens at periodic in-person events.

Where research stands

The 2024 paper gives a detailed design analysis. Its authors came from OpenAI, Microsoft, Harvard, MIT, the University of Oxford, UC Berkeley's Center for Human-Compatible AI, the Partnership on AI and identity-technology groups. It recommends piloting credentials that build on existing ones, such as digital driver's licences; re-examining remote identity checks that AI can now fool, such as live-video tests; measuring how common deceptive accounts are; and building trust infrastructure for AI agents online. There is at least one large deployment but no agreed standard.

In March 2024 Spain's data protection agency ordered Tools for Humanity, the company behind the Worldcoin (now World) project, to stop collecting personal data in Spain, citing complaints about poor information, data from minors and consent that could not be withdrawn. In December 2024 the Bavarian data protection authority found several GDPR breaches and ordered deletion of all iris codes stored since the project began. The paper itself names four challenges: equitable access, free expression, checks on power, and robustness to attack and error.

Key results

  • Personhood credentials can let users prove they are real people to online services without disclosing personal information, using two requirements: at most one credential per person per issuer, and unlinkable service-specific pseudonyms. Steven Adler, Zoë Hitzig, Shrey Jain, Catherine Brewer, Wayne Chang, Renée DiResta, Eddy Lazzarin, Sean McGregor, et al. (32 authors), 2024
  • Identity-based approaches (ID checks, biometrics, self-sovereign identity, trust networks) have basic flaws for digital participation; personhood should come first, and enhanced pseudonym parties could reduce coercion and vote-buying. Bryan Ford, 2020
  • A European regulator found that the largest biometric proof-of-personhood project breached the GDPR and ordered deletion of all iris codes stored since launch. Agencia Española de Protección de Datos (reporting a Bavarian data protection authority decision), 2024

Open problems

  • Ensure equitable access, so that people who struggle to obtain a credential are not shut out of online services. source
  • Keep issuers from becoming a point of control over who can speak online. source
  • Make credentials robust to theft, sale and coerced use, and to errors in the issuing check. source
  • Measure how common deceptive and fake accounts are, with standard methods, to know how much these tools would help. source

Criticisms

  • Governments should attest to citizenship or residency, not to whether someone is human; the personhood-credentials paper leans too heavily on governments as issuers. Daniel Hardman (W3C Credentials Community Group mailing list)
  • Biometric uniqueness checks collect highly sensitive data; Spain's regulator halted one such project over inadequate information, data from minors and consent that could not be withdrawn. Agencia Española de Protección de Datos (AEPD)

Status

Proposal with design analysis. The paper reviews benefits and deployment risks and gives next steps for policymakers, technologists and standards bodies. A related biometric proof-of-personhood system, Worldcoin, is already running and has met regulatory action in Europe: in December 2024 Bavaria's data protection authority ordered it to delete all iris codes stored since the project began.

Not yet named in any of the 24 overview sources in the chart. It is here because the people under “Who works on it” have published or practised it.

In their own words

In this paper, we analyze the value of a new tool to address this challenge: "personhood credentials" (PHCs), digital credentials that empower users to demonstrate that they are real people -- not AIs -- to online services, without disclosing any personal information. Such credentials can be issued by a range of trusted institutions -- governments or otherwise.

Steven Adler, Zoë Hitzig, Shrey Jain et al. (32 authors), Personhood credentials: Artificial intelligence and the value of privacy-preserving tools to distinguish who is real online, 2024

Credential limits: The issuer of a PHC gives at most one credential to an eligible person. ... Unlinkable pseudonymity: PHCs let a user interact with services anonymously through a service-specific pseudonym; the user's digital activity is untraceable by the issuer and unlinkable across service providers, even if service providers and issuers collude.

Steven Adler, Zoë Hitzig, Shrey Jain et al. (32 authors), Personhood credentials: Artificial intelligence and the value of privacy-preserving tools to distinguish who is real online, 2024
