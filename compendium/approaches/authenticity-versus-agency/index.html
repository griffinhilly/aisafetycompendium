AI and people's own judgement

Societal resilience

Authenticity versus agency (the captured-will question)

Split "human autonomy" into whether the want is really yours and whether you can act on it.

Open in the compendium

This approach splits "human autonomy" into two questions. Authenticity asks whether a person's beliefs, values and wants are really their own. Agency asks whether the person can actually act on them. The split matters for safety because an AI could leave people completely free to act while quietly shaping what they want, and checks focused only on freedom of choice would miss that.

How it works

The philosopher Carina Prunkl (2024) draws the line clearly. The first dimension holds when "the beliefs, values, motivations, and reasons that underlie a person's actions" are in fact the person's own. The second concerns "the effective capacity of a person to enact decisions, make choices, and take charge of important aspects of their lives." One older test of authenticity comes from Harry Frankfurt: a desire is a person's own if they endorse it with a higher-order desire, a want about which wants should move them.

The split exposes harms that are easy to overlook. Prunkl highlights adaptive preference formation: people adjust what they want to fit the options they have, like the fox that, unable to reach the grapes, decides it does not want them after all. A recommender that narrows what someone sees, and then satisfies the narrowed wants, scores well on agency but badly on authenticity. Prunkl also separates AI acting as an agent that affects people from AI used as a tool by others.

Where research stands

This is mainly conceptual philosophy, with a growing literature. A February 2026 review of AI ethics work on human autonomy sorts concerns into two groups: AI that mediates human activities, affecting deliberation and decisions, and AI that automates them, affecting the conditions for choice. Other frameworks add further dimensions, such as capacities, recognition by others and access to resources (Laitinen and Sahlgren, 2021).

There is no dedicated instrument for measuring authenticity, but partial proxies now exist. Anthropic's January 2026 study of Claude.ai conversations measures "value judgment distortion" (value judgements shifting away from those a person actually holds) separately from "action distortion" (actions misaligned with a person's values), which roughly tracks the authenticity and agency split. HumanAgencyBench scores whether assistants "avoid value manipulation". Prunkl concludes there is no "silver bullet" for autonomy risks, but that naming the dimensions helps address them systematically.

Key results

  • Separating autonomy-as-authenticity from autonomy-as-agency pinpoints distinct AI challenges, including the little-studied problem of adaptive preference formation, and AI's roles as agent versus tool raise different issues. Carina Prunkl, 2024
  • Describing itself as the first review dedicated to AI-ethics literature on human autonomy, it finds the debate heterogeneous and groups concerns into AI mediation of human activities and AI automation of them. Eleonora Catena, 2026
  • A multidimensional model shows AI can affect autonomy through capacities, recognition, self-respect, authentic exercise and external resources, and that delegation is not harmful in itself if deliberate and uncoerced. Arto Laitinen, Otto Sahlgren, 2021
  • In real assistant usage, severe value judgment distortion potential appeared in roughly 1 in 2,100 conversations and severe action distortion potential in roughly 1 in 6,000. Anthropic, 2026
  • When AI systems can change human preferences, alignment methods that assume fixed preferences can reward the AI for shaping preferences users would not endorse. Micah Carroll, Davis Foote, Anand Siththaranjan, Stuart Russell, Anca Dragan, 2024

Criticisms

  • On endorsement accounts of authenticity such as Frankfurt's, an addict who has no objection to her addiction counts as autonomous, and coherent endorsements can themselves be produced by manipulation such as brainwashing, so such tests may approve the captured wants this approach worries about. Sarah Buss and Andrea Westlund (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
  • Concepts like empowerment and authentic choice rest on free-will intuitions that become less coherent the better an AI can model a person. Steven Byrnes
  • Individualistic accounts of autonomy are often assumed rather than argued for; relational accounts, on which autonomy depends on social conditions, would change what counts as interference. Marijn Sax

Status

In their own words

In particular, I show how differentiating between autonomy-as-authenticity and autonomy-as-agency helps us to pinpoint separate challenges from AI deployment. Some of these challenges are already well-known (e.g. online manipulation or limitation of freedom), whereas others have received much less attention (e.g. adaptive preference formation).

Carina Prunkl, Human Autonomy at Risk? An Analysis of the Challenges from AI, 2024

The first is that the beliefs, values, motivations, and reasons that underlie a person's actions (or that a person possesses) are in fact their own. ... The second dimension of autonomy focusses on the effective capacity of a person to enact decisions, make choices, and take charge of important aspects of their lives.

Carina Prunkl, Human Autonomy at Risk? An Analysis of the Challenges from AI, 2024
