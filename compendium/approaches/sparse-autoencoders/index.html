Sparse autoencoders and causal abstraction

Break a model's internals into parts a person can understand, using specific tools such as sparse autoencoders and causal abstraction.

Open in the compendium

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and causal abstraction are two tools for breaking a model's internals into parts a person can understand. SAEs pull out many single-concept 'features' from a layer without being told what to look for. Causal abstraction tests whether a human-readable model of an algorithm matches what the network computes. For safety, they promise a way to find concepts such as deception or power-seeking inside a model, and to check explanations rigorously.

How it works

Single neurons in language models often respond to several unrelated things. A leading explanation is 'superposition', in which the model stores more concepts than it has neurons by packing them into overlapping patterns. An SAE is a second, much wider network trained on recorded activations from one layer. It must rebuild those activations while using only a few of its units (features) for any one input. Each feature then tends to stand for one concept, which researchers label by reading the texts that activate it most. In May 2024 Anthropic used this method to extract millions of features from Claude 3 Sonnet. Turning up one feature for the Golden Gate Bridge made Claude claim to be the bridge. A variant, the transcoder, imitates a layer's MLP block and is the building block of Anthropic's circuit tracing.

Causal abstraction works from the other direction. A researcher writes a simple, human-readable algorithm the network might be running, then aligns its variables with parts of the network. 'Interchange interventions' swap those parts between two inputs and check whether the network's output changes exactly as the simple algorithm predicts. Distributed alignment search finds these alignments by gradient descent instead of brute force.

Where research stands

SAEs drew heavy investment in 2024, with large open releases. OpenAI trained a 16-million-feature SAE on GPT-4, Google DeepMind released Gemma Scope (over 400 SAEs, more than 30 million features), and Goodfire released SAEs for Llama 3.1 8B and Llama 3.3 70B in January 2025. Enthusiasm then cooled. In March 2025 Google DeepMind's interpretability team reported that SAE-based probes did worse than simple linear probes at detecting harmful intent on new inputs, and said it was deprioritising fundamental SAE research. On other benchmarks, simple baselines beat SAEs for steering, and SAE probes rarely beat ordinary probes. SAEs trained on random, untrained networks also score about as well on common quality metrics.

For open-ended discovery, though, SAEs still help. OpenAI used SAE comparisons to find a 'toxic persona' feature behind emergent misalignment, and an SAE helped one team in Anthropic's auditing game find a hidden objective. Google DeepMind's Gemma Scope 2 suite now covers every layer of Gemma 3 with SAEs and transcoders.

Causal abstraction is smaller and more academic. Its main contribution is a common theory: a 2023 paper, last revised in May 2025, shows that patching, causal tracing, circuit analysis, SAEs and steering can all be described as forms of causal abstraction. Its authors also built AxBench, the benchmark that found SAEs uncompetitive for steering.

Key results

  • Dictionary learning on a 512-neuron layer of a small transformer extracted more than 4,000 features for topics such as DNA sequences, legal text and Hebrew, which were invisible at the level of single neurons. Anthropic, 2023
  • SAEs scaled to Claude 3 Sonnet found millions of features, including safety-relevant ones for code backdoors, power-seeking, manipulation, secrecy and sycophantic praise. Anthropic, 2024
  • k-sparse autoencoders gave clean scaling laws and supported a 16-million-latent SAE trained on GPT-4 activations. Leo Gao, Tom Dupré la Tour, Henk Tillman et al. (OpenAI), 2024
  • Comparing SAE features before and after fine-tuning surfaced a 'toxic persona' feature that most strongly controls emergent misalignment and predicts it. Miles Wang, Tom Dupré la Tour, Olivia Watkins et al. (OpenAI), 2025
  • SAE-based probes underperformed simple linear probes at detecting harmful intent on out-of-distribution prompts, where linear probes reached about 0.999 AUROC. Lewis Smith, Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Arthur Conmy et al. (Google DeepMind), 2025
  • On a steering and concept-detection benchmark for Gemma-2 models, prompting and simple difference-in-means methods beat SAEs, which were not competitive on either task. Zhengxuan Wu, Aryaman Arora, Atticus Geiger et al., 2025

Open problems

  • Finding a full set of features is cost-prohibitive with current methods, so every SAE is a partial list of a model's concepts. source
  • Common SAE quality metrics often cannot tell trained models from random ones, so the field needs better evaluations, such as randomised baselines. source
  • Showing that SAEs add value on real downstream safety tasks over strong simple baselines. source
  • Causal abstraction needs a candidate high-level algorithm to test, and searching for good alignments in large models is hard. source

Criticisms

  • The interpretability community is somewhat over-invested in SAEs: they lost to linear probes on a safety-relevant detection task, so the team deprioritised fundamental SAE research. Google DeepMind mechanistic interpretability team
  • Even simple baselines outperform SAEs for steering, and difference-in-means beats them for concept detection. Zhengxuan Wu, Christopher Potts et al. (AxBench)
  • High automated interpretability scores do not by themselves show that meaningful features were recovered, since SAEs on random networks score similarly. Thomas Heap, Tim Lawson, Lucy Farnik, Laurence Aitchison
  • In an auditing game, a plain semantic search over training data was as effective as SAEs at surfacing the evidence, so the SAE approach may have worked for a simpler reason. Anthropic (auditing hidden objectives study)

Who works on it

  • Anthropic (interpretability team): Pioneered dictionary learning on language models and scaled it to Claude; now builds on transcoders. Mapping the Mind of a Large Language Model (2024)
  • OpenAI: Trained SAEs on GPT-4 and used SAE model-diffing to find persona features behind misalignment. Persona Features Control Emergent Misalignment (2025)
  • Google DeepMind (mechanistic interpretability team): Released the open Gemma Scope SAE suites and published negative results for SAEs on downstream tasks. Gemma Scope (2024)
  • Goodfire: Released open SAEs for Llama 3.1 8B and Llama 3.3 70B that power its Ember interpretability API. Open-source SAEs for Llama 3.1 8B and Llama 3.3 70B (2025)
  • EleutherAI: Built an open pipeline that uses language models to label and score millions of SAE features. Automatically Interpreting Millions of Features in Large Language Models (2024)

In their own words

We used a technique called "dictionary learning", borrowed from classical machine learning, which isolates patterns of neuron activations that recur across many different contexts. In turn, any internal state of the model can be represented in terms of a few active features instead of many active neurons.

Anthropic, Mapping the Mind of a Large Language Model, 2024

In this method, neural representations are aligned with variables in interpretable causal models, and then interchange interventions are used to experimentally verify that the neural representations have the causal properties of their aligned variables.

Atticus Geiger, Hanson Lu, Thomas Icard, Christopher Potts, Causal Abstractions of Neural Networks, 2021
