Planned handover of AGI control

A lab commits in advance to hand control of an aligned AGI to a more democratically legitimate body.

Open in the compendium

A planned handover means an AI company commits in advance that, if it builds an aligned artificial general intelligence (AGI), it will not keep control itself but pass it to a more democratically legitimate body. The idea responds to a gap that alignment research leaves open: even an AI that does exactly what its owners want raises the question of who those owners should be. A company answerable to shareholders, the argument goes, should hold such power only briefly.

How it works

Alan Chan set out the idea in a January 2023 essay, 'Alignment is not enough'. He imagines a company, 'Onus', that announces an aligned AGI. Its shareholders were not elected to represent humanity, so its control should be treated as a transition to a more legitimate controller. The ideal would be a global democratic government. A more practical option is an 'AGI security council' of major states such as the US and China, in which the AGI could be used only if all members agreed, with the technical keys split among them.

For this to work, the commitment has to be made and built into the company before the AGI exists. Chan lists what would reassure him: leading companies broadening their boards, publicly committing to externally vetted plans even at some cost to capability, and eventually handing over the AGI. Some labs have built partial versions of such commitments. Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust, set up in September 2023, is a body of five financially disinterested trustees that will elect a majority of the company's board within four years. OpenAI's 2018 Charter pledged that if a 'value-aligned, safety-conscious project' came close to AGI first, OpenAI would stop competing with it and start assisting it.

Where research stands

This is a single-author essay proposal. No lab has publicly committed to hand control of an AGI to an outside public body, and no candidate body exists. The literature is thin, so the approach has few organisations working on it directly. Nearby work asks what a legitimate controller would look like: Forethought's International AGI Project series (discussed in a January 2026 podcast) compares UN-led, single-government, company and coalition models, including an 'Intelsat model' that treats AGI as commercial infrastructure.

The record of voluntary corporate commitments is mixed. OpenAI's nonprofit, renamed the OpenAI Foundation, kept control of its for-profit arm when the company recapitalised in October 2025, and Delaware's attorney general did not object. But in April 2026 OpenAI published new 'principles' that do not mention stepping aside for a safety-focused rival, the clause of its 2018 Charter that came closest to a handover commitment; the Charter itself is still posted on OpenAI's site. The Future of Life Institute's AI Safety Index now grades company structures and mandates as one sign of whether such commitments are durable.

Key results

  • Chan argues alignment is necessary but not sufficient, and that a company holding an aligned AGI should treat its control as a transition to a more legitimate body, such as an AGI security council acting by unanimity. Alan Chan, 2023
  • Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust holds a special class of stock that lets it elect a growing share of the board, reaching a majority within four years, to balance stockholder interests with those of people affected by the company. Anthropic, 2023
  • OpenAI's April 2026 principles do not repeat the 2018 Charter's pledge to stop competing with and assist a safety-conscious project close to AGI; they promise to work with governments, international agencies and other AGI efforts to solve serious alignment, safety or societal problems before proceeding further. Sam Altman (OpenAI), 2026
  • In OpenAI's October 2025 recapitalisation, the nonprofit OpenAI Foundation kept control of the new OpenAI Group public benefit corporation, and Delaware's attorney general issued a statement of no objection. Fortune, 2025

Open problems

  • Identifying a body that is both legitimate and able to act, since a global democratic government does not exist and building one takes time and may fail. source
  • Making a company's handover commitment binding, so it survives commercial pressure and changes of leadership. source
  • Choosing among governance models for an AGI project, from UN-led bodies to coalitions of states to commercial consortia. source
  • Handing over control without giving the receiving body, or a single government, unchecked power. source

Criticisms

  • Companies and states are both transitional; a team of smart, ethical people with a lot of slack, which could be a company, might steward a superintelligence better than governments. Donald Hobson, comment on 'Alignment is not enough'
  • Concentrating control of AGI in one project, including a government-led one, risks dangerous power concentration. Rose Hadshar and Tom Davidson, Forethought
  • The author himself doubts that an aligned AGI could safely solve the control question, since it might require coercion, and first uses of force make later ones easier to justify. Alan Chan

Who works on it

  • Forethought: Runs an International AGI Project series comparing who could legitimately govern AGI development. Should There Be an International AGI Project? (2026)

In their own words

Should Onus be the sole controllers of the AGI? I think the answer is very clearly no. Onus is a tech company with shareholder obligations, shareholders who were not elected to represent humanity. ... We should treat Onus's control as a transition period to a regime where we have a more legitimate body control the AGI.

Alan Chan, Alignment is not enough, 2023

What sort of body might actually be achievable? I'm just throwing ideas out here, but maybe we can get the big states like China and the US on board for a kind of AGI security council (AGISC). The idea is that the AGI could only be used collectively if the body unanimously agrees to each application.

Alan Chan, Alignment is not enough, 2023
