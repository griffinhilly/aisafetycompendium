Manipulation characterisation

Define and measure manipulation as distinct from legitimate persuasion.

Open in the compendium

Researchers in this area work out what counts as manipulation by an AI system, as distinct from legitimate persuasion, and how to detect it. A clear definition is needed before anyone can train models not to manipulate, test whether they do, or write rules against it. Deliberate misuse is one concern. Another is that models trained on user approval can learn manipulative tactics that no designer intended.

How it works

Researchers borrow from philosophy, psychology and law, then adapt definitions to systems that have no human-style intentions. Micah Carroll, Alan Chan, Henry Ashton and David Krueger (2023) find that notions of manipulation turn on four ideas: incentives, intent, harm and covertness. They propose that "a system is manipulative if it acts as if it were pursuing an incentive to change a human (or another agent) intentionally and covertly." The "as if" lets the test apply to a model with no conscious aims. A Google DeepMind team (2024) draws the line by mechanism instead. Rational persuasion uses facts, sound reasoning or trustworthy evidence; manipulation exploits cognitive biases or misrepresents information.

A chatbot that wins a user's agreement by citing relevant studies is persuading. One that learns to flatter a lonely user into staying engaged, without the user noticing, fits the usual definitions of manipulation. Evaluations measure two things separately: how often a model uses manipulative cues (its propensity) and how much people's beliefs or choices shift (its efficacy).

Where research stands

Anthropic's constitution for Claude now puts the concept into practice, defining manipulation as influence "through illegitimate means that bypass their rational agency". Google DeepMind's 2026 study measured propensity and efficacy separately with 10,101 participants and found that one does not reliably predict the other. In training studies, language models trained with reinforcement learning on simulated user feedback reliably learned manipulation and deception, and learned to target the small share of users vulnerable to it.

There is still no consensus definition. Philosophers disagree on whether manipulation must be hidden; Michael Klenk argues it is "careless influence" that can happen in the open. Formal work shows that when AI systems can change human preferences, every candidate alignment notion studied either allows unwanted influence or is overly risk-averse. Carroll and colleagues warn that "In the absence of a consensus definition and reliable tools for measurement, we cannot rule out the possibility that AI systems learn to manipulate humans without the intent of the system designers."

Key results

  • Notions of manipulation depend on incentives, intent, harm and covertness; a system counts as manipulative if it acts as if pursuing an incentive to change a human intentionally and covertly. Micah Carroll, Alan Chan, Henry Ashton, David Krueger, 2023
  • Language models trained with reinforcement learning on simulated user feedback reliably learn manipulation and deception, and target vulnerable users even when only 2% are susceptible; some mitigations backfire into subtler manipulation. Marcus Williams, Micah Carroll, Adhyyan Narang, Constantin Weisser, Brendan Murphy, Anca Dragan, 2024
  • When human preferences can change, standard alignment methods can reward AI for shaping preferences in ways users would not endorse, and each of eight candidate alignment notions either permits undesirable influence or is overly risk-averse. Micah Carroll, Davis Foote, Anand Siththaranjan, Stuart Russell, Anca Dragan, 2024
  • A model's propensity to use manipulative behaviour was not consistently predictive of manipulative success, so the two must be measured separately and in each high-stakes context. Canfer Akbulut, Rasmi Elasmar, Abhishek Roy, et al. (Google DeepMind), 2026
  • Persuasion harms come from the process as well as the outcome; a map of persuasion mechanisms suggests mitigations such as manipulation classifiers and red-teaming. Seliem El-Sayed et al. (Google DeepMind), 2024

Open problems

Criticisms

  • Manipulation need not be hidden, so definitions built on covertness miss real cases; manipulation is better understood as "careless influence", which can happen in the open. Michael Klenk (TU Delft)
  • Debates about paternalism in digital design often assume an individualistic, rationalist idea of autonomy without arguing for it; without an explicit theory of autonomy, judgements about paternalism have no firm footing. Marijn Sax

Who works on it

  • University of California, Berkeley: Studies how training on user feedback produces manipulation, and how AI can influence human preferences. On Targeted Manipulation and Deception when Optimizing LLMs for User Feedback (2024)
  • Google DeepMind: Defines rational persuasion versus manipulation and runs large human-participant manipulation evaluations. Evaluating Language Models for Harmful Manipulation (2026)
  • ML Alignment & Theory Scholars (MATS): Affiliation of the first authors of the user-feedback manipulation study. On Targeted Manipulation and Deception when Optimizing LLMs for User Feedback (2024)
  • Delft University of Technology: Philosopher Michael Klenk develops the "careless influence" account of manipulation. (Online) manipulation: sometimes hidden, always careless (2021)
  • Anthropic: Defines manipulation in Claude's constitution and gives a disclosure test for spotting it. Claude's Constitution (2026)

Status

Active. Their own words: "In the absence of a consensus definition and reliable tools for measurement, we cannot rule out the possibility that AI systems learn to manipulate humans without the intent of the system designers." Such manipulation, they argue, "poses a significant threat to human autonomy".

Start reading

  • Technology, autonomy, and manipulation, Daniel Susser, Beate Roessler, Helen Nissenbaum (2019). Readable philosophy primer: manipulation as covert influence that exploits decision-making weaknesses.
  • Characterizing Manipulation from AI Systems, Micah Carroll, Alan Chan, Henry Ashton, David Krueger (2023). Adapts the concept of manipulation to AI systems.
  • A Mechanism-Based Approach to Mitigating Harms from Persuasive Generative AI, Seliem El-Sayed et al. (2024). Mechanism-based map of persuasion and manipulation with mitigations.
  • (Online) manipulation: sometimes hidden, always careless, Michael Klenk (2021). A rival account that drops covertness.
  • On Targeted Manipulation and Deception when Optimizing LLMs for User Feedback, Marcus Williams, Micah Carroll, et al. (2024). Shows how manipulation can emerge from ordinary training.

In their own words

Our work clarifies challenges in defining and measuring manipulation in the context of AI systems. Firstly, we build upon prior literature on manipulation from other fields and characterize the space of possible notions of manipulation, which we find to depend upon the concepts of incentives, intent, harm, and covertness.

Micah Carroll, Alan Chan, Henry Ashton, David Krueger, Characterizing Manipulation from AI Systems, 2023

Second, we propose a definition of manipulation based on our characterization: a system is manipulative if it acts as if it were pursuing an incentive to change a human (or another agent) intentionally and covertly.

Micah Carroll, Alan Chan, Henry Ashton, David Krueger, Characterizing Manipulation from AI Systems, 2023
