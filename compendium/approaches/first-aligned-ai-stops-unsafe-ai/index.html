Using the first aligned AI to stop unsafe AI

A limited AI that its builders can control is used for one decisive act, such as stopping hostile superintelligent AI from emerging.

Open in the compendium

This idea holds that whoever first builds an AI they can reliably control should use it for one decisive action, a "pivotal act", that stops anyone else from building uncontrolled superintelligent AI. Eliezer Yudkowsky, founder of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI), and colleagues developed the concept. It is among the most contested ideas in AI safety: supporters see it as possibly the only way out of a race, while critics see it as a recipe for fear, arms races and unaccountable power.

How it works

The argument starts from a worry about timing. If many groups can eventually build powerful AI, and aligning it is very hard, then sooner or later someone will build one that is unsafe. So, the argument goes, the first team with an aligned system must use it to "flip the gameboard" while few or only one actor has such a system. Yudkowsky's 2022 "AGI Ruin" essay gives "burn all GPUs" as a deliberately extreme placeholder example, and says it is not what he thinks one would actually do. His key claim is that no "pivotal weak act" exists: anything strong enough to stop every other project is too powerful to be safe just because it is weak. So the act needs a highly capable system that is truly aligned.

The term "pivotal act" is a guarded definition on the LessWrong wiki. It means actions that decisively settle a win, as opposed to work that only has some indirect effect.

Related but distinct ideas appear in national-security strategy. Leopold Aschenbrenner's 2024 "Situational Awareness" argues that whoever leads on superintelligence gains a "decisive military advantage". Dario Amodei's October 2024 essay proposes an "entente strategy" in which democracies seek a clear, even temporary, AI advantage and offer its benefits to countries that support them. These are about national advantage rather than a single act, but they share the logic of using an early lead to set the rules.

Where research stands

This is a debated concept with no public programme behind it, and its original home has moved away from it in practice. In January 2024 MIRI announced that policy and communications were now its top focus. Its main policy goal is an international agreement to halt progress toward smarter-than-human AI, or at least an "off switch" capability. Andrew Critch's 2022 counter-proposal of "pivotal processes" (the same safe outcome achieved by many institutions acting together) offers an alternative from within the safety community.

The strategic environment has also shifted. In March 2025 Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt and Alexandr Wang argued that "any state's aggressive bid for unilateral AI dominance" would likely be met with sabotage by rivals, a regime they call Mutual Assured AI Malfunction (MAIM). On that view, openly preparing a pivotal act would invite pre-emptive attack. Debate continues on whether an AI-enabled decisive advantage is coming anyway, and if so who should hold it.

Key results

  • Defines a pivotal act as an event that decisively settles a win or drastically raises its probability, and guards the term against over-extension to merely related work. Eliezer Yudkowsky, Rob Bensinger, et al., 2026
  • Argues no passively safe "pivotal weak act" exists; preventing other unaligned AGI requires aligning a system that can do some single very large thing. Eliezer Yudkowsky, 2022
  • Intending a pivotal act harms the institution that holds the intention (bad external and internal relations, fear among rivals), and the arguments for it are flawed. Andrew Critch, 2022
  • The same safe outcome could come from a "pivotal process" in which many states and companies each build parts of the solution, with less extreme risk-taking. Andrew Critch, 2022
  • Any state's aggressive bid for unilateral AI dominance is likely to be met by preventive sabotage from rivals, a deterrence regime already in place by default. Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt, Alexandr Wang, 2025

Criticisms

  • A lab intending to forcibly shut down others will spread fear and drive rivals to race for their own version, while damaging trust inside and outside the lab. Andrew Critch
  • If we have to limit people, it is better to limit everyone equally and cooperate, rather than one party seeking dominance, which combines the flaws of races and centralisation. Vitalik Buterin
  • Bids for unilateral AI dominance invite preventive sabotage from rival states, making them destabilising. Dan Hendrycks, Eric Schmidt and Alexandr Wang

Who works on it

  • Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI): Founded by Eliezer Yudkowsky, who set out the case for a pivotal act; since 2024 its top focus is policy for an international halt. MIRI 2024 Mission and Strategy Update (2024)

Status

Debated concept since 2015.

In their own words

The term 'pivotal act' in the context of AI alignment theory is a guarded term to refer to actions that will make a large positive difference a billion years later. ... researchers use 'pivotal' and 'pivotal act' to refer to good events that upset the current gameboard - events that decisively settle a win, or drastically increase the probability of a win.

Eliezer Yudkowsky, Rob Bensinger, et al., Pivotal act (LessWrong wiki, imported from Arbital), 2015

We need to align the performance of some large task, a 'pivotal act' that prevents other people from building an unaligned AGI that destroys the world. While the number of actors with AGI is few or one, they must execute some "pivotal act", strong enough to flip the gameboard, using an AGI powerful enough to do that.

Eliezer Yudkowsky, AGI Ruin: A List of Lethalities, 2022
