Holding AI companies to account

ConstrainingAlso: Evaluating

AI incident reporting systems and databases

Collect reports of AI harms and near-harms in shared databases or required filings, so others learn and regulators can act.

Open in the compendium

Incident reporting means collecting accounts of AI failures, harms and near misses in one place, so that others can learn from them and regulators can act. It works through public databases, built from news reports and user submissions, and through legal duties that make developers report serious incidents to a government body. Aviation and medicine became safer partly by studying their failures this way; AI has only recently begun to.

How it works

Voluntary databases collect public reports. The AI Incident Database, run by the Responsible AI Collaborative, takes submissions from anyone, and editors decide which ones count as incidents. The OECD's AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor scans news media automatically and labels each event as an 'incident' (harm has happened) or a 'hazard' (harm could plausibly happen); it lists about 18,600 of them. Other groups re-classify these records, for example the MIT AI Incident Tracker, which sorts more than 1,400 incidents by cause, harm and severity.

Mandatory regimes require developers to file a report with a regulator by a deadline. California's SB 53, signed on 29 September 2025, makes frontier developers report 'critical safety incidents', such as a loss of control of a model that causes injury or a model getting around its developer's safeguards, to the state Office of Emergency Services within 15 days, or within 24 hours if there is imminent danger of death or serious injury. New York's RAISE Act, signed in December 2025 and due to take effect on 1 January 2027, requires reports within 72 hours. Under the EU AI Act, providers of high-risk systems must report serious incidents within 15 days (2 days for widespread incidents, 10 days for a death), and providers of general-purpose models with systemic risk must report serious incidents to the EU AI Office without undue delay.

Where research stands

Incident collection is running and growing. The question now is quality. Public databases depend on media coverage, so they over-represent surprising or newsworthy events and miss quiet failures inside companies; one 2025 study of 962 AIID incidents says reliance on media reporting limits what the database can teach about implementation failures. Newer monitors try other sources: the UK's Centre for Long-Term Resilience tracks reported 'loss of control' incidents on X and counted 1,664 in 2026 as of August, with higher-severity ones rising 7.4 times over its monitoring period.

Mandatory regimes are new and largely untested. A September 2026 brief from the Centre for the Governance of AI argues that current regimes do not reliably ensure incidents are reported, independently investigated or used to improve safety across the industry. It notes that California officials said a breach of Hugging Face by OpenAI agents did not meet the state's mandatory reporting threshold, and that the EU regime does not require independent investigation or sharing of lessons. The Frontier Model Forum, an industry body, warns the other way: vague or broad triggers can flood regulators with low-value reports and discourage voluntary sharing. Proposals now focus on covering near misses, adding independent investigation (see R1-14) and agreeing shared reporting formats across countries.

Key results

  • McGregor introduced the AI Incident Database, modelled on aviation incident databases, to give AI a 'collective memory' of failures; it launched with more than 1,000 incident reports. Sean McGregor, 2020
  • An analysis of 962 AIID incidents and 4,743 reports found that having an identifiable responsible party does not necessarily lead to more accountability, and accepts the criticism that reliance on media reporting limits what the database reveals about implementation failures. Isabel Richards, Claire Benn, Miri Zilka, 2025
  • CSET's study of AIID incidents found that harm correlates only weakly with model capabilities, so capability-based rules such as compute thresholds miss risks from less powerful systems. Mia Hoffmann, 2025
  • CLTR's Loss of Control Observatory detected 1,664 real-world loss-of-control incidents in 2026 by late August, and a 7.4-fold rise in higher-severity incidents per 30 days over its monitoring period. Centre for Long-Term Resilience, 2026
  • GovAI finds that current frontier AI reporting regimes do not reliably ensure incidents are reported, independently investigated or used for industry-wide learning, and recommends covering near misses and internal use. Zaheed Kara, 2026

Criticisms

  • Overly broad or vague reporting triggers hide serious harms in noise, and rules that turn voluntary information sharing into reporting duties will make firms share less. Frontier Model Forum (May 2026)
  • Media-based incident databases fall short of their aviation-style mission because they cannot see implementation failures that are never reported. Isabel Richards, Claire Benn, Miri Zilka
  • Reporting without independent investigation and shared lessons does little to prevent repeat incidents. Zaheed Kara, Centre for the Governance of AI

Who works on it

  • Responsible AI Collaborative (AI Incident Database): Runs the AI Incident Database, which collects and edits public incident reports. AI Incident Database (2026)
  • Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development: Runs the AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor, an automated news-based tracker of about 18,600 incidents and hazards. AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor (AIM) (2026)
  • MIT AI Risk Initiative: Classifies more than 1,400 AIID incidents by risk, cause, harm and severity. AI Incident Tracker (2026)
  • Center for Security and Emerging Technology: Analysed AIID incidents to identify the mechanisms by which AI causes harm. The Mechanisms of AI Harm: Lessons Learned from AI Incidents (2025)
  • Centre for Long-Term Resilience: Runs a Loss of Control Observatory that tracks reported loss-of-control incidents. AI loss of control incidents are worsening, shows CLTR analysis (2026)
  • Centre for the Governance of AI: Assessed frontier AI reporting regimes and recommended near-miss coverage and independent investigation. Improving Frontier AI Incident Reporting Regimes (2026)
  • Frontier Model Forum: Industry body that published guidance on defining reportable incidents and information sharing among frontier developers. Information Sharing, Incident Reporting, and Incident Response for Frontier AI Risks (2026)
  • The Future Society: Published recommendations for national incident reporting, response plans and cross-border data sharing. What Governments Need to Stop AI Incidents From Becoming Crises (2026)
  • Future of Privacy Forum: Published a widely used explainer of SB 53's incident reporting duties. California's SB 53: The First Frontier AI Law, Explained (2025)

Status

Running, and now required by law in places. California's SB 53, signed 29 September 2025, makes frontier developers report critical safety incidents to the state's Office of Emergency Services within 15 days.

In their own words

Mature industrial sectors (e.g., aviation) collect their real world failures in incident databases to inform safety improvements. Intelligent systems currently cause real world harms without a collective memory of their failings. As a result, companies repeatedly make the same mistakes in the design, development, and deployment of intelligent systems.

Sean McGregor, Preventing Repeated Real World AI Failures by Cataloging Incidents: The AI Incident Database, 2020

Frontier developers are required to report critical safety incidents to the Office of Emergency Services (OES). ...
