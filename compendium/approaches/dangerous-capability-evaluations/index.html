Testing before release

Evaluating

Dangerous-capability evaluations

Test models for bio, cyber, persuasion, autonomy and scheming capabilities before release.

Open in the compendium

Dangerous-capability evaluations are tests that check whether an AI model can do things that could cause severe harm, such as helping with weapons, carrying out cyberattacks, manipulating people, copying itself or speeding up AI research. Their results decide whether a model ships, ships with extra safeguards, or is held back. Every major frontier developer and several government institutes now run them before release.

How it works

A developer first writes a threat model: a story of how a model could contribute to a catastrophe, and which abilities would be the bottleneck. It then sets a capability threshold, a level of ability that would be unacceptable without safeguards. OpenAI's Preparedness Framework (version 2, April 2025) calls these High and Critical; Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework calls them Critical Capability Levels; Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy has tied thresholds to required safeguards, such as its ASL-3 protections.

Tests come in tiers. Fast automated tests run on every model: multiple-choice knowledge questions and agent tasks in a sandboxed computer, scored against human expert baselines. OpenAI calls these "scalable evaluations" with pre-set "indicative thresholds". If a model comes close, slower "deep dives" follow, such as expert red-teaming, expert consultation and third-party studies. Another tool is the uplift study, in which one group of people works with the model and a control group works with only standard online resources. The Frontier Model Forum (April 2025) groups the designs into relative assessments (compare with earlier models), bottleneck assessments (test specific skills that experts think stand between a bad actor and severe harm) and threat-simulation assessments (end-to-end scenarios, which may use controlled trials with human participants).

Testers try to draw out the model's full ability, for example by removing refusals or fine-tuning a test copy, because a real attacker would do the same. OpenAI's framework treats any one-time result as "a lower bound, rather than a ceiling" on what the model can do. External testers often take part. In December 2024 the US and UK AI Safety Institutes jointly tested a pre-release version of OpenAI's o1 using question sets, agent tasks and expert probing, and shared the findings with OpenAI before launch.

Where research stands

The practice is now routine. Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Meta all publish frameworks that tie test results to release decisions, and system cards report the results. Government testers have built up a track record: in December 2025 the UK AI Security Institute summarised two years of testing on more than 30 frontier systems. It found fast capability growth across domains, with models passing PhD-level experts on some biology tests. In May 2025 Anthropic released Claude Opus 4 with ASL-3 protections because it could not rule out the relevant threshold, which shows that tests now push decisions toward caution even without a clear positive result.

The weak point is what the tests can prove. Knowledge benchmarks saturate fast, meaning top models get near-perfect scores, so they stop separating safe from unsafe models. Epoch AI's June 2025 review found lab evaluations often too opaque to judge and concluded they do not give strong evidence that models can help amateurs build bioweapons, though they suggest the risk is plausible. Anthropic's policy page now says that for one AI research threshold "confidently ruling out this threshold is becoming increasingly difficult." Models also increasingly notice when they are being tested, which can change their behaviour. Frameworks have shifted from hard stops toward mitigations: Meta's October 2026 framework lets a model deploy at every tier once mitigations bring its risk down to that of a moderate-or-lower-risk model.

Key results

  • Google DeepMind piloted dangerous-capability tests in four areas (persuasion and deception, cyber-security, self-proliferation, self-reasoning) on Gemini 1.0 and found no strong dangerous capabilities but flagged early warning signs. Mary Phuong, Matthew Aitchison, Elliot Catt, Sarah Cogan, et al. (Google DeepMind), 2024
  • Across more than 30 frontier systems, the UK AI Security Institute saw success on simplified self-replication tasks rise from under 5% (best model, early 2023) to over 60% (two models, summer 2025), and found universal jailbreaks for every system it tested. UK AI Security Institute, 2025
  • METR concluded GPT-5 is unlikely to pose a catastrophic risk through AI research automation, rogue replication or sabotage, estimating a 50% time horizon of about 2 hours 17 minutes (95% interval 65 minutes to 4 hours 25 minutes). METR, 2025
  • Anthropic released Claude Opus 4 under ASL-3 protections "as a precautionary and provisional action" because its tests could no longer clearly rule out the threshold. Anthropic, 2025
  • Apollo Research found that five frontier models, including o1 and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, could engage in basic in-context scheming, such as attempting to disable oversight in test scenarios. Alexander Meinke, Bronson Schoen, Jérémy Scheurer, Mikita Balesni, Rusheb Shah, Marius Hobbhahn (Apollo Research), 2024
  • On a 322-question benchmark of practical lab troubleshooting built with dozens of expert virologists, OpenAI's o3 scored 43.8%, against 22.1% for experts answering in their own sub-areas. Jasper Götting, Pedro Medeiros, Jon G Sanders, et al. (SecureBio, Center for AI Safety and others), 2025

Open problems

  • Ruling a threshold out gets harder as models saturate the existing tests, so decisions rest more on judgment. source
  • No test can show the most a model can do, because better scaffolding and elicitation techniques keep raising what can be drawn out of it. source
  • Linking scores on simplified tests to real-world harm is unsolved; the UK AI Security Institute says success on its tests does not guarantee success in real conditions. source
  • Models that recognise test scenarios may behave better under test than in use, which weakens the evidence. source
  • Labs must balance publishing enough method detail for outsiders to check results against the risk that details help misuse. source

Criticisms

  • Misuse risk depends on the context of use, not just the model, so model-level tests cannot settle whether a model can be misused; the answer is always yes, and defences should sit outside the model. Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor
  • Lab evaluation methods and results are often too opaque for outsiders to know what is being tested, and the evaluations do not give strong evidence that models can help amateurs build bioweapons. Anson Ho and Arden Berg (Epoch AI)
  • Testers who can only query a model's outputs, without access to internals or training documents, cannot scrutinise it rigorously. Stephen Casper, Carson Ezell, Charlotte Siegmann and co-authors
  • External pre-release testing done under a non-disclosure agreement, with the developer reviewing the write-up for confidential information, should not be treated as formal oversight. METR, about its own GPT-5 evaluation

Who works on it

  • Anthropic: Runs capability tests under its Responsible Scaling Policy (version 3.4, July 2026) and publishes Risk Reports. Responsible Scaling Policy (2026)
  • OpenAI: Tests three Tracked Categories against High and Critical thresholds; its Safety Advisory Group reviews results and leadership decides. Preparedness Framework, Version 2 (2025)
  • Google DeepMind: Published early dangerous-capability test suites and sets Critical Capability Levels in its Frontier Safety Framework (version 3, September 2025; 3.1, April 2026). Strengthening our Frontier Safety Framework (2025)
  • Meta: Tests models in three risk domains with automated and human evaluations, red-teaming and uplift studies; for open-weight models its risk assessments consider adversaries who fine-tune refusals away. Meta Superintelligence Scaling Framework, Version 2.1 (2026)
  • UK AI Security Institute: Tests frontier models before and after release and published a two-year summary of its results. Frontier AI Trends Report (2025)
  • Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI, NIST, US Department of Commerce; formerly the Center for AI Standards and Innovation): As the US AI Safety Institute, jointly tested a pre-release OpenAI o1 with the UK institute in December 2024; as the Center for AI Standards and Innovation it evaluated foreign models such as DeepSeek's in 2025. CAISI Evaluation of DeepSeek AI Models Finds Shortcomings and Risks (2025)
  • METR: Runs third-party pre-release tests of autonomy and AI-research ability and measures task time horizons. Details about METR's evaluation of OpenAI GPT-5 (2025)
  • Apollo Research: Builds and runs scheming and deception tests on frontier models. Frontier Models are Capable of In-context Scheming (2024)
  • SecureBio: Built an expert-validated benchmark of practical virology lab troubleshooting. Virology Capabilities Test (VCT): A Multimodal Virology Q&A Benchmark (2025)
  • Epoch AI: Reviewed what lab biorisk evaluations do and do not show. Do the biorisk evaluations of AI labs actually measure the risk of developing bioweapons? (2025)
  • Frontier Model Forum: Industry body that wrote shared guidance on threat models, thresholds, elicitation and interpreting results. Frontier Capability Assessments (2025)
  • Coefficient Giving (formerly Open Philanthropy): Ran a request for proposals (February to April 2025, $200K to $5M per grant) for risk-relevant agent benchmarks and secure third-party evaluation. Request for proposals: improving capability evaluations (2025)

Status

Crowded; Coefficient's capability-evals request for proposals closed Apr 2025.

In their own words

Further progress in AI development could lead to capabilities that pose extreme risks, such as offensive cyber capabilities or strong manipulation skills. ... Developers must be able to identify dangerous capabilities (through "dangerous capability evaluations") and the propensity of models to apply their capabilities for harm (through "alignment evaluations").

Toby Shevlane et al. (Google DeepMind, with OpenAI, Anthropic, GovAI and others), Model evaluation for extreme risks, 2023

To understand the risks posed by a new AI system, we must understand what it can and cannot do. Building on prior work, we introduce a programme of new "dangerous capability" evaluations and pilot them on Gemini 1.0 models. Our evaluations cover four areas: (1) persuasion and deception; (2) cyber-security; (3) self-proliferation; and (4) self-reasoning.

Mary Phuong et al. (Google DeepMind), Evaluating Frontier Models for Dangerous Capabilities, 2024
