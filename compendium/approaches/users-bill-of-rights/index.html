Users' bill of rights in model constitutions

Work out a bill of rights for a model's users, such as notice, explanations and ways to contest harmful outputs. Write it into the model's governing document, backed by independent oversight.

Open in the compendium

This proposal says a model's governing document, such as Claude's constitution, should promise users concrete rights and set up independent oversight, not just tell the model how to behave. Its main statement is a February 2026 essay by Suzanne Nossel, a member of Meta's Oversight Board, writing for herself rather than for the full Board. It matters for safety and trust because a model that means well can still harm people, and today users have few ways to learn why, object, or get a remedy.

How it works

Nossel's starting point is that Claude's constitution is written as directions to the model, not as assurances to users. She proposes two additions. The first is a bill of rights: clear, accessible rules; notice and an explanation when a decision materially affects a user; and ways to contest harmful outputs, unexplained refusals or patterns of error. For example, when Claude refuses a request, it should be able to explain the basis for the refusal and consider whether its answer was the least restrictive option available.

The second is an independent oversight body. She points to the expert reviews the Oversight Board does for Meta's content decisions as a model: an outside body that issues reasoned, public decisions, gives civil society a channel to be heard, and benchmarks the company against international human rights law.

Where research stands

This is at the commentary stage: one essay, not yet a research programme or a company policy. It draws on older precedents. In October 2022 the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy published a non-binding 'Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights' with five principles, including 'Notice and Explanation' and 'Human Alternatives, Consideration, and Fallback'. The EU AI Act of 2024 (Article 86) gives people a legal right to an explanation when a deployer's decision based on certain high-risk AI systems listed in the Act has legal effects on them, or similarly significant effects that they consider harmful.

The proposal fits a wider critique of corporate model documents: that they borrow the language of public constitutions without the accountability that public constitutions carry. The main doubt is about the oversight half. The Oversight Board model it borrows from has itself been criticised for taking few cases, moving slowly and having limited impact.

Key results

  • Claude's constitution frames safety as directions to the model rather than assurances to users, and lacks appeals, remedies or external review; users need a bill of rights and an independent oversight mechanism. Suzanne Nossel, 2026
  • The U.S. government's 2022 Blueprint set out five non-binding principles for automated systems, including notice and explanation and access to a human alternative. White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, 2022
  • The EU AI Act gives affected people a right to clear and meaningful explanations of an AI system's role in decisions made with certain high-risk systems. European Union, 2024
  • Binding obligations for developers and users of Claude sit in commercial contracts and policies, while the constitution itself is drafted, revised and interpreted by Anthropic alone. Lisa Klaassen, Ralph Schroeder, 2026

Open problems

  • Designing pathways for users to contest harmful outputs and unexplained refusals, and to get remedies, which Claude's constitution does not provide. source
  • Making an oversight body independent of the company that funds it, a problem the Oversight Board has faced with Meta. source
  • Giving users real explanations of refusals; Nossel suggests that an equivalent of 'chain of thought' functionality could help. source

Criticisms

  • The model the proposal borrows from has under-delivered: after five years the Oversight Board has taken few cases, decided slowly and had limited impact, and depends on Meta's money. Casey Newton (Platformer, December 2025)
  • The proposal's own author concedes that the Oversight Board is imperfect, its powers are sharply limited, and it is not a court. Suzanne Nossel

Who works on it
