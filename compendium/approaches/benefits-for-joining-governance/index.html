Benefit-sharing for joining governance (Atoms for Peace model)

Offer states access to AI benefits in return for joining safety governance, as Atoms for Peace did for nuclear technology.

Open in the compendium

Under this proposal, countries would get a share of advanced AI's benefits, such as access to models, computing power or funding, in return for joining a safety regime and accepting its limits. The model is 'Atoms for Peace', the 1953 US offer to share civilian nuclear technology in exchange for safeguards. Countries that gain little from a regime have little reason to accept its restrictions, and benefit-sharing could make joining worthwhile.

How it works

In Cullen O'Keefe's 'Chips for Peace' (July 2024), member states make three linked commitments. They regulate frontier AI at home to prevent catastrophic risks. They contribute to benefit-sharing, with the states that gain most paying into programmes that help others, especially in the Global South. They also cooperate to keep non-members from building or obtaining high-risk frontier AI. Benefit-sharing pays states back for the costs of regulation and nonproliferation, and nonproliferation stops outsiders from undercutting the deal. Benefits could include subsidised access to models, help adapting models to local needs, dedicated computing capacity, local capacity-building or cash.

Ho and colleagues (July 2023) propose a different version, a 'Frontier AI Collaborative' that would spread access to cutting-edge AI, and suggest such access could be an incentive to join a governance regime. In Dario Amodei's 'entente strategy' (October 2024), a coalition of democracies offers AI's benefits to countries that support it while denying key inputs to rivals. The UN High-level Advisory Body (September 2024) recommended a global fund for AI to give countries without enough local capacity access to shared computing and testing tools, though not as a condition of joining a safety regime.

Where research stands

The bargain exists in papers and proposals. As of October 2026, no AI regime offers benefits in exchange for safety commitments. A February 2025 GovAI report sorts benefit-sharing into three forms (sharing resources such as compute or data, widening access to AI systems, and transferring part of AI's financial gains) and three motives (inclusive growth, technological self-determination and geopolitical goals). It warns that some forms could raise security risks and create dependence on frontier AI states.

Prasad and Hatz (April 2025) credit the original Atoms for Peace with helping create the IAEA and shape nuclear cooperation norms, but note that in some cases it sped up proliferation and entrenched Cold War divisions. A 2019 RAND commentary notes that India and Pakistan used technology gained through the programme in their early weapons work. Prasad and Hatz argue the model is hard to copy for AI because AI has no clear 'weapons-grade' equivalent to anchor the bargain, and verifying that AI is safe is currently very difficult.

Key results

  • Chips for Peace links safety regulation, benefit-sharing and nonproliferation so that each commitment supports the others; benefit-sharing must be empowering to recipients yet robust to their defection. Cullen O'Keefe (Institute for Law & AI), 2024
  • A Frontier AI Collaborative could spread access to cutting-edge AI, but it may be difficult to do this without spreading dangerous AI capabilities around the world. Lewis Ho, Joslyn Barnhart, Robert Trager, Yoshua Bengio, Miles Brundage and others, 2023
  • International AI benefit-sharing can take three forms, resources, access or financial transfers, and some forms carry security risks, so low-risk forms could start now while broader deals are negotiated. Claire Dennis, Sam Manning, Stephen Clare, Boxi Wu, Jake Okechukwu Effoduh, Chinasa T. Okolo, Lennart Heim, Katya Klinova (GovAI), 2025
  • Direct transfer of the Atoms for Peace model to AI is hard because AI has no clear weapons-grade equivalent and AI safety is hard to verify; the authors suggest if-then commitments tied to capability evaluations instead. Amritanshu Prasad, Sophia Hatz, 2025
  • The UN advisory body recommended a global fund for AI, managed independently, to give countries without local capacity access to shared compute and testing tools. UN High-level Advisory Body on Artificial Intelligence, 2024

Criticisms

  • Atoms for Peace in some cases accelerated proliferation and entrenched Cold War divisions, and restricting chips could repeat those dynamics and risk an arms race. Amritanshu Prasad and Sophia Hatz (2025)
  • Powerful states may pursue benefit-sharing mainly for their own economic and security interests, and some arrangements could create dependencies that frontier AI states can exploit. Claire Dennis and colleagues (GovAI, 2025)
  • AI code is far more diffuse and widely available than nuclear material, which limits how far nuclear-style bargains can carry over. Patrick S. Roberts (RAND), War on the Rocks, December 2019

Who works on it

  • Institute for Law & AI: Its director of research proposed Chips for Peace, a safety, benefit-sharing and nonproliferation package. Chips for Peace (2024)
  • Centre for the Governance of AI: Mapped the forms, motives and risks of international AI benefit-sharing. Options and Motivations for International AI Benefit Sharing (2025)
  • UN High-level Advisory Body on Artificial Intelligence: Recommended a global fund for AI and discussed the IAEA 'grand bargain' as a model. Governing AI for Humanity: Final Report (2024)
  • Anthropic: Its chief executive proposed an entente in which democracies offer AI benefits to supporting countries. Machines of Loving Grace (2024)

Status

Discussed in papers and essays. Ho et al. propose a body to get cutting-edge AI to underserved groups, and suggest access to it could be an incentive to join a governance regime. Prasad and Hatz say direct transfer of the Atoms for Peace model is hard, because AI has no clear weapons-grade equivalent and safety is hard to verify.

In their own words

Chips for Peace is a package of three commitments-safety regulation, benefit-sharing, and nonproliferation-which complement and strengthen each other. For example, benefit-sharing compensates states for the costs associated with safety regulation and nonproliferation, while nonproliferation prevents nonmembers from undermining the regulation and benefit-sharing commitments.

Cullen O'Keefe (Institute for Law & AI; Lawfare), Chips for Peace: How the U.S. and Its Allies Can Lead on Safe and Beneficial AI, 2024

To legitimize and drive broad adoption of Chips for Peace as a whole ... members would also commit to benefit-sharing. States that stand to benefit the most from frontier AI development and deployment by default would be obligated to contribute to programs that ensure benefits from frontier AI are broadly distributed, especially to member states in the Global South.

Cullen O'Keefe (Institute for Law & AI; Lawfare), Chips for Peace: How the U.S. and Its Allies Can Lead on Safe and Beneficial AI, 2024
