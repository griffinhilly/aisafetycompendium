Holding AI companies to account

ConstrainingAlso: Societal resilience

Public data trust

Set up a public body that controls training data and licenses it to AI developers in return for a share of revenue.

Open in the compendium

A public data trust would be an independent public body that collects the internet's text and images as a shared resource and controls who may train AI on them. Commercial developers would license the data in return for a share of their revenue, and the trust could attach conditions such as audits. The idea matters for safety because control of training data is a lever over how frontier models are built, and it puts that lever in public hands rather than with a few firms.

How it works

Alan Chan, Herbie Bradley and Nitarshan Rajkumar set out the idea in 2023. The trust would scrape the web itself, as a 'digital commons', and curate the result, removing errors, poisoned data and personal information. It would also try to gather data that cannot simply be scraped, such as human feedback data. A developer that wanted to train a commercial model would sign a licence. The licence could require a percentage of the model's revenue, rate-limited access to curb mass low-quality content, and regular internal and external audits.

The revenue would go to people whose work makes up the commons or who lose work to automation. The authors give the example of a fund for artists, paid from the revenue of an image model trained on artists' work, much as Canada's Copyright Board levies cassette and CD sales for artists. To make the deal stick, developers would need to prove they trained only on the trust's data. The paper sketches technical checks for this, such as 'proof of learning' records of each training step, and admits they have gaps.

Where research stands

This is a proposal in one paper, accepted at the AIES conference in 2023. No public body of this kind exists as of October 2026, and no published follow-up or critique of the paper was found. Nearby work exists. In 2018-19 the Open Data Institute ran three data trust pilots with the government's Office for Artificial Intelligence and Innovate UK. The UK's January 2025 AI Opportunities Action Plan put a National Data Library at the centre of its plans to open up public and private data for AI. EleutherAI's Common Pile (June 2025) showed that an 8-terabyte corpus of only openly licensed and public-domain text can train 7-billion-parameter models that compete with similar models trained on unlicensed data.

Two trends cut both ways. Websites are closing to AI crawlers fast: the Data Provenance Initiative found that 2023-24 restrictions fully blocked 5% or more of the C4 dataset, and 28% or more of its most important sources. That raises the value of a curated, licensed commons. But US courts have weakened the legal leverage a trust would need. In June 2025 a federal judge in Bartz v. Anthropic ruled that training on lawfully bought books was fair use, while pirated copies were not; the $1.5 billion settlement was approved in July 2026 and sets no precedent. The authors also note that if AI-generated synthetic data replaces human data, the trust loses bargaining power.

Key results

  • The authors propose a public trust that scrapes the internet as a digital commons and licenses it to commercial developers for a percentage of deployment revenue, backed by verification that developers used only the trust's data. Alan Chan, Herbie Bradley, Nitarshan Rajkumar, 2023
  • An audit of 14,000 web domains found that in one year about 5% or more of all C4 tokens, and 28% or more of its most critical sources, became fully restricted for AI use. Shayne Longpre, Robert Mahari, Ariel Lee et al., 2024
  • Models trained only on an 8 TB corpus of public-domain and openly licensed text performed competitively with Llama 1 and 2 7B models trained on unlicensed text at similar compute. Nikhil Kandpal, Brian Lester, Colin Raffel et al., 2025
  • In Bartz v. Anthropic, the court found training on lawfully purchased books to be fair use but downloads from shadow libraries not; the resulting $1.5 billion settlement, about $3,000 per book, received final approval on 20 July 2026. The Next Web, 2026

Open problems

  • Verifying that a developer trained only on the trust's data, and that the model it deploys is the one it trained. source
  • Keeping the trust independent of both the companies that want its data and governments that might misuse it. source
  • Holding bargaining power if courts treat training as fair use or synthetic data replaces human data. source
  • Extending a national trust (the authors suggest the US or UK) to an international setting, which the paper leaves open. source

Criticisms

  • Training on copyrighted works should be presumptively fair use, because licensing millions of works from thousands of owners is impractical and denying fair use would block the use rather than pay owners. Mark A. Lemley and Bryan Casey, Texas Law Review
  • The proponents themselves warn of corporate capture through lobbying or corrupting board members, of government capture, and of the trust losing leverage as model-generated data improves. Alan Chan, Herbie Bradley, Nitarshan Rajkumar

Who works on it

  • EleutherAI: Non-profit research lab; a co-author of the data-trust paper worked there, and it built the Common Pile, an openly licensed training corpus. The Common Pile v0.1 (2025)
  • Data Provenance Initiative: Research group that audits the licensing and consent status of AI training data. Consent in Crisis: The Rapid Decline of the AI Data Commons (2024)
  • Open Data Institute: Ran three data trust pilots with the Office for Artificial Intelligence and Innovate UK (2018-19) and has published a white paper on an AI-ready National Data Library. How an AI-ready National Data Library would help UK science (2025)

In their own words

Democratization of AI means not only that people can freely use AI, but also that people can collectively decide how AI is to be used. In particular, collective decision-making power is required to redress the negative externalities from the development of increasingly advanced AI systems, including degradation of the digital commons and unemployment from automation.

Alan Chan, Herbie Bradley, Nitarshan Rajkumar, Reclaiming the Digital Commons: A Public Data Trust for Training Data, 2023

In this work, we propose that a public data trust assert control over training data for foundation models. In particular, this trust should scrape the internet as a digital commons, to license to commercial model developers for a percentage cut of revenues from deployment.

Alan Chan, Herbie Bradley, Nitarshan Rajkumar, Reclaiming the Digital Commons: A Public Data Trust for Training Data, 2023
