AI and people's own judgement

Societal resilience

Agent-based modelling of agency loss

Simulate disempowerment as accumulated small surrenders rather than a takeover event.

Open in the compendium

This approach uses computer simulations of many interacting individuals (agent-based models) to study how a population could lose agency through many small, sensible choices to rely on AI. Instead of a single takeover event, the models look for tipping points where reliance becomes self-reinforcing. They matter because gradual disempowerment is hard to observe until late, and simulation is one of the few ways to explore it in advance.

How it works

An agent-based model gives each simulated person simple rules and lets the population evolve. A typical design: each agent has a skill level and decides each round whether to do a task itself or hand it to an AI. Handing it over is individually rational because the AI is faster, but unused skills decay, so next round the agent is even more likely to delegate. Researchers then add social effects (people copy their neighbours) and tunable AI properties (how good the AI is, how persuasive) and run thousands of simulations to see when the population tips into dependence.

A Fall 2026 SPAR project (SPAR, "Research program for AI risks", is a part-time, remote fellowship that pairs new researchers with mentors) shows the design in detail. Its agents differ in education, age and other traits. Three mechanisms interact: an "atrophy ratchet" (reliance lowers confidence and skill, which raises reliance), threshold-based susceptibility to AI-mediated manipulation, and social reinforcement. AI capability is a dial, so how capable AI must be before tipping points appear is an output of the model, not an assumption.

Where research stands

This is a very early, scattered area: a few individual papers and mentored student projects, not a named research cluster. The founding gradual-disempowerment paper is conceptual, and Raymond Douglas's 2025 list of follow-on projects includes "Simulating entire civilizations" as an open technical direction. Two models posted in 2026 are preprints that have not been peer-reviewed. One models capability and delegation as a dynamical system with an agent-based version, and reports an abrupt collapse in human capability past a critical threshold. Another uses a NetLogo simulation (a common agent-based modelling tool) to separate AI use that amplifies human thinking from use that creates dependence.

Related work comes at the problem from other angles. RAND's April 2026 model is mathematical rather than a simulation; the SPAR proposal builds on it to add behavioural dynamics. A Spring 2026 SPAR project studies the Meiji Restoration, which gradually displaced Japan's samurai class, as a historical case of gradual displacement. Agent-based models in general are hard to validate against real data, so findings here are qualitative: whether tipping points exist and what shifts them, not forecasts.

Key results

  • In a preprint that has not been peer-reviewed, a two-variable model of human capability and AI delegation, with an agent-based version of 100 agents, predicts that capability collapses abruptly past a critical threshold, and that periodic AI failures or 20% mandatory practice preserve much more capability. Jeongju Park, Musu Kim, Sekyung Han, 2026
  • In a preprint (under review), a NetLogo agent-based simulation found that no baseline setting produced a collaborative gain over the best standalone performer, even with zero skill atrophy; gains appeared only when retained human capability fed directly into the joint output. Eduardo Di Santi, Carla Florida, 2026
  • A project proposal specifies an agent-based model with an atrophy ratchet, manipulation thresholds and social reinforcement, to locate tipping points and test interventions such as education, friction in delegation and exposure limits. Zhamilia Klycheva (mentor), 2026

Open problems

  • Find whether population-level tipping points into self-reinforcing agency loss exist, and how capable AI must be before they appear. source
  • Scale simulation from individuals to whole economies and states, as proposed in the gradual-disempowerment research agenda. source
  • Add degraded participation (such as cognitive atrophy) and time dynamics to formal models of collective agency. source
  • Calibrate models to real data; current ones are stylised testbeds, not realistic models of human cognition. source

Criticisms

  • Agent-based models face basic validation problems: results can be fragile to modelling choices, models are hard to compare, and many different models can fit the same data. Paul Windrum, Giorgio Fagiolo, Alessio Moneta
  • If gradual disempowerment is mainly a lens on misalignment or human power concentration, simulations of reliance alone may model the wrong mechanism. Tom Davidson

In their own words

This paper examines the systemic risks posed by incremental advancements in artificial intelligence, developing the concept of 'gradual disempowerment', in contrast to the abrupt takeover scenarios commonly discussed in AI safety. We analyze how even incremental improvements in AI capabilities can undermine human influence over large-scale systems that society depends on, including the economy, culture, and nation-states.

Jan Kulveit, Raymond Douglas, Nora Ammann, Deger Turan, David Krueger, David Duvenaud, Gradual Disempowerment: Systemic Existential Risks from Incremental AI Development, 2025

This project builds an agent-based model (ABM) of that process. Heterogeneous agents - differing in education, age, and other social and political attributes that shape susceptibility - face repeated, individually rational choices to rely on AI-mediated information and delegation.

Zhamilia Klycheva (SPAR mentor), Loss of Human Agency in the Age of Advanced AI - An Agent-Based Model (SPAR project proposal, Fall 2026), 2026
