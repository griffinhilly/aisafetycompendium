Holding AI companies to account

Outside pressure on AI developers

Use employees, public campaigns and organised civil society to push labs toward responsible behaviour.

Open in the compendium

Outside pressure means using people who are not regulators, such as employees, former staff, watchdog groups, campaigners and the public, to push AI companies toward safer behaviour. The tools include open letters, public scorecards, reports that document broken promises, protests and complaints to officials. It matters because laws are thin and slow, so public scrutiny is often the main cost a company pays for cutting corners.

How it works

Employee voice is one route. In June 2024, 13 current and former staff of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic published 'A Right to Warn about Advanced Artificial Intelligence', asking companies not to enforce agreements that bar risk-related criticism, to create anonymous channels to the board and regulators, and not to retaliate against staff who go public after internal routes fail. The month before, after Vox reported that OpenAI's exit agreements threatened departing staff's vested equity if they criticised the company, OpenAI told current and former employees it would not cancel vested units.

Watchdog groups are a second route. The Midas Project tracks quiet changes to companies' safety policies and checks whether the 16 original signatories of the 2024 Seoul safety commitments followed through. The AI Whistleblower Initiative, backed by a coalition of more than 30 organisations and experts, asks companies to publish their internal whistleblowing policies. A campaign called 'Not For Private Gain', whose 2026 review was written by Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology and Encode AI, opposed OpenAI's move from nonprofit to for-profit control; on 28 October 2025 the Delaware Attorney General approved it on conditions, including that a nonprofit safety committee can halt model releases.

Public campaigns are a third route. Jamie Harris argued in 2023 that animal-welfare groups won about 3,000 corporate policies in ten years through sustained pressure on named companies, and that AI safety lacks groups running such campaigns. PauseAI and Evitable now try to organise public opposition to the race to build superintelligence.

Where research stands

Outside pressure is growing but scattered, and its effects are hard to measure. There are clear cases where it changed company behaviour, such as OpenAI's reversal on exit agreements in May 2024 and the conditions attached to its restructuring in October 2025. But most watchdog outputs are reports and scorecards whose influence on decisions is not tested, and the Midas Project's own pages describe trackers rather than outcomes. A September 2026 review by the 'Not For Private Gain' coalition found OpenAI compliant with some restructuring conditions and possibly not with others, showing how much follow-up enforcement depends on outside monitoring.

There is little rigorous evidence on which tactics work for AI. Lessons are mostly borrowed from animal-welfare and other corporate campaigns. Open questions include whether protests polarise the issue or spend political capital needed for inside influence, and how to protect insiders who speak up.

Key results

  • Thirteen current and former frontier-lab employees set out four commitments companies should make so staff can raise risk concerns, endorsed by Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton and Stuart Russell. Current and former employees of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic, 2024
  • After press reports on exit contracts, OpenAI told current and former staff it 'has not canceled, and will not cancel, any vested units' over non-disparagement terms. Richard Speed (The Register), 2024
  • Animal advocates won about 3,000 corporate welfare policies in ten years, with about 88% of due cage-free pledges fulfilled, suggesting sustained company-specific campaigns could work for AI. Jamie Harris, 2023
  • Delaware's Attorney General approved OpenAI's recapitalization only with conditions, including a nonprofit Safety and Security Committee able to halt model releases. Delaware Department of Justice, 2025
  • Of six major AI companies rated, only one had published its internal whistleblowing policy, and none disclosed evidence of how well its system works. AI Whistleblower Initiative, 2025

Open problems

  • Measuring which pressure tactics actually change company decisions, as opposed to producing reports. source
  • Whether protests and confrontational campaigns polarise AI safety or spend political capital needed for inside influence. source
  • Monitoring compliance with commitments that only outside groups are watching, such as conditions on OpenAI's restructuring. source
  • Giving insiders safe, verifiably anonymous channels to raise risks without retaliation. source

Criticisms

  • Pause advocacy and protests could turn AI into a partisan issue and hurt the reputation of AI safety as a research field. Joseph Miller (in a post about the 2023 international PauseAI protest)
  • AI safety asks are less clear and more technical than animal-welfare asks, which makes corporate campaigns harder to design and judge. Jamie Harris (caveats to his own argument)
  • The deeper problem is the concentrated power of AI companies, so public pushback should be framed as a contest over power; AI Now's roadmap is titled "Make AI a Fight About Power, Not Progress". AI Now Institute (Kate Brennan, Amba Kak, Sarah Myers West)

Who works on it

  • The Midas Project: Watchdog that tracks changes to company safety policies, Seoul-commitment compliance and OpenAI's governance. Projects (Watchtower, Seoul Tracker, The OpenAI Files) (2026)
  • AI Whistleblower Initiative: Runs a coalition campaign asking AI companies to publish their whistleblowing policies. Publish Your Whistleblowing Policies (2025)
  • Legal Advocates for Safe Science and Technology: Co-authored the 'Not For Private Gain' review of OpenAI's compliance with its restructuring conditions. Not For Private Gain: September 2026 update (2026)
  • Encode AI: Co-authored the 'Not For Private Gain' compliance review and joined the whistleblowing-policy coalition. Not For Private Gain: September 2026 update (2026)
  • Evitable: New nonprofit founded by David Krueger to inform and organise the public against the race to build superintelligence. About us
  • PauseAI: Movement calling for an international pause on the most powerful general AI systems, with local groups and lobbying. PauseAI
  • AI Now Institute: Offers strategies for organisers, policymakers and the public to push back against concentrated AI industry power. Artificial Power: 2025 Landscape Report (2025)

Status

Scattered advocacy; the 2024 letter had 13 signatories and asked for whistleblower protections.

In their own words

However, AI companies have strong financial incentives to avoid effective oversight, and we do not believe bespoke structures of corporate governance are sufficient to change this. ... So long as there is no effective government oversight of these corporations, current and former employees are among the few people who can hold them accountable to the public.

Current and former employees of OpenAI and Google DeepMind (open letter; signed by Jacob Hilton, Daniel Kokotajlo, Ramana Kumar, Neel Nanda, William Saunders, Carroll Wainwright, Daniel Ziegler and six anonymous signatories; endorsed by Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton, Stuart Russell), A Right to Warn about Advanced Artificial Intelligence, 2024

Negotiations and pressure campaigns have proven effective at driving corporate change across industries and movements. I expect that AI safety/governance can learn from this! ... Successful corporate reform campaigns in animal welfare and other movements suggest an important principle - the need for an "ecology" of different organisations, roles, and tactics.

Jamie Harris (EA Forum), Corporate campaigns work: a key learning for AI Safety, 2023
