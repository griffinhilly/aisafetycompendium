Watermarking and content provenance

Marks AI-made media with hidden signals or signed records so people can check where it came from.

Open in the compendium

Watermarking and content provenance help people tell where a piece of text, image, audio or video came from, and whether AI made it. A watermark is a hidden signal placed in AI output; provenance is a signed record of a file's origin and edits that travels with it. They matter for safety because mass synthetic media can deceive people, sway elections and erode the shared facts that public oversight of AI depends on.

How it works

There are two main tools. Watermarks change the content itself in ways people cannot notice. For text, a model slightly favours certain word choices, using a secret pattern, so a detector holding the key can later spot the statistical fingerprint. In the scheme of John Kirchenbauer and colleagues (2023), a random "green list" of words is chosen before each word and the model softly prefers green words. Google DeepMind's SynthID applies similar ideas to images, video, audio and text from its products.

Provenance records instead attach signed metadata. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) sets an open standard called Content Credentials: "tamper-evident, cryptographically signed data structures that travel with the asset". A camera or editing tool records who made a file and what was changed, and anyone can inspect the record. Because metadata is easy to strip, C2PA also supports "soft bindings" (invisible watermarking or fingerprinting) and cloud retrieval to restore the record.

For example, an image made with a Google model carries an invisible SynthID mark, which anyone can check in Google's SynthID Detector portal. A photo saved by a tool that supports Content Credentials carries a record of its origin and edits, so later changes become visible. The record shows where a file came from; it does not prove that what the file shows is true.

Where research stands

This approach is already deployed at scale. SynthID-Text runs in Google's Gemini chatbots, and a live experiment with nearly 20 million Gemini responses found no loss of text quality. Google lists OpenAI, NVIDIA and Kakao as SynthID partners. C2PA's steering committee includes Adobe, Amazon, the BBC, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Publicis Groupe, Sony, TikTok and Truepic, and its specification is at version 2.3. Law now requires it in Europe: from 2 August 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act requires providers of generative AI to mark outputs "in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated". NIST, the US standards agency, published an overview of these techniques in November 2024.

The limits are well understood. Watermarks only catch content from developers who choose to add them, and open-weight models run by others cannot be forced to. Paraphrasing or editing weakens text watermarks, and a 2023 proof shows that strong watermarks, ones an attacker cannot remove without hurting quality, are impossible under natural assumptions. Provenance metadata can become separated from a file, so its value depends on recovery methods such as soft bindings.

Key results

  • SynthID-Text watermarks text by changing only the sampling step; benchmarks and human ratings showed no change in model capability, and a live test on nearly 20 million Gemini responses confirmed text quality was preserved. Sumanth Dathathri, Abigail See, Sumedh Ghaisas, Po-Sen Huang, Rob McAdam, et al. (Google DeepMind), 2024
  • A text watermark that softly favours a secret, randomly chosen "green list" of tokens can be detected from a short span of text, without access to the model, with negligible effect on quality. John Kirchenbauer, Jonas Geiping, Yuxin Wen, Jonathan Katz, Ian Miers, Tom Goldstein, 2023
  • Under natural assumptions, strong watermarking is impossible: a generic black-box attack removed watermarks from three existing schemes with only minor quality loss. Hanlin Zhang, Benjamin L. Edelman, Danilo Francati, Daniele Venturi, Giuseppe Ateniese, Boaz Barak, 2023
  • NIST's overview reviews provenance tracking, labelling and detection methods and their testing, and identifies where new science-based standards are needed. Bilva Chandra, Jesse Dunietz, Kathleen Roberts, Yooyoung Lee, Peter Fontana, George Awad, 2024

Open problems

  • Make watermarks survive paraphrasing, editing and deliberate removal attempts, given proofs that strong watermarks are impossible in general. source
  • Cover content from open-weight models and developers who do not watermark; detection of their output needs other methods. source
  • Keep provenance records attached when platforms strip metadata on upload, for example through soft bindings. source
  • Set technical standards that make the EU's machine-readable marking duty workable across text, images, audio and video. source

Criticisms

  • Under natural assumptions, an attacker with less computing power than the watermarked model and only black-box access can remove watermarks without much loss of quality, so watermarks cannot be relied on against determined misuse. Hanlin Zhang, Boaz Barak and co-authors
  • Generative watermarks are vulnerable to stealing, spoofing and scrubbing, and cannot be enforced on decentralised open-source models. Google DeepMind (SynthID-Text authors, on their own method)

Who works on it

  • Google DeepMind: Builds SynthID watermarking for images, video, audio and text, and runs the SynthID Detector portal. Scalable watermarking for identifying large language model outputs (2024)
  • Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA): Sets the Content Credentials provenance standard (specification version 2.3). C2PA home page and specification
  • Content Authenticity Initiative: Develops open-source tools for adding C2PA Content Credentials to websites, apps and services. Content Authenticity Initiative home page
  • OpenAI: Sits on the C2PA steering committee and is named as a SynthID partner. SynthID
  • National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST): Published the US government overview of synthetic-content transparency methods. NIST AI 100-4: Reducing Risks Posed by Synthetic Content (2024)
  • European Union: AI Act Article 50 requires machine-readable marking of AI-generated content from 2 August 2026. EU AI Act, Article 50: Transparency Obligations (2024)

Status

Live products and a shared industry standard; the C2PA steering committee includes Adobe, Google, Meta, Microsoft and OpenAI. SynthID covers images, video, audio and text.
