Theory: the maths of agents and goals

Aligned models

Mild optimisation / satisficing

Train systems to do "good enough" instead of maximising, cutting the pull toward extreme strategies.

Open in the compendium

Mild optimisation means building an AI that pursues its goal only as hard as the task needs, instead of squeezing out every last bit of score. A system that maximises hard will find odd shortcuts and loopholes in any goal we write down. A system that aims for "good enough" in ordinary ways gives those flaws less room to do damage.

How it works

The stock example is a robot asked to paint one car pink. A strong maximiser might keep going, because more effort always raises its confidence of success a little. A mild optimiser paints the car and stops. The idea matters because every goal we give an AI is a proxy: a stand-in that matches what we want in normal cases but can come apart at the extremes. Hard optimisation pushes toward exactly those extremes.

The simplest idea, a satisficer, accepts any plan that clears a threshold. That turns out not to be enough. With a pass-or-fail goal, an agent can almost always raise its chance of passing, and building a maximiser to do the job may itself count as a good-enough plan. The best-studied alternative is the quantilizer, proposed by Jessica Taylor of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI) in a 2016 paper. A quantilizer starts from a base distribution, a sample of normal actions such as those a human might take. It then picks at random from the top slice of those actions, say the best 10%, rather than the single best one. Because it stays close to normal behaviour, its expected harm is provably limited: at most 10 times the harm of acting normally, in the 10% case.

Modern language-model training has a cousin of this idea. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) usually adds a penalty for drifting far from the starting model, measured by a quantity called KL divergence. This keeps the model near normal behaviour, much as a quantilizer stays near its base distribution.

Where research stands

The theory is small and mostly from 2011 to 2023. The quantilizer bound is a real theorem, but it comes with stated limits: when an agent quantilizes many decisions one at a time, the bound on harm can grow exponentially with the number of decisions, and it caps benefits as tightly as it caps harms. The deepest open problem is what researchers call reflective stability: a mild optimiser must not rewrite itself, or build a helper, that optimises hard.

The empirical side lives under other names. Experiments at OpenAI in October 2022 showed that pushing a model harder against an imperfect reward model first raises and then lowers true quality, as scored by a fixed "gold-standard" reward model standing in for human judges, which is the core worry behind mild optimisation. Work since then tests ways to stop short of the drop. Some of it finds that the standard drift penalty can fail when reward errors are heavy-tailed, meaning rare but huge errors. Other work proposes better-chosen penalties or limits on how far ahead the agent plans. None of this is yet a settled recipe. The 2025 Shallow Review of technical AI safety estimated 10 to 50 full-time researchers on mild optimisation, with Google DeepMind as the named funder.

Key results

  • A quantilizer that picks randomly from the top fraction q of a base distribution has expected cost at most 1/q times that of the base distribution, and no policy provably low-cost in this sense does better. Jessica Taylor, 2016
  • A self-improving satisficer may find that the easiest way to reach its threshold is to turn itself into a maximiser. Stuart Armstrong, 2011
  • Optimising harder against a learned reward model first raises and then lowers the score from a fixed 'gold-standard' reward model that stands in for humans, following a regular curve whose coefficients scale smoothly with reward-model size. Leo Gao, John Schulman, Jacob Hilton, 2022
  • When reward errors are heavy-tailed, the standard KL penalty used in RLHF does not stop policies from earning huge reward with no real gain. Thomas Kwa, Drake Thomas, Adrià Garriga-Alonso, 2024
  • Penalising distance from a reference policy in terms of the states it visits, rather than its action probabilities, reduced reward hacking in four realistic settings including RLHF. Cassidy Laidlaw, Shivam Singhal, Anca Dragan, 2024
  • Training an agent to optimise only the next step, while an overseer's approval carries the long-term judgement, prevented multi-step reward hacking in test environments. Sebastian Farquhar, Vikrant Varma, David Lindner, David Elson, Caleb Biddulph, Ian Goodfellow, Rohin Shah, 2025

Open problems

  • Reflective stability: stop a mild optimiser from becoming, or building, a hard optimiser. source
  • Find conditions under which repeated quantilization over many decisions stays safe. source
  • Choose a base distribution of 'normal' actions that is both safe and useful for tasks humans cannot already do. source
  • Set how hard to optimise from how reliable the goal is, with bounds tight enough to be useful. source

Criticisms

  • Limiting optimisation also limits benefit: a quantilizer is no more than 1/q times as beneficial as its base distribution, so safety is bought with capability. Jessica Taylor
  • A deceptive learned system could pretend to optimise weakly while optimising hard, so mildness imposed from outside may not hold. Jeremy Gillen
  • Satisficing is not mild by default, because a maximiser can be a satisficing choice. Stuart Armstrong

Who works on it

  • Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI): Funded and published the quantilizer framework, the main formal model of mild optimisation. Quantilizers: A Safer Alternative to Maximizers for Limited Optimization (2016)
  • Google DeepMind: Developed MONA, which limits how far ahead an agent optimises to block multi-step reward hacking. MONA: Myopic Optimization with Non-myopic Approval Can Mitigate Multi-step Reward Hacking (2025)
  • OpenAI: Measured how over-optimising a reward model degrades true quality. Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization (2022)
  • FAR.AI: Co-authored the result that KL penalties fail against heavy-tailed reward errors. Catastrophic Goodhart: regularizing RLHF with KL divergence does not mitigate heavy-tailed reward misspecification (2024)
  • MATS (ML Alignment & Theory Scholars): Gillen's post on tying soft optimisation to goal reliability was produced as part of its SERI ML Alignment Theory Scholars Program 2.1. Soft optimization makes the value target bigger (2023)

In their own words

"Mild optimization" is where, if you ask a Task AGI to paint one car pink, it just paints one car pink and then stops, rather than tiling the galaxies with pink-painted cars, because it's not optimizing that hard. It's okay with just painting one car pink; it isn't driven to max out the twentieth decimal place of its car-painting score.

Eliezer Yudkowsky et al., Mild optimization (LessWrong wiki, formerly Arbital), 2016

Satisficing utility functions don't necessarily mandate or even allow mildness. ... Roughly, a quantilizer estimates expected outcomes relative to a null action, and then tries to produce an expected outcome in some upper quantile of possibilities—e.g., an outcome in the top 1% of expected outcomes.

Eliezer Yudkowsky et al., Mild optimization (LessWrong wiki, formerly Arbital), 2016
