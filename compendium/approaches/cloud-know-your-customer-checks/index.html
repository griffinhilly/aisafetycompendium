Holding AI companies to account

Constraining

Know-your-customer checks by compute and cloud providers

Require cloud and compute providers to verify their customers and report large training runs, making providers checkpoints on frontier development.

Open in the compendium

Training a frontier AI model needs huge amounts of computing power, and most of it is rented from a few cloud companies. Know-your-customer (KYC) rules, borrowed from banking, would make these cloud providers verify who their customers are and keep records or report when a customer runs a very large training job. This turns cloud providers into checkpoints where governments can see, and if needed stop, risky frontier development.

How it works

A bank must check a new customer's identity and report suspicious transactions. A cloud KYC scheme works the same way. When a customer signs up, the provider verifies who they are. If the customer's workloads cross a compute threshold that signals frontier-scale training, the provider keeps records and reports the customer to the government. In a 2023 paper, Janet Egan and Lennart Heim proposed that the threshold be dynamic, reassessed as training gets more efficient, so that it catches frontier work without burdening ordinary users.

Providers can do much of this without reading customers' data or code. A 2024 whitepaper by Heim, Tim Fist, Egan and others argues that providers already hold non-confidential information that reveals two key facts about a workload: its type, such as large-scale training versus inference, and how much compute it has used. On that basis, providers could act as record keepers, verifiers of customer activity, and enforcers that suspend access to rule-breakers. Renting is easier to police than chip sales, because access can be cut off after the fact.

Where research stands

The US came close to adopting this and then backed away. In January 2024, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) proposed a rule requiring US cloud providers and their foreign resellers to run customer identification programmes for foreign customers. The rule also implemented an Executive Order 14110 requirement to report to the government when foreign customers trained certain large AI models. President Trump revoked EO 14110 on 20 January 2025, and the federal regulatory agenda records the whole proposal as withdrawn on 16 December 2025. Microsoft, which had itself proposed 'know your cloud, customers and content' (KY3C) in June 2023, argued in its April 2024 comments for targeted rules and against 'burdensome and ineffective' data collection.

The idea has partly moved into export-control law instead. On 12 January 2026, the US House passed the Remote Access Security Act, which would let export controls cover foreign adversaries' remote access to controlled AI chips through cloud services. The research base is mostly policy papers from compute-governance researchers. Known weaknesses are that compute thresholds go stale as training gets cheaper, that efficiency gains and distributed training weaken any compute-based check, and that leading AI firms which run their own data centres, or partner closely with a provider, are harder to oversee this way.

Key results

  • Egan and Heim propose that the US require compute providers to run KYC schemes with a dynamic compute threshold, record-keeping and reporting of high-risk entities, and international alignment. Janet Egan, Lennart Heim, 2023
  • Compute providers can serve as securers, record keepers, verifiers and enforcers, using non-confidential data that reveals a workload's type and the compute it has used. Lennart Heim, Tim Fist, Janet Egan, Sihao Huang, Stephen Zekany, Robert Trager, Michael A Osborne, Noa Zilberman, 2024
  • BIS proposed on 29 January 2024 that US cloud providers and their foreign resellers run customer identification programmes, partly to stop foreign malicious actors using US cloud services to train large AI models. US Bureau of Industry and Security, 2024
  • The federal Unified Agenda lists the cloud KYC proposal (RIN 0694-AJ35) as withdrawn on 16 December 2025. Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (reginfo.gov), 2025
  • The House passed the Remote Access Security Act on 12 January 2026, extending export controls to cloud-based access to controlled items such as AI chips. House Select Committee on the CCP, 2026

Open problems

  • Keeping a compute threshold meaningful as algorithms get more efficient and training gets cheaper. source
  • Balancing customer confidentiality and privacy against the government's need to see risky workloads. source
  • Coordinating internationally so customers cannot move to providers outside the scheme. source
  • Detecting training that uses distributed methods, which weaken compute-based checks. source

Criticisms

  • Broad KYC data collection on cloud customers is burdensome and ineffective; requirements should be targeted at data most likely to deter abuse. Microsoft, comments on the BIS proposed rule (April 2024)
  • A compute threshold is 'too fast moving of a target' as training costs fall; who the customer is matters more than how much compute they use, and such rules could entrench large incumbents. Kevin Xu, Interconnected (September 2023)
  • The proponents note that a KYC scheme will not address all AI risks. Janet Egan and Lennart Heim

Status

Proposed, then withdrawn. The January 2024 Commerce proposal would have made US cloud providers verify the identities of foreign customers and report, within 15 days, deals with foreign customers that could train a large AI model with possible malicious cyber uses. The AI reporting part came from Executive Order 14110, which was revoked on 20 January 2025. The federal regulatory agenda lists the whole proposal as withdrawn on 16 December 2025.

In their own words

To address security and safety risks stemming from highly capable artificial intelligence (AI) models, we propose that the US government should ensure compute providers implement Know-Your-Customer (KYC) schemes.

Janet Egan, Lennart Heim, Oversight for Frontier AI through a Know-Your-Customer Scheme for Compute Providers, 2023

Compute providers can play an essential role in a regulatory ecosystem via four key capacities: as securers, safeguarding AI systems and critical infrastructure; as record keepers, enhancing visibility for policymakers; as verifiers of customer activities, ensuring oversight; and as enforcers, taking actions against rule violations.

Lennart Heim, Tim Fist, Janet Egan et al., Governing Through the Cloud: The Intermediary Role of Compute Providers in AI Regulation, 2024
