Scalable oversight

Let humans, helped by AI, supervise outputs too complex or costly to check by hand.

Open in the compendium

Scalable oversight is the problem of supervising AI systems whose work is too complex, too long or too expert for people to check by hand. Its methods give human overseers AI help, such as critiques, debates or task breakdowns, so their judgements stay accurate as models get stronger. It matters because learning from feedback only works if the feedback is right.

How it works

The basic move is to let AI help the human judge. A critic model reads a piece of code and points out likely bugs; the human checks the bugs instead of reading every line. Other methods split a hard task into smaller pieces a person can judge (task decomposition), or pit two models against each other so that each exposes the other's errors (debate).

Researchers test these ideas with a stand-in setup. They pick tasks where experts can find the right answer but non-experts and the model alone cannot. Then they check whether non-experts, helped by the model, can reach expert-level accuracy. In a November 2022 Anthropic study on multiple-choice and reading questions, people who chatted with an unreliable model assistant beat both the model alone and their own unaided scores.

Where research stands

This is a crowded, active area at the major labs and at the UK AI Security Institute. There are working tools: OpenAI's CriticGPT (June 2024) wrote code critiques that people preferred over human critiques 63% of the time and caught more bugs than paid human reviewers. Google DeepMind names "amplified oversight", alongside robust training, as a model-level defence against misalignment, and the UK AI Security Institute's alignment team made scalable oversight its starting focus in May 2025.

What is missing is evidence that these methods keep working when the gap between overseer and model is large. Most tests use today's models, which do not yet broadly beat human experts. A 2025 MIT study modelled oversight as a game between stronger and weaker players; at a large capability gap, even the best protocol it tested succeeded only about half the time, and the others 9% to 14% of the time. Critic models can also invent bugs that mislead people.

Key results

  • People helped by an unreliable chat assistant did substantially better on hard question sets than either the assistant alone or the people working unaided. Samuel R. Bowman et al. (Anthropic), 2022
  • Model-written code critiques were preferred over human critiques in 63% of cases, and the critic models caught more bugs than paid human reviewers. Nat McAleese, Rai Michael Pokorny, Juan Felipe Ceron Uribe, Evgenia Nitishinskaya, Maja Trebacz, Jan Leike (OpenAI), 2024
  • Training a model to write solutions that a small checker model can verify made those solutions easier for time-limited humans to check. Jan Hendrik Kirchner, Yining Chen, Harri Edwards, Jan Leike, Nat McAleese, Yuri Burda (OpenAI), 2024
  • In simulated oversight games, chained oversight across a 400-point Elo gap succeeded 51.7% of the time for debate but only 9.4% to 13.5% for the other three games tested. Joshua Engels, David D. Baek, Subhash Kantamneni, Max Tegmark (MIT), 2025
  • Weak model judges answered reading questions more accurately with debate than with a single arguing model, but debate's advantage over plain question answering was mixed on tasks without hidden information. Zachary Kenton, Noah Y. Siegel et al. (Google DeepMind), 2024

Open problems

  • A model may learn to exploit systematic errors in the oversight signal, and testbeds that inject such errors are still being built. source
  • Nobody knows how well oversight holds when the model is far stronger than its overseer; simulated success rates fall as the gap grows. source
  • AI critics can hallucinate problems that mislead human judges. source
  • Empirical work is hard because today's models do not yet broadly exceed human abilities, so experiments rely on stand-in setups. source

Criticisms

  • A dishonest model can build a long argument with a hidden fatal flaw that is very hard to locate, which may limit debate and amplification-style oversight. Beth Barnes and Paul Christiano
  • Measured as games between stronger and weaker players, nested oversight often fails at large capability gaps, so current protocols may not scale on their own. Joshua Engels, David D. Baek, Subhash Kantamneni, Max Tegmark

Who works on it

  • Anthropic (Alignment Science team): Proposed a way to test oversight with expert-only tasks and lists scalable oversight as a recommended research direction. Recommendations for Technical AI Safety Research Directions (2025)
  • OpenAI: Built critic models and prover-verifier training to help people check model outputs. LLM Critics Help Catch LLM Bugs (2024)
  • Google DeepMind: Names amplified oversight as a model-level defence against misalignment and tests debate with weak judges. An Approach to Technical AGI Safety and Security (2025)
  • UK AI Security Institute (alignment team): Made scalable oversight for honest AI its starting research focus, built around debate. UK AISI's Alignment Team: Research Agenda (2025)
  • Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT): Researchers derived scaling laws for how oversight success changes with the capability gap. Scaling Laws For Scalable Oversight (2025)

In their own words

Developing safe and useful general-purpose AI systems will require us to make progress on scalable oversight: the problem of supervising systems that potentially outperform us on most skills relevant to the task at hand. Empirical work on this problem is not straightforward, since we do not yet have systems that broadly exceed our abilities.

Samuel R. Bowman et al. (Anthropic), Measuring Progress on Scalable Oversight for Large Language Models, 2022

To help address this concern, we propose training agents via self play on a zero sum debate game. Given a question or proposed action, two agents take turns making short statements up to a limit, then a human judges which of the agents gave the most true, useful information.

Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, Dario Amodei (OpenAI), AI safety via debate, 2018
