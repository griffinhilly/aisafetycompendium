Competition policy and antitrust

Use competition law to limit how far a few firms can concentrate control of AI.

Open in the compendium

Competition policy and antitrust law aim to stop a few firms from gaining and abusing control of a market. Applied to AI, they would limit how much of the technology's key inputs (chips, cloud computing, data and talent) and its leading models a handful of companies can control. For safety, the approach cuts two ways: less concentration spreads power and reduces single points of failure, but antitrust law can also block the cooperation between labs that some safety proposals need.

How it works

Competition authorities have three main tools. They can review mergers and investments, including the large stakes cloud companies have taken in AI labs. They can bring cases against firms that abuse a dominant position, for example by tying an AI product to a search engine or cloud service. And some jurisdictions have newer ex ante rules, which set conduct duties in advance for designated 'gatekeepers'. Scholars such as Tejas Narechania and Ganesh Sitaraman (2024) go further and propose 'antimonopoly' tools for the concentrated layers of the 'AI stack' (chips, cloud, models and applications): the law of networks, platforms and utilities, industrial policy, public options and cooperative governance.

A concrete example: in January 2024 the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) used its study powers to order Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet, along with OpenAI and Anthropic, to provide information on their AI partnerships. Its January 2025 staff report found the deals could shape who gets access to compute, raise the cost for labs of switching cloud providers, and give cloud partners access to the labs' sensitive information. In the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) concluded in March 2025 that Microsoft had a 'high level of material influence' over OpenAI but not control, so the partnership did not qualify for merger review.

Where research stands

The tool exists and authorities are watching AI closely, but enforcement so far is mostly studies and statements. In July 2024 the EU's competition commissioner and the heads of the CMA, the US Justice Department's Antitrust Division and the FTC issued a joint statement naming three risks: concentrated control of key inputs, incumbents extending their market power into AI, and partnerships that blunt competition. Researchers at the AI Now Institute and the Open Markets Institute argue for using these tools to break up concentrated AI power.

In 2026 the tension with safety became concrete. On 12 September 2026, Anthropic's chief executive Dario Amodei called for industry-wide coordination to 'pace the frontier', and rival lab leaders endorsed the idea. On 18 September, paying subscribers filed a class action alleging that Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX AI and Google had agreed to slow AI development in breach of the Sherman Act. A 28 September 2026 Congressional Research Service sidebar found that sharing security information is unlikely to raise significant antitrust concerns, but that a stand-alone agreement to pause development could be condemned outright. A bill in Congress (S. 5105 / H.R. 9914) would create a conditional exemption for some AI safety agreements; as of late September 2026 it had not been enacted.

Key results

  • Narechania and Sitaraman show market concentration at and across layers of the AI stack and argue that ex ante competition regulation will likely work better than after-the-fact antitrust enforcement. Tejas N. Narechania, Ganesh Sitaraman, 2024
  • The FTC staff report found that cloud-lab partnerships can affect access to compute, raise switching costs and give cloud partners sensitive information unavailable to others. US Federal Trade Commission, 2025
  • US, UK and EU competition chiefs jointly named concentrated control of key inputs, entrenchment of market power, and arrangements between key players as the main risks to competition in generative AI. European Commission, UK CMA, US DOJ, US FTC, 2024
  • The CMA found Microsoft had a high level of material influence over OpenAI but no change of control, so the partnership did not qualify for merger review. TechCrunch, 2025
  • Under US antitrust law, sharing security threat information is low-risk, joint testing is judged case by case, and a standalone agreement to pause development could face per se condemnation. Congressional Research Service, 2026
  • Hua and Belfield examine how cooperation strategies meant to ease racing pressures, from sharing windfall profits to assisting rivals, fit with EU competition law, which exists to protect rivalry between firms. Shin-Shin Hua, Haydn Belfield, 2021

Criticisms

  • Cooperation between AI developers that could make systems safer, such as easing a race for the lead, can conflict with competition law, whose purpose is to protect rivalry between firms. Shin-Shin Hua and Haydn Belfield
  • Congress should not exempt AI safety coordination from antitrust law, because exemptions require predicting market structure and could entrench dominant firms; existing law already allows procompetitive safety collaboration. Fred Ashton, American Action Forum (October 2026)

Status

Existing legal tool, now being studied for AI. The FTC staff report (January 2025) examined cloud-provider stakes in Anthropic and OpenAI and flagged risks to access to compute and talent. A 2026 Congressional Research Service note covers antitrust limits on safety collaboration among labs.

In their own words

In this Article, we make the case for an antimonopoly approach to governing artificial intelligence. We show that AI's industrial organization, which is rooted in AI's technological structure, evinces market concentration at and across a number of layers. And we argue that an unregulated AI oligopoly has undesirable economic, national security, social, and political consequences.

Tejas N. Narechania and Ganesh Sitaraman, An Antimonopoly Approach to Governing Artificial Intelligence (Yale Law & Policy Review 43:95), 2024

In light of these conclusions, we show how antimonopoly market shaping tools--the law of networks, platforms, and utilities; industrial policy; public options; and cooperative governance--can all help facilitate competition and combat inequality.

Tejas N. Narechania and Ganesh Sitaraman, An Antimonopoly Approach to Governing Artificial Intelligence (Yale Law & Policy Review 43:95), 2024
