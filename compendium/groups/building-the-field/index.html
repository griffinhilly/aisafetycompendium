Group · Building the field

Building the field

Building the field is the meta approach: it makes AI safety larger and better resourced, and gets talented people working in it. It does not make any AI system safer by itself. Instead it adds people, money, organisations and know-how, and so feeds every other group.

Open in the compendium

Why it matters

Every other approach needs people to do it, money to pay for it and organisations to house it. Coefficient Giving, a major funder, says money is no longer the main limit [Taking on unowned problems and agendas]. Its safety and fieldbuilding programmes were on track to direct more than $1 billion in 2026, and it names founders and organisations as the binding constraint [Grantmaking and regranting; Taking on unowned problems and agendas]. Applications to training programmes are rising fast, while jobs and senior mentors after training have become the bottleneck [Research training and upskilling programmes; Recruiting senior talent from other fields].

What is in it

The approaches follow the group's test: people, money, organisations, know-how. People: public communication brings newcomers to the topic [Public communication and content creation]; career advice and job boards steer them to roles [Career advice and job matching]; courses, bootcamps and fellowships train them [Research training and upskilling programmes]; hubs and forums give them places to work and publish [Hubs and forums for safety researchers]; remote and local routes widen who can join [Reducing geographic constraints on AI-safety talent]; and recruiting from law, security and policy brings in senior people [Recruiting senior talent from other fields]. Money: grantmaking and regranting move funds to good projects [Grantmaking and regranting]. Organisations: founders take on problems no team owns [Taking on unowned problems and agendas], and a proposed think tank would advise AI companies [Independent safety advice for AI companies]. Know-how: forecasting informs priorities [Forecasting], and philosophical analysis sharpens the field's concepts [Philosophical analysis of safety concepts].

How it connects

This group feeds all four main bands, rather than sitting beside them. New organisations aim at gaps in other groups, such as independent auditors and incident investigators [Taking on unowned problems and agendas]. Forecasts feed the international scientific assessment of AI [Forecasting; International scientific assessment panel (IPCC-style)]. Automated alignment research may change which skills trainees need [Research training and upskilling programmes; Automated alignment research]. Philosophical analysis works on concepts such as corrigibility and power-seeking from Deciding what AI should value and Theory [Philosophical analysis of safety concepts]. Public communication links to outside pressure on AI companies and to the case for a halt [Public communication and content creation].

Debates

Hubs or remote work: hubs cluster the field in a few cities and push away mid-career people who cannot move, but insiders argue that unsettled research needs a shared office [Hubs and forums for safety researchers; Reducing geographic constraints on AI-safety talent]. Newcomers or seniors: critics say the pipeline is over-optimised for researchers, while employers lack senior staff to supervise juniors [Research training and upskilling programmes; Recruiting senior talent from other fields]. Founding or joining: the main funder urges would-be founders to talk to existing teams first, and critics doubt that more incubators fix the real bottlenecks [Taking on unowned problems and agendas]. Funding concentration: by one newsletter's estimate, one pipeline has supplied roughly 30 to 40 percent of tracked AI-safety funding since 2017 [Grantmaking and regranting; Independent safety advice for AI companies]. Simple messages: some warn that hyper-simplified public messages could do more harm than good [Public communication and content creation].

The 11 approaches

  • Public communication and content creationExplain AI risk to wide audiences through video, podcasts, books, social media and journalism.
  • Career advice and job matchingGive people free career research, one-on-one advice and curated job listings that steer them into AI safety roles.
  • Research training and upskilling programmesRun courses, bootcamps and mentored fellowships that train newcomers for AI safety research, engineering and policy work.
  • Hubs and forums for safety researchersRun the offices, campuses, conferences and online forums where safety researchers work side by side, meet and publish.
  • Reducing geographic constraints on AI-safety talentWiden the talent pool by expanding where AI-safety work gets done. People who cannot move to the Bay Area, London or Washington DC can then contribute through remote and part-time programmes, paid entry roles and local hubs.
  • Recruiting senior talent from other fieldsBring experienced people from law, security, hardware, policy and entrepreneurship into senior and founding roles in AI safety.
  • Grantmaking and regrantingMove money to safety work quickly through grantmakers and regrantors who know the field well enough to spot good projects.
  • Taking on unowned problems and agendasTake on safety problems and research agendas that no team owns, for example by founding a new organisation or lab.
  • Independent safety advice for AI companiesGive AI companies' safety and governance teams independent expert advice on demand, and publish playbooks of good safety practice that any company can adopt.
  • ForecastingPredict capability timelines as an input to prioritisation.
  • Philosophical analysis of safety conceptsUse academic philosophy's literature and methods to test and sharpen the concepts AI safety relies on, such as corrigibility, impact, honesty and rational agency.
