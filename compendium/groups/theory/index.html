Group · Making aligned models

Theory: the maths of agents and goals

This group produces proofs, formal models and definitions about agents and goals, rather than methods used on today's models. It asks what an agent and a goal are, why goal-driven systems might seek power or resist shutdown, and what designs would remove those pulls. The power-seeking theorems describe optimal agents in fully specified settings, and what they show about trained neural networks is disputed [Power-seeking formalism].

Why it matters

Theory gives the field some of its core vocabulary and warnings [Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective); Power-seeking formalism]. Inner versus outer alignment is now standard vocabulary with some experimental support [Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective)], and the corrigibility that labs set in their specs began as a formal problem [Corrigibility as a formal target; Setting AI corrigibility]. Agent foundations bets that powerful AI cannot be aligned reliably while ideas such as agent and goal stay fuzzy [Agent foundations].

What is in it

Foundations: agent foundations [Agent foundations] and mesa-optimisation, the gap between the goal training rewards and the goal a system learns [Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective)]. The problem: power-seeking formalism, which tries to prove that goal-driven agents tend to seek power and avoid shutdown [Power-seeking formalism]. Formal fixes for one agent: corrigibility as a formal target [Corrigibility as a formal target], assistance games in which the AI is unsure what the human wants [Assistance games (CIRL)], mild optimisation [Mild optimisation (satisficing)], impact measures [Impact measures] and empowerment of the human [Empowerment (information-theoretic)]. Proof-backed systems: guaranteed-safe AI and formal verification [Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verification]. Many agents: multi-agent alignment and commitment dynamics [Multi-agent alignment and commitment dynamics] and group agency [Group agency and collective intentionality].

How it connects

Theory feeds the rest of Making aligned models: shielding in training gives runtime guarantees of the kind formal verification seeks [Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verification; Constrained reinforcement learning and shielding], and labs' corrigibility settings trace back to the formal problem [Corrigibility as a formal target; Setting AI corrigibility]. Evaluating tests its predictions: model organisms and evaluations look for hidden goals and shutdown avoidance [Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective); Power-seeking formalism; Model organisms of misalignment]. Formal empowerment underlies measures of human disempowerment in AI and people's own judgement [Empowerment (information-theoretic); Disempowerment formalism]. Group agency links to AI welfare through legal personhood modelled on corporations [Group agency and collective intentionality; Legal personhood and standing for AI]. Verification links to checking international agreements in Global coordination [Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verification; Agreement verification technology].

Debates

Relevance: one reviewer found no case where full formal descriptions of AI systems helped fix them, and MIRI itself became pessimistic about results in time [Agent foundations]. Optimal or trained agents: the power-seeking theorems describe optimal agents, and their lead author said he regretted the work, then said he had overstated that regret [Power-seeking formalism]; critics argue the counting arguments for scheming would also wrongly predict that networks always overfit [Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective)]. Lasting deference: an AI that expects to learn the human's goal has a reason to avoid shutdown until it does [Assistance games (CIRL); Corrigibility as a formal target]. Safety against capability: mild optimisation caps benefits as tightly as harms, and proofs cover models of the world, not the world itself [Mild optimisation (satisficing); Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verification].

The 11 approaches

  • Agent foundationsFormal theory of what an agent, a goal and rational choice even are.
  • Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective)Distinguish the goal a training process rewards from the goal the trained system actually pursues.
  • Power-seeking formalismProve that optimal policies tend to acquire power, as a structural risk result.
  • Corrigibility as a formal targetDesign systems that reliably accept correction and shutdown.
  • Assistance games (CIRL)Model the AI as uncertain about what the human wants, so it defers rather than assumes.
  • Mild optimisation (satisficing)Train systems to do "good enough" instead of maximising, cutting the pull toward extreme strategies.
  • Impact measuresPenalise the AI for making large or irreversible changes to the world state.
  • Empowerment (information-theoretic)Measure how much future control an agent has, and reward an assistant for increasing the human's control.
  • Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verificationBuild systems with mathematical proofs of safe behaviour, not just tests.
  • Multi-agent alignment and commitment dynamicsPrevent collusion, conflict or destabilising races between many AI systems.
  • Group agency and collective intentionalityFormal philosophy of when many agents constitute one agent.

Its band: Making aligned models

Making aligned models builds the model so it wants and does the right thing. It covers deciding what the model should value, the theory of agents and goals, and the data and training that shape the model.
