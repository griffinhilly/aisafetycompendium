Holding AI companies to account

This group checks, constrains, insures or sets rules for AI companies inside one country, whoever does it. It moves from inside the company outward: the lab's own choices and staff, then private checkers and payers, then the state at home. Its tools range from system cards and whistleblower protections to insurance, liability, compute rules and antitrust.

Open in the compendium

Why it matters

Frontier models are built inside a few companies, so their internal risk management is today the main thing between a dangerous capability and its release [Frontier-lab internal policy and safety culture]. Companies grade their own work, and an independent check is the main way outsiders can test their claims [Independent third-party evaluation and audit]. Binding rules make safety practices compulsory, so a company cannot skip them to win a race [Regulation, standards and licensing].

How it connects

Many tools here rest on Evaluating: auditors run capability tests, system cards report them, and regulators could require safety cases [Independent third-party evaluation and audit; Dangerous-capability evaluations; Model cards, system cards and transparency reporting; Regulation, standards and licensing; Safety cases]. Compute governance and know-your-customer checks connect to Releasing and running, since both limit who gets frontier capability [Compute governance; Know-your-customer checks by compute and cloud providers; Limiting proliferation of high-risk models]. Compute governance shares tools with Global coordination, such as hardware-based verification of agreements [Compute governance; Agreement verification technology]; a national project would face sabotage threats from rival states [National AI project; Mutual sabotage deterrence (MAIM)]. Antitrust, a national project and a planned handover all link to Society's research on concentration of power [Competition policy and antitrust; National AI project; Planned handover of AGI control; Concentration-of-power and lock-in research]. Insurance links to Society's market-driven resilience [Commercial AI liability insurance; Market-driven resilience mechanisms].

Debates

Strict rules or room to innovate: critics say licensing protects incumbents, strict liability would chill open releases, and transparency laws punish missed paperwork rather than harm [Regulation, standards and licensing; Liability and tort mechanisms]. Can markets carry the risk: mainstream insurers are adding AI exclusions, and critics say no one can insure what no one can quantify [Commercial AI liability insurance; Government as insurer of last resort]. Independence: evaluators chosen and paid by the companies they assess face conflicts, and under a voluntary system findings reach the public only with the company's consent [Independent third-party evaluation and audit]. Inside the race: critics say safety-motivated labs speed up capabilities and worsen race dynamics [Safety-motivated frontier labs]. Who should hold control: supporters say a national project could secure the work, while critics say it would intensify an arms race and concentrate power [National AI project; Planned handover of AGI control]. Safety or competition: antitrust law can conflict with cooperation between labs that some safety proposals need [Competition policy and antitrust].

The 24 approaches

Inside the company

  • Safety-motivated frontier labsA safety-focused lab builds frontier AI itself, so it can do safety research on the most capable systems and keep influence.
  • Frontier-lab internal policy and safety cultureShape the labs' own risk management, red lines and incident reporting.
  • Model cards, system cards and transparency reportingPublish a standard document with each model on its data, evaluations, limits and risks, so outsiders can check the developer's claims.
  • Planned handover of AGI controlA lab commits in advance to hand control of an aligned AGI to a more democratically legitimate body.
  • Whistleblower protections for AI workersGive AI-company staff legal protection and anonymous channels to report safety risks without retaliation or gagging contracts.
  • Outside pressure on AI developersUse employees, public campaigns and organised civil society to push labs toward responsible behaviour.

Checkers and payers: auditors, insurers, bonds, private regulators

  • Independent third-party evaluation and auditOutside parties test and verify a lab's safety and capability claims.
  • Regulatory marketsGovernments license competing private regulators, and AI firms must buy oversight from one of them.
  • Commercial AI liability insuranceActually sell cover against model underperformance and deviation from expected behaviour.
  • Industry mutual insurer (nuclear-industry model)Frontier labs jointly own an insurer that pools liability and sets safety commitments. It checks members through incident reporting and third-party evaluations.
  • Catastrophe bonds and risk bondsSell tradeable securities that lose principal if a defined AI disaster occurs, so the market prices the risk.

The state at home: regulation, liability, compute rules, antitrust, national projects

  • Regulation, standards and licensingGovernment or standards-body rules that constrain development or deployment.
  • Dual-use research reviewFunders and institutions screen research for misuse risk before they fund or allow it.
  • Duties for deployers (companies that put models into use)Put legal duties on the companies that use AI systems, such as human oversight, monitoring, keeping logs and telling affected people.
  • Fiduciary duties for AI deployersDeployers of AI owe users duties of loyalty, care and confidentiality, which terms of service cannot waive.
  • Compute governanceGovern hardware and compute inputs as a lever on who can train frontier systems.
  • Know-your-customer checks by compute and cloud providersRequire cloud and compute providers to verify their customers and report large training runs, making providers checkpoints on frontier development.
  • Public data trustSet up a public body that controls training data and licenses it to AI developers in return for a share of revenue.
  • AI incident reporting systems and databasesCollect reports of AI harms and near-harms in shared databases or required filings, so others learn and regulators can act.
  • Independent AI incident investigation (NTSB-style)Outside investigators review real AI incidents after the fact and publish lessons, as air-crash boards do.
  • Liability and tort mechanismsMake developers legally bear the cost of catastrophic AI harm.
  • Government as insurer of last resortA mandatory, risk-priced indemnification programme for risks private markets will not cover.
  • Competition policy and antitrustUse competition law to limit how far a few firms can concentrate control of AI.
  • National AI projectRun frontier AI development as a government-led effort, for security against espionage and safety management.

Its band: Constraining

Constraining limits what a model, or the companies that build it, can do. It controls who gets a model and watches it in use, and it checks, insures and sets rules for AI companies.
