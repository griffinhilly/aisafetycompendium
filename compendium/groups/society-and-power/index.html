Society: defence and who holds power

This group changes the world outside the AI so harms do less damage, or shapes who ends up with power and the gains. Its first half is defence: speeding up protective technology, hardening cyber and bio defences, improving decisions and preparing for incidents. Its second half asks who will hold power once AI can replace the many people whose cooperation rulers have always needed [Concentration-of-power and lock-in research].

Open in the compendium

Why it matters

Efforts to stop models from helping attackers will sometimes fail, so society needs defences that keep severe harm costly [Hardening society's defences]. Even well-built AI can go badly if governments, labs and publics misread the situation or fail to coordinate [Improved epistemics and decision quality]. AI could remove an old safeguard against seizures of power: the need for many people to cooperate [Concentration-of-power and lock-in research].

How it connects

Defence complements Releasing and running: limiting the spread of offence-favouring capabilities and building defences answer the same threat [Offence–defence balance research; Hardening society's defences; Limiting proliferation of high-risk models]. Capability evaluations in Testing show what to prepare for [Hardening society's defences; Dangerous-capability evaluations]. Power work links to Deciding what AI should value, where hard constraints and law-following AI resist power grabs [Concentration-of-power and lock-in research; Hard constraints against concentration of power; Law-following AI]. It links to Holding AI companies to account through antitrust and incident reporting [Spreading AI capability widely (diffusion); Competition policy and antitrust; Preparing for warning shots; AI incident reporting systems and databases], and to Global coordination through benefit-sharing between states [Predistribution and windfall mechanisms; Benefit-sharing for joining governance (Atoms for Peace model)]. Gradual disempowerment, in AI and people's own judgement, is the AI-driven cousin of a human power grab [Concentration-of-power and lock-in research; Gradual disempowerment].

Debates

Concentrate or spread: spreading capability is a main counter to power grabs, but critics say it also spreads the means of catastrophe, and some argue concentration lowers race pressure [Spreading AI capability widely (diffusion); Concentration-of-power and lock-in research]. Speed up defence or also slow the danger: the safety community's strongest objection to d/acc, as Buterin reports it, is that on short timelines dangerous capabilities must also be slowed, and defences rarely arrive before the harm [Defence acceleration (d/acc); Hardening society's defences]. One decisive actor: using the first aligned AI to stop others is among the most contested ideas, criticised for spreading fear and driving rivals to race [Using the first aligned AI to stop unsafe AI]. Surveillance: critics warn that such a system would be hard to dismantle and too potent in the wrong hands [Preventive surveillance with structured transparency]. Warning shots: some argue there will never be a clear fire alarm, and that strategies should work without one [Preparing for warning shots].

The 14 approaches

  • Differential technology developmentDelay risky innovation and accelerate safer substitutes, adjusting relative timing.
  • Defence acceleration (d/acc)Deliberately speed up defensive technology faster than offensive, across physical, bio, cyber and information domains, plus social technology.
  • Offence–defence balance researchModel how AI shifts the relative cost of attack versus defence in a domain.
  • Hardening society's defencesMake society harder to harm with AI-enabled attacks, by building defences in the areas AI most helps attackers, above all computer security and biological threats, using AI tools among other means.
  • Market-driven resilience mechanismsUse market incentives to build society's capacity to absorb anticipated AI threats.
  • Improved epistemics and decision qualityStrengthen forecasting and institutional decision-making as a general safety lever.
  • Preparing for warning shotsPrepare responses in advance so a non-catastrophic AI incident can drive lasting action.
  • Reducing the motive to misuseLower the number of people who want to cause mass harm, by fixing social causes such as inequality and poor mental health care.
  • Preventive surveillance with structured transparencyMonitor everyone closely enough to stop misuse of a dangerous technology, using privacy-preserving methods so the data cannot easily be abused.
  • Concentration-of-power and lock-in researchStudy how AI lets a small group entrench control that cannot be contested or reversed.
  • Using the first aligned AI to stop unsafe AIA limited AI that its builders can control is used for one decisive act, such as stopping hostile superintelligent AI from emerging.
  • Spreading AI capability widely (diffusion)Spread AI capability and control across many people and groups, so no single actor holds most of the power.
  • Predistribution and windfall mechanismsDecide who holds equity, access and governance rights before the gains arrive, not after.
  • Worker bargaining power over AI at workGive workers and unions a negotiated say over how employers use AI, through contracts and labour agreements with tech firms.

Its band: Societal resilience

Societal resilience makes the world outside the AI hold up. It protects people's own judgement, gets countries to act together, and strengthens society's defences and the balance of power.
