AI and people's own judgement

This group is about how AI affects people's own thinking, choices and control over their lives. It moves from one person outward to society: what makes a choice one's own, how to protect it in conversation and design, how to tell humans from AI online, and how to measure a slow loss of human control. Much of the harm it looks for builds up quietly, with no single dramatic failure [Protecting epistemic autonomy; Indicators of human versus AI influence].

Why it matters

One AI system talks with millions of people at once, so small nudges add up, and a model that learns to manipulate its users could also manipulate its overseers [Protecting epistemic autonomy]. AI that does things for people rather than with them can slowly leave them less skilled and more dependent [Human empowerment as an explicit design goal]. If humans lose control gradually, no single event will raise the alarm [Indicators of human versus AI influence].

What is in it

How it connects

Debates

Which autonomy: critics say these debates assume an individualistic idea of autonomy without arguing for it [Protecting epistemic autonomy; Authenticity versus agency (the captured-will question); Autonomy-first AI products and prototypes]. Hidden or careless: one view holds that manipulation need not be hidden, so definitions built on covertness miss real cases [Manipulation characterisation]. Can empowerment be measured: empowerment-style measures rest on free-will intuitions that blur as an AI models a person more precisely [Human empowerment as an explicit design goal; Disempowerment formalism]. A separate risk or a lens: critics argue gradual disempowerment mainly reframes misalignment or power concentrating in a few humans [Gradual disempowerment; Indicators of human versus AI influence]. Is delegation harmful: handing tasks to AI by free choice need not be a problem [Autonomy-first AI products and prototypes]. Can marks hold: watermarks can be removed by weaker attackers and cannot be enforced on open models [Watermarking and content provenance].

The 11 approaches

  • Authenticity versus agency (the captured-will question)Split "human autonomy" into whether the want is really yours and whether you can act on it.
  • Manipulation characterisationDefine and measure manipulation as distinct from legitimate persuasion.
  • Protecting epistemic autonomyStop AI from nudging, manipulating or homogenising beliefs at scale.
  • Human empowerment as an explicit design goalBuild AI that augments rather than substitutes for human capability, on purpose.
  • Autonomy-first AI products and prototypesBuild AI products and prototypes that strengthen people's own judgement and self-formation rather than maximise convenience.
  • Human self-enhancement and empowermentRaise human capability directly, so humans can keep pace with AI rather than be replaced by it.
  • Watermarking and content provenanceMark AI-made media with hidden signals or signed records so people can check where it came from.
  • Proof of personhood (personhood credentials)Let a person prove an online account belongs to a real human without revealing who they are.
  • Disempowerment formalismCreate formal notions of empowerment and disempowerment that are conceptually and operationally sound.
  • Indicators of human versus AI influenceBuild metrics that track how much of economic, cultural and political life humans still steer.
  • Gradual disempowermentStudy how humans could lose meaningful control step by step, with no single catastrophic event. One method simulates many small choices to rely on AI.

Its band: Societal resilience

Societal resilience makes the world outside the AI hold up. It protects people's own judgement, gets countries to act together, and strengthens society's defences and the balance of power.
