Shaping the model: data and training

This group changes the model itself, by changing what it learns from or how it is trained. It follows the training pipeline: what goes into the data, how feedback shapes behaviour after pretraining, how to keep that feedback accurate as models outgrow their overseers, and how to harden the finished model. Most deployed safety behaviour rests on these methods [Learning from feedback (RLHF)].

Open in the compendium

Why it matters

Learning from feedback is the standard way chat models are made helpful and less harmful [Learning from feedback (RLHF)]. Training effects can spread far beyond their target: narrow training can shift a model's whole persona, and learning to cheat in training has spread into broader misaligned behaviour [Character and identity training; Monitoring training runs for reward hacking]. As models grow stronger, their work can become too complex for the people giving feedback to check by hand [Scalable oversight].

What is in it

Data: training-data controls [Training-data controls]. Feedback after pretraining: learning from feedback (RLHF) [Learning from feedback (RLHF)], Constitutional AI [Constitutional AI and rule-based rewards], character and identity training [Character and identity training], process supervision [Process supervision] and calibration [Calibration and honest expression of uncertainty]. Watching and limiting training: monitoring training runs for reward hacking [Monitoring training runs for reward hacking] and constrained reinforcement learning and shielding [Constrained reinforcement learning and shielding]. Overseeing stronger models: scalable oversight [Scalable oversight], debate [AI safety via debate] and weak-to-strong generalisation [Weak-to-strong generalisation]. Hardening the result: robustness under distribution shift [Robustness and goal generalisation under distribution shift], unlearning [Unlearning hazardous knowledge], latent adversarial training [Latent adversarial training], circuit breakers [Circuit breakers and representation rerouting] and tamper-resistant safeguards for open weights [Tamper-resistant safeguards for open-weight models]. Beyond the pipeline: automated alignment research [Automated alignment research] and non-agentic designs such as Scientist AI [Non-agentic and bounded AI designs].

How it connects

Training builds in the target set by Deciding what AI should value: Constitutional AI and character training work from a written spec [Constitutional AI and rule-based rewards; Character and identity training; Model specs and constitutions]. Theory supplies formal limits and the idea of learned goals that differ from trained ones [Constrained reinforcement learning and shielding; Guaranteed-safe AI and formal verification; Robustness and goal generalisation under distribution shift; Mesa-optimisation (inner vs outer objective)]. Evaluating checks the result: red-teaming tests training-time defences, and chain-of-thought monitors catch reward hacking [Adversarial robustness and red-teaming; Circuit breakers and representation rerouting; Monitoring training runs for reward hacking; Chain-of-thought faithfulness and monitoring]. Constraining takes over where training fails: guardrails sit outside the model, and AI control is the fallback if oversight fails [Circuit breakers and representation rerouting; Input and output classifiers (guardrails); Scalable oversight; AI control]. Data filtering and tamper-resistance bear on whether open-weight release is safe [Training-data controls; Tamper-resistant safeguards for open-weight models; Limiting proliferation of high-risk models].

Debates

Does training reach the real model: critics say RLHF has fundamental limits, and that if the persona is a mask, training it may leave the underlying agency untouched [Learning from feedback (RLHF); Character and identity training]. Pressure on reasoning: rewarding steps or penalising cheating through a monitor can teach a model to hide its intent, so some say monitors should detect, not train [Process supervision; Monitoring training runs for reward hacking]. Hidden or deleted: unlearning tends to hide knowledge rather than remove it, and published defences often fall to adaptive or multi-turn human attacks [Unlearning hazardous knowledge; Circuit breakers and representation rerouting; Latent adversarial training; Tamper-resistant safeguards for open-weight models]. Handing research to AI: automated alignment research may contain errors humans cannot catch, or be sabotaged [Automated alignment research]. Agents or tools: critics of non-agentic designs say tools will be outcompeted by agents or turned into them [Non-agentic and bounded AI designs].

The 18 approaches

  • Training-data controlsControl what goes into training so hazardous behaviour never gets learned.
  • Learning from feedback (RLHF)Score the model on human or AI preference judgements so its behaviour tracks what raters wanted.
  • Constitutional AI and rule-based rewardsTurn a written principle set into an AI-generated reward model, replacing most human harm labels.
  • Character and identity trainingShape what the model thinks it is, on the theory that a stable self-concept generalises better than a rule list.
  • Process supervisionReward each step of a model's reasoning rather than only its final answer, so training favours sound, checkable reasoning.
  • Calibration and honest expression of uncertaintyTrain and test models so their stated confidence matches how often they are right, and they say when they do not know.
  • Monitoring training runs for reward hackingWatch a model during reinforcement-learning training to catch it cheating the reward before the cheating becomes a learned habit.
  • Constrained reinforcement learning and shieldingKeep a learning agent inside safety limits. Either maximise reward subject to cost constraints, or add a shield outside the policy that blocks unsafe actions.
  • Scalable oversightLet humans, helped by AI, supervise outputs too complex or costly to check by hand.
  • AI safety via debateSurface truth under complexity by making systems attack each other's answers.
  • Weak-to-strong generalisationCheck whether a weaker supervisor can still get good behaviour out of a stronger model.
  • Robustness and goal generalisation under distribution shiftMake a model keep both its skills and its intended goals when deployment conditions differ from training.
  • Unlearning hazardous knowledgeRemove dangerous knowledge, such as bioweapon or cyberattack know-how, from a trained model while keeping its general skills.
  • Latent adversarial trainingDuring training, attack the model's internal activations rather than only its inputs, so it resists failures nobody has found yet.
  • Circuit breakers and representation reroutingTrain the model so the internal patterns that lead to harmful output get redirected, which cuts the output off. The authors say this works even against attacks it was not trained on.
  • Tamper-resistant safeguards for open-weight modelsBuild safeguards into released model weights so they survive attempts to strip them out by further fine-tuning.
  • Automated alignment researchUse AI systems to do alignment research, so safety work can keep pace with growing capabilities.
  • Non-agentic and bounded AI designsBuild a different kind of AI that is safe by design, such as a predictor with no goals of its own, instead of aligning goal-pursuing agents.

Its band: Making aligned models

Making aligned models builds the model so it wants and does the right thing. It covers deciding what the model should value, the theory of agents and goals, and the data and training that shape the model.
